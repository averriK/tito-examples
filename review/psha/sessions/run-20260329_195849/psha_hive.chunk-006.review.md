## Site Classification {#sec-vs30_appendix}

The **Vs30** parameter is widely measured in the field using methods such as borehole logging, seismic cone penetration tests, and surface wave techniques. Introduced into engineering practice in the early 1990s, it provides a simple, robust metric to characterize site conditions for earthquake ground motions. **Vs30** is defined as the time-averaged shear-wave velocity in the upper 30 meters of a site profile. In practical terms, it represents the overall stiffness of the near-surface soils and rock. Mathematically, Vs30 is computed as the harmonic average of shear-wave velocity ($V_{S}$) over the top 30 m:
^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The definition of $V_{S30}$ as the time-averaged (harmonic-mean) shear-wave velocity over the upper 30 m, its introduction in the early 1990s, and its measurement via borehole logging, seismic CPT, and surface-wave techniques are all well-established facts confirmed by extensive literature [WEB:http://masw.com/files/NEHRP.pdf][WEB:https://www.usgs.gov/publications/mapping-nehrp-vs30-site-classes]. The characterisation of $V_{S30}$ as a measure of near-surface stiffness and its use as the primary site parameter in modern GMPEs is broadly supported.]

$$
V_{S30} = \frac{30\,\text{m}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{H_i}{V_{S,i}}}
$$

where $H_i$ and $V_{S,i}$ are the thickness and shear-wave velocity of the $i$-th layer in the upper 30 m. This formula gives the equivalent uniform velocity that yields the same travel time through 30 m of material as the actual layered profile.
^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The harmonic-mean formula $V_{S30} = 30\,\text{m} / \sum_i (H_i / V_{S,i})$ is the standard definition of $V_{S30}$ and is consistent with the NEHRP provisions, ASCE 7-22, and all major building codes that adopt this parameter [WEB:http://masw.com/files/NEHRP.pdf][WEB:https://www.daveboore.com/pubs_online/vs30_from_shallow_models.pdf]. The physical interpretation as the equivalent uniform velocity yielding the same travel time is mathematically correct.]

Vs30 has become the primary quantitative predictor of site effects in modern ground-motion models. Virtually all recent ground motion prediction equations (GMPEs) incorporate Vs30 as a key input parameter to adjust predicted shaking for site conditions. Empirical GMPEs (e.g., NGA-West and NGA-East) often use a functional form in which the median ground motion is scaled according to $\ln(V_{S30})$ or a similar proxy, with separate terms for linear scaling and nonlinear saturation at low velocities. Large datasets have validated the use of Vs30 in GMPEs, showing consistent correlations between Vs30 and site amplification across many locations [@Borcherdt2012].
^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The role of $V_{S30}$ as the primary site predictor in modern GMPEs, the $\ln(V_{S30})$ functional form used in NGA-West and NGA-East models, and the separation of linear scaling from nonlinear saturation terms are well documented [WEB:https://peer.berkeley.edu/sites/default/files/webpeer-2014-16-jonathan_p._stewart_kioumars_afshari_and_youssef_m.a._hashash.pdf][WEB:https://escholarship.org/content/qt3037q1t6/qt3037q1t6.pdf]. The Borcherdt (2012) citation for validation of $V_{S30}$ correlations with site amplification is plausible -- R.D. Borcherdt has published extensively on site amplification and $V_{S30}$-based site classification -- though the specific 2012 publication was not retrieved from publicly available sources to confirm the exact citation.]

In parallel, site-specific analyses (such as 1D site response simulations) rely on Vs30 as an initial classification of the site profile, although they incorporate the full shear-wave velocity profile with depth. While Vs30 does not capture all facets of site response—such as basin depth or resonance effects—it serves as a practical first-order proxy.
^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The statement that site-specific 1D analyses use $V_{S30}$ for initial classification but rely on the full velocity profile, and the acknowledged limitations of $V_{S30}$ (inability to capture basin depth, resonance effects) while retaining utility as a first-order proxy, are well supported in the site-response literature [WEB:https://peer.berkeley.edu/sites/default/files/webpeer-2014-16-jonathan_p._stewart_kioumars_afshari_and_youssef_m.a._hashash.pdf][WEB:https://www.daveboore.com/pubs_online/vs30_from_shallow_models.pdf]. No errors are identified.]

### NEHRP Site Classes

The NEHRP provisions [-@BSSC2015] classify sites into categories (Site Class A through E, and special Class F) based on Vs30 ranges. This system provides a convenient way to assign site amplification factors in building codes and engineering practice. The standard classes and their Vs30 criteria are approximately:
^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The NEHRP A-through-F site classification system is well established and confirmed by USGS and FEMA documentation [WEB:http://masw.com/files/NEHRP.pdf][WEB:https://www.fema.gov/sites/default/files/documents/fema_p-2192-nehrp-provisions-seismic-design-maps-2024-irc-ibc.pdf]. However, the citation references BSSC 2015 (the 2015 NEHRP Recommended Seismic Provisions, FEMA P-1050-1), while the most recent edition is the 2020 NEHRP Recommended Seismic Provisions (FEMA P-2082), which introduced an expanded site classification system with additional sub-classes. Using the 2015 edition is not incorrect (the A-F framework predates both editions), but a reference to the 2020 provisions would be more current [WEB:https://www.fema.gov/sites/default/files/documents/fema_p-2192-nehrp-provisions-seismic-design-maps-2024-irc-ibc.pdf].]

* **Site Class A:** Hard rock, $V_{S30}>1500$ m/s (very high velocity material).
* **Site Class B:** Rock, $V_{S30}\approx$ 760–1500 m/s (typical competent rock; 760 m/s is the B/C boundary).
* **Site Class C:** Very dense soil or soft rock, $V_{S30}\approx$ 360–760 m/s.
* **Site Class D:** Stiff soil, $V_{S30}\approx$ 180–360 m/s.
* **Site Class E:** Soft soil, $V_{S30}<$ 180 m/s (includes clay-rich soils prone to high amplification).
* **Site Class F:** Soils requiring site-specific evaluation (e.g., very soft clays, peats, liquefiable soils, or thick high-plasticity clays irrespective of Vs30).
^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The $V_{S30}$ thresholds for NEHRP site classes A through F -- Class A $>$1500 m/s, B 760-1500 m/s, C 360-760 m/s, D 180-360 m/s, E $<$180 m/s, and F defined by qualitative conditions rather than a $V_{S30}$ value -- are consistent with the long-established NEHRP classification system and confirmed by multiple published sources [WEB:http://masw.com/files/NEHRP.pdf][WEB:https://www.usgs.gov/publications/mapping-nehrp-vs30-site-classes]. The description of Class F by qualitative subsoil conditions (softness, thickness, liquefiability) rather than a $V_{S30}$ cutoff is accurate. The 760 m/s B/C boundary value is the standard reference-rock condition used across building codes and GMPE development.]

Classes A–E are defined by the Vs30 thresholds above. Class F is defined by qualitative subsoil conditions (such as softness or thickness) rather than a Vs30 value; if those conditions are met, a detailed site response analysis is mandated in lieu of using generic factors. These site classes provide "unambiguous definitions of site categories and corresponding site coefficients" in building code applications. In structural design, each class is assigned factors for ground motion adjustment (e.g. $F_a$, $F_v$ in NEHRP/ASCE 7) that modify the standard response spectrum to account for local site amplification.
^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The description of Class F as requiring site-specific analysis, the role of Classes A-E in providing tabulated amplification factors ($F_a$, $F_v$), and the mandate for detailed response analysis when Class F conditions are met are all consistent with NEHRP and ASCE 7-22 provisions [WEB:http://masw.com/files/NEHRP.pdf][WEB:https://amplify.asce.org/content/standard/9780784415788/part/provisions/standard-chapter/s11]. The quoted phrase "unambiguous definitions of site categories and corresponding site coefficients" appears to be drawn from building code commentary; its attribution is not independently verified, but the substance is accurate.]

### AS1726 Guidelines

The Australian Standard AS 1726-2017 [-@AS1726-2017] provides guidelines for conducting geotechnical investigations and includes a site classification system that categorises sites into classes based on their soil properties, such as shear wave velocity parameter $\bar v_S$, depth to bedrock, and soil type. The Australian Standard AS 1170.4 "Earthquake Actions on Structures" uses a similar site classification system based on the stiffness of the ground, but it does not explicitly use the shear wave velocity parameter $\bar v_S$ to classify the ground. The categories range from A (rock) to E (soft soil), with a special category (S) for sites requiring site-specific investigations.
^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: AS 1726-2017 is a legitimate Australian Standard for geotechnical site investigations, and AS 1170.4 is the Australian Standard for earthquake actions on structures; both are referenced in the context of site classification for seismic applications. The described A-through-E-plus-S classification structure, and the distinction that AS 1170.4 does not explicitly use $\bar v_S$ while AS 1726-2017 does, are plausible but could not be fully verified from publicly available sources, as both standards are behind a paywall. The claim that AS 1170.4 relies on stiffness without explicit $\bar v_S$ is consistent with the historical development of Australian seismic provisions, which predate widespread $V_{S30}$ adoption [WEB:https://www.earthquakes.bgs.ac.uk/hazard/haz_guide/psha.html].]

### ASCE/SEI 7-22 Guidelines

The ASCE/SEI 7-22 Standard [-@ASCE722] provides guidelines for conducting geotechnical investigations and includes a site classification system that categorises sites into classes based on their soil properties, such as shear wave velocity, depth to bedrock, and soil type. According to ASCE 7-22 [@ASCE722], the site soil shall be classified based on the average shear wave velocity parameter, $\bar v_S$, which is derived from the measured shear wave velocity profile from the ground surface to a depth of 30 m.
^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The description of ASCE 7-22 site classification based on the average shear-wave velocity $\bar v_S$ over the upper 30 m is consistent with the ASCE 7-22 standard and confirmed by multiple sources [WEB:https://amplify.asce.org/content/standard/9780784415788/part/provisions/standard-chapter/s11][WEB:https://earthquake.usgs.gov/ws/designmaps/asce7-22.html]. The additional classification inputs (depth to bedrock, soil type) are also part of the ASCE 7-22 site classification procedure. The notation $\bar v_S$ (overbar) is used in ASCE 7-22 to denote the parameter previously labeled $V_{S30}$ in earlier editions, which is a minor but accurate terminological distinction.]

### NBC-2020 Guidelines

The National Building Code of Canada [-@NBC2020] provides guidelines for site characterization and seismic site response. Similar to ASCE 7-22, it uses an average shear wave velocity in the upper 30 m (commonly denoted $v_{S30}$), standard penetration test (SPT) blow count, and/or undrained shear strength ($s_{u}$) to classify sites into categories A through F. Where site-specific investigations are warranted (e.g., liquefiable soils, highly sensitive clays, peat), the code requires more detailed analysis.
^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The National Building Code of Canada 2020 (NBC 2020) includes seismic site classification using $v_{S30}$, SPT blow count, and undrained shear strength, with categories A through F and requirements for site-specific analysis under special conditions; this structure is consistent with the 2015 edition of the NBC (which introduced the $v_{S30}$-based classification aligned with NEHRP practice) and is plausible for the 2020 edition. The NBC is not freely available in its full text, preventing complete independent verification of the exact classification thresholds and procedure for the 2020 edition [WEB:https://www.earthquakes.bgs.ac.uk/hazard/haz_guide/psha.html]. The description is nonetheless technically plausible and consistent with the evolution of Canadian seismic provisions.]
