The reservoir wall, base slab, and roof slab was modeled with shell elements and interior columns with beam elements. Elastic material model for concrete was used because no significant material nonlinearity is expected due to the existing prestressing. Contact elements to allow for uncoupled behavior between the roof and the wall and between the wall and the foundations were used. Mass of the water inside the reservoir was modeled as impulsive and convective components per ACI 350.3-06 [@ACI3503]. The impulsive mass was rigidly attached to the reservoir wall. Force associated with the convective mass was applied as a static load in the direction of maximum response; a reasonable assumption because of the very long calculated sloshing period.
^[Verdict: TRUE, Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: This paragraph describes the structural modeling approach for the reservoir SSI analysis. The use of shell elements for walls and slabs and beam elements for columns is standard finite element modeling practice for thin-walled structures. The assumption of elastic material behavior for prestressed concrete under seismic loading is reasonable when prestressing maintains compressive stresses that prevent cracking and nonlinear response. Contact elements for modeling uncoupled behavior at roof-wall and wall-foundation interfaces are appropriate for structures with sliding connections or separation potential. The treatment of water mass as impulsive and convective components per ACI 350.3-06 is the correct and standard approach for seismic analysis of liquid-containing structures [WEB:https://priodeep.weebly.com/uploads/6/5/4/9/65495087/aci_350.3r-01_seismic_design_of_liquid-containing_concrete_structures_and_commentary__aci_350.3-06_.pdf][WEB:http://www.bts-co.net/Files/PaperFiles/21/pdf_ACI-350_3-06_Seismic_Design_of_Liquid-Co.pdf]. ACI 350.3-06 specifies a two-component dynamic model where the impulsive portion $W_I$ is rigidly attached to the tank wall at height $h_I$, while the convective portion $W_C$ is attached by springs at height $h_C$ [WEB:https://www.nzsee.org.nz/db/2003/View/Paper007s.pdf]. The impulsive component moves with the tank walls during ground shaking, while the convective component represents sloshing fluid pressures. The simplification of applying convective mass force as a static load "in the direction of maximum response" is a reasonable engineering approximation when the sloshing period is very long compared to the structural response period. For tall liquid-containing structures, convective (sloshing) periods can be several seconds, making quasi-static treatment appropriate. All modeling approaches described are consistent with accepted practice for nonlinear SSI analysis of liquid-containing structures.]

Soil around and below the reservoir was modeled with solid elements. Separate hysteretic soil material models were used to model till, fill and the drainage material around the reservoir. Input parameters included mass density; bulk modulus at the reference pressure; and cut-off pressure. Custom curves defining shear stress versus shear strain were defined for each material. The nonlinear material model was validated using simple finite element models of a retaining wall and comparing the calculated passive soil pressure with theoretical values. The interface between the soil and the structure was modeled with interface elements that do not transfer tension and allow separation of soil from the structure during intense shaking.

To avoid reflection of pressure waves from the perimeter of the finite element model, nonreflecting boundary condition was specified at the model perimeter. Earthquake ground motion time histories selected for the project are applied at the base of the model and propagated through vertical soil columns along the edges of the model. The vertical soil column along the edges was modeled with its size much larger than the reservoir diameter. Nodal constraints were applied at the nodes of the soil mesh boundary to enable 1-D like soil behavior to represent free-field motion. The analysis includes radiation damping in the soil.

Nonreflecting (absorbing) boundary conditions at the model perimeter are essential to prevent artificial wave reflections in finite element models representing unbounded soil domains. LS-DYNA supports multiple methods for modeling unbounded domains including perfectly matched layers (PML) and other absorbing boundaries that effectively absorb outward-propagating waves [WEB:https://inldigitallibrary.inl.gov/sites/sti/sti/7323634.pdf][WEB:https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0266352X23002343]. Applying earthquake ground motion time histories at the model base and propagating them through vertical soil columns is consistent with LS-DYNA's effective seismic input method [WEB:https://lsdyna.ansys.com/knowledge-base/soil-structure-interaction/]. The approach of using vertical soil columns along edges "much larger than the reservoir diameter" helps ensure free-field conditions are maintained away from the structure. Nodal constraints applied to enable "1-D like soil behavior to represent free-field motion" is a standard technique in SSI analysis, where boundary columns are constrained to move together laterally while allowing vertical differential movement, thus simulating one-dimensional wave propagation in the free field [WEB:https://inldigitallibrary.inl.gov/sites/sti/sti/7323634.pdf][WEB:https://lwrs.inl.gov/content/uploads/11/2024/03/INL-EXT-15-35687-Advanced-Seismic-Soil-Structure-Modeling.pdf]. Radiation damping represents energy dissipation through wave propagation into the surrounding soil and is an important component of total damping in SSI analysis.
