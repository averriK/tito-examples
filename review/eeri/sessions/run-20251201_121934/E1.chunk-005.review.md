The reservoir wall, base slab, and roof slab was modeled with shell elements and interior columns with beam elements. Elastic material model for concrete was used because no significant material nonlinearity is expected due to the existing prestressing. Contact elements to allow for uncoupled behavior between the roof and the wall and between the wall and the foundations were used. Mass of the water inside the reservoir was modeled as impulsive and convective components per ACI 350.3-06 [@ACI3503]. The impulsive mass was rigidly attached to the reservoir wall. Force associated with the convective mass was applied as a static load in the direction of maximum response; a reasonable assumption because of the very long calculated sloshing period.^[Verdict: TRUE, Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: This paragraph describes a comprehensive and technically sound finite element modeling approach that aligns with established engineering practices and standards. LS-DYNA documentation confirms that shell elements are commonly used for modeling walls and slabs while beam elements are appropriate for columns in structural analysis [WEB:https://www.dynasupport.com/tutorial/ls-dyna-users-guide/elements]. The use of elastic material models for prestressed concrete is justified when significant material nonlinearity is not expected, as prestressing maintains concrete in compression and limits cracking under service and moderate seismic loads [WEB:https://www.ideastatica.com/support-center/prestressing-in-detail-pre-tensioned-strands]. LS-DYNA's contact elements are well-documented to model separation and uncoupled behavior between structural components [WEB:https://www.dynasupport.com/tutorial/ls-dyna-users-guide/contact-modeling-in-ls-dyna]. The methodology for modeling water mass follows ACI 350.3-06, which is the authoritative standard for seismic design of liquid-containing concrete structures. ACI 350.3 explicitly prescribes separating liquid behavior into impulsive and convective components based on the Housner method [WEB:http://www.bts-co.net/Files/PaperFiles/21/pdf_ACI-350_3-06_Seismic_Design_of_Liquid-Co.pdf]. The standard specifies that impulsive mass Wi should be rigidly attached to tank walls, as it moves with the walls during ground shaking [WEB:https://www.nzsee.org.nz/db/2003/View/Paper007s.pdf]. Applying convective mass as a static load is a valid simplification when the sloshing period is very long relative to the structural period, as the convective response is quasi-static in such cases. This approach is consistent with standard practice documented in seismic design of liquid-containing structures [WEB:https://www.academia.edu/33913472/Seismic_Design_of_Liquid_Containing_Concrete_Structures_and_Commentary_ACI_350_3_06].]

Soil around and below the reservoir was modeled with solid elements. Separate hysteretic soil material models were used to model till, fill and the drainage material around the reservoir. Input parameters included mass density; bulk modulus at the reference pressure; and cut-off pressure. Custom curves defining shear stress versus shear strain were defined for each material. The nonlinear material model was validated using simple finite element models of a retaining wall and comparing the calculated passive soil pressure with theoretical values. The interface between the soil and the structure was modeled with interface elements that do not transfer tension and allow separation of soil from the structure during intense shaking.^[Verdict: TRUE, Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: This paragraph accurately describes standard finite element modeling practices for soil in SSI analysis. Solid elements are the appropriate element type for three-dimensional soil modeling in finite element analysis. Hysteretic soil material models are well-established for representing the nonlinear, energy-dissipating behavior of soils under cyclic loading conditions [WEB:https://www.geoengineer.org/education/numerical-constitutive-modeling/constitutive-modeling-soil-behavior/the-hardening-soil-model]. The input parameters listed - mass density, bulk modulus at reference pressure, and cut-off pressure - are standard parameters for advanced soil material models. Bulk modulus relates stress to volumetric strain while shear modulus relates shear stress to shear strain, and these are fundamental to soil constitutive modeling [WEB:https://environment.uwe.ac.uk/geocal/SoilMech/basic/stiffness.htm]. Custom shear stress-strain curves are commonly used to define backbone curves for hysteretic soil models, as documented in LS-DYNA material model implementations [WEB:https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/publications/research/safety/04094/04.cfm]. The validation approach using retaining wall models and passive soil pressure is a recognized method in geotechnical engineering; multiple studies document finite element validation against theoretical passive earth pressure solutions [WEB:https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0038080620328614][WEB:https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-01565-3]. Interface elements that do not transfer tension and allow separation are standard in earthquake SSI analysis, with extensive literature documenting their use to model soil-structure separation and sliding during seismic events [WEB:https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/built-environment/articles/10.3389/fbuil.2021.676408/full][WEB:https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0267726116000361].]

To avoid reflection of pressure waves from the perimeter of the finite element model, nonreflecting boundary condition was specified at the model perimeter. Earthquake ground motion time histories selected for the project are applied at the base of the model and propagated through vertical soil columns along the edges of the model. The vertical soil column along the edges was modeled with its size much larger than the reservoir diameter. Nodal constraints were applied at the nodes of the soil mesh boundary to enable 1-D like soil behavior to represent free-field motion. The analysis includes radiation damping in the soil.^[Verdict: TRUE, Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: This paragraph describes technically correct procedures for earthquake input and boundary conditions in finite element SSI analysis. Nonreflecting (also called absorbing or artificial) boundary conditions are essential in dynamic soil-structure interaction analyses to prevent spurious wave reflections from finite domain boundaries; extensive research documents various implementations including viscous boundaries, viscous-spring boundaries, and perfectly matched layers [WEB:https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0886779817305503][WEB:https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/0165212590900434]. When finite element methods are used for seismic SSI problems, artificial boundaries must be introduced to transform infinite domains to finite domains while properly absorbing scattered waves [WEB:https://docs.itascacg.com/flac3d700/3dec/docproject/source/options/dynamic/dynamic_boudaryconditions.html]. The approach of applying earthquake ground motion at the base and propagating it through vertical soil columns is a standard method known as free-field boundary conditions. Free-field columns represent the seismic behavior of soil at infinite distance from the structure [WEB:https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3263/12/2/83][WEB:https://static.rocscience.cloud/assets/verification-and-theory/RS2/Dynamic-Analysis-Theory-Manual.pdf]. Modeling soil columns with dimensions much larger than the structure is consistent with the requirement that free-field boundaries adequately represent far-field conditions. Applying nodal constraints to create 1-D like soil behavior at boundaries is a recognized technique for representing free-field motion in which soil elements respond as vertical columns subjected to vertically propagating shear waves [WEB:https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0267726116000361]. Radiation damping refers to energy dissipation due to wave propagation away from the structure into the surrounding soil, distinct from material damping; it is a critical component of SSI analysis and is properly implemented through appropriate boundary conditions and far-field modeling [WEB:https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10518-011-9261-7][WEB:https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0266352X06000255][WEB:https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2590123025008321].]
