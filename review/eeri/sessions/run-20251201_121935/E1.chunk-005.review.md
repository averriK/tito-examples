## Soil-Structure Interaction Analysis

SSI analysis was performed using LS-Dyna with the schematic of the model shown in Figure 5.

^[Verdict: TRUE, Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: LS-DYNA is a widely used and validated finite element software for nonlinear soil-structure interaction analysis. The software has comprehensive capabilities for seismic SSI analysis of large civil structures including concrete dams, nuclear power plants, and underground structures [WEB:https://www.researchgate.net/publication/336074024_Recent_Advances_in_Non-Linear_Soil-Structure_Interaction_Analysis_using_LS-DYNA][WEB:https://lsdyna.ansys.com/knowledge-base/soil-structure-interaction/]. LS-DYNA employs novel methods for SSI analysis including the effective seismic input method to incorporate earthquake forces efficiently and models the unbounded domain accurately at low computational cost. The software includes material libraries for clay, silt, sand, and rock to simulate nonlinear behavior with varying degrees of complexity and uses Terzaghi's concept of effective stress to simulate materials with pore pressure. LS-DYNA has been specifically applied to dam-reservoir-foundation systems and has been validated for seismic soil-structure interaction applications requiring explicit time integration and large deformation analysis [WEB:https://inldigitallibrary.inl.gov/sites/sti/sti/7323634.pdf].]

The reservoir wall, base slab, and roof slab was modeled with shell elements and interior columns with beam elements. Elastic material model for concrete was used because no significant material nonlinearity is expected due to the existing prestressing. Contact elements to allow for uncoupled behavior between the roof and the wall and between the wall and the foundations were used. Mass of the water inside the reservoir was modeled as impulsive and convective components per ACI 350.3-06 [@ACI3503]. The impulsive mass was rigidly attached to the reservoir wall. Force associated with the convective mass was applied as a static load in the direction of maximum response; a reasonable assumption because of the very long calculated sloshing period.

^[Verdict: TRUE, Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The structural modeling approach described is consistent with standard finite element analysis practices and follows established design standards for liquid-containing structures. The use of shell elements for slabs and walls with beam elements for columns is appropriate for thin-walled structural components and is standard practice in finite element modeling. The assumption of elastic material behavior for prestressed concrete is justified given that prestressing places the concrete in compression, reducing the likelihood of cracking and material nonlinearity under service and moderate earthquake loads. The modeling of contact elements to allow uncoupled behavior with separation capability is essential for capturing realistic seismic response where roof-wall and wall-foundation interfaces may experience relative movement. The treatment of water mass according to ACI 350.3-06 is the standard approach for seismic design of liquid-containing structures [WEB:https://priodeep.weebly.com/uploads/6/5/4/9/65495087/aci_350.3r-01_seismic_design_of_liquid-containing_concrete_structures_and_commentary__aci_350.3-06_.pdf][WEB:http://www.bts-co.net/Files/PaperFiles/21/pdf_ACI-350_3-06_Seismic_Design_of_Liquid-Co.pdf]. ACI 350.3 specifies that the dynamic model assumes the impulsive portion of liquid weight is rigidly attached to the tank wall while the convective portion is attached by springs of finite stiffness and damping. The impulsive pressures are associated with inertia forces produced by wall accelerations and are directly proportional to these accelerations, while convective pressures result from fluid oscillations. For concrete tanks with relatively thick, rigid walls and roof, this results in a two-degree-of-freedom system where impulsive and convective components have periods that are generally far apart. The simplification of applying convective forces as a static load in the direction of maximum response is reasonable when the sloshing period is very long compared to the predominant periods of ground motion, as convective effects become quasi-static in such cases.]

Soil around and below the reservoir was modeled with solid elements. Separate hysteretic soil material models were used to model till, fill and the drainage material around the reservoir. Input parameters included mass density; bulk modulus at the reference pressure; and cut-off pressure. Custom curves defining shear stress versus shear strain were defined for each material. The nonlinear material model was validated using simple finite element models of a retaining wall and comparing the calculated passive soil pressure with theoretical values. The interface between the soil and the structure was modeled with interface elements that do not transfer tension and allow separation of soil from the structure during intense shaking.

^[Verdict: TRUE, Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The soil modeling approach described follows established practices for nonlinear soil-structure interaction analysis. The use of solid elements with separate hysteretic material models for different soil types (till, fill, drainage material) allows proper representation of varying soil properties and nonlinear stress-strain behavior. LS-DYNA has extensive material libraries for soil modeling including models for clay, silt, sand, and rock with varying complexity [WEB:https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/publications/research/safety/04095/04095.pdf][WEB:https://lsdyna.ansys.com/knowledge-base/soil-structure-interaction/]. The input parameters mentioned (mass density, bulk modulus at reference pressure, cut-off pressure) are standard parameters for soil constitutive models, with cut-off pressure representing the tensile strength limit. The use of custom shear stress versus shear strain curves is appropriate for capturing nonlinear hysteretic soil behavior under cyclic loading. The validation approach using retaining wall models and comparing calculated passive soil pressure with theoretical values demonstrates engineering rigor and is a standard verification procedure. The modeling of soil-structure interface elements that do not transfer tension and allow separation is critical for realistic seismic analysis of buried structures. Interface elements with no-tension capability are well-established in geotechnical earthquake engineering to model gap formation and sliding at soil-structure boundaries during strong shaking [WEB:https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0267726116000361][WEB:https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-94-011-1755-5_6].]

To avoid reflection of pressure waves from the perimeter of the finite element model, nonreflecting boundary condition was specified at the model perimeter. Earthquake ground motion time histories selected for the project are applied at the base of the model and propagated through vertical soil columns along the edges of the model. The vertical soil column along the edges was modeled with its size much larger than the reservoir diameter. Nodal constraints were applied at the nodes of the soil mesh boundary to enable 1-D like soil behavior to represent free-field motion. The analysis includes radiation damping in the soil.

^[Verdict: TRUE, Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The boundary conditions and input motion application described represent standard and rigorous practices in computational seismic soil-structure interaction analysis. Nonreflecting boundary conditions are essential when using the finite element method for SSI problems to transform the infinite soil domain to a finite computational domain and prevent spurious wave reflections from artificial boundaries [WEB:https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0886779817305503][WEB:https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1467-8667.1993.tb00214.x]. Various implementations exist including viscous boundaries, viscous-spring boundaries, and perfectly matched layers. The viscous-spring boundary can simulate radiation damping and elasticity recovery of infinite or semi-infinite media with good stability and acceptable accuracy [WEB:https://www.issmge.org/uploads/publications/1/120/ICSMGE_2022-171.pdf]. The approach of applying earthquake ground motion at the base and propagating through vertical soil columns along model edges with nodal constraints to achieve 1-D behavior is the standard free-field loading combined viscous-spring boundary technique [WEB:https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0165212590900434]. This method ensures that the far-field soil motion represents free-field conditions unaffected by the structure. The specification that vertical soil columns have size much larger than reservoir diameter ensures that near-field effects are properly separated from boundary effects. The explicit mention that analysis includes radiation damping confirms proper treatment of geometric damping associated with wave propagation away from the structure into the infinite soil medium, which is distinct from material hysteretic damping and is critical for accurate dynamic response prediction in SSI analysis.]
