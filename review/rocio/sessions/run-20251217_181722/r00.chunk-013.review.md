## 9. Fuente de agua

### 9.1 Fuente de agua principal

La fuente principal de agua de la cuenca Titijones, mayormente lo constituye las altas cumbres del volcánico fisural Limani, presenta alturas entre 4500 a 5200 msnm, actualmente con remanentes glaciares aislados, sin embargo, durante épocas de altas nevadas están cubiertas en su mayor parte; los deshielos y escurrimientos discurren por las artesas, circos glaciares y vertientes los cuales están direccionadas hacia las partes bajas de la cuenca Titijones. (Figura 9-1)

^[Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH. Rationale: The identification of the Limani volcanic fissure's high peaks (4500-5200 masl) as the principal water source for the Titijones basin is hydrogeologically sound and supported by regional evidence. Research confirms that "groundwater stable isotopes depleted in deuterium (2H) and oxygen-18 (18O) suggest that the main source of aquifer recharge is precipitation at high elevations (∼4000 m a.s.l.) of the Altiplano and Andean mountain ridge" [WEB:https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/26/483/2022/]. The elevation range of 4500-5200 masl is appropriate for high-altitude recharge zones in the southern Peruvian Andes. The description of recharge mechanisms—glacial meltwater and runoff flowing through glacial troughs (artesas), cirques (circos glaciares), and tributaries (vertientes)—accurately characterizes the hydrogeological pathways typical of formerly glaciated volcanic terrains. Studies confirm that "wet seasons deliver 100–130 mm monthly in some puna regions, while dry periods restrict inputs to 3–12 mm monthly, rendering perennial groundwater discharge from aquifers and springs indispensable" [WEB:https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-021-87162-6], indicating that high-elevation recharge zones sustain baseflow. The mention of "isolated remnant glaciers" (remanentes glaciares aislados) with seasonal expansion during heavy snow periods aligns with ongoing glacial recession documented across the Andes. However, the attribution of "principal" water source exclusively to Limani requires qualification: the report also identifies other volcanic centers (Iruma, Titine, Arundaya, Cancavine) as contributing recharge through seeps and springs. While Limani may be the largest contributor given its proximity to the most productive springs (Manante 1 at 20 l/s attributes water to Limani/Tutupaca), a comprehensive water balance would be needed to confirm its dominance. The absence of quantitative recharge estimates or isotopic data prevents full verification of the "principal source" claim.]

## Summary of Findings

**Overall Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH**

The section on fuente de agua (water source) provides a reasonable conceptual model of recharge to the Titijones aquifer system, though with limited quantitative support. Key strengths include:

1. **Elevation-based recharge**: Correctly identifies high-elevation zones (4500-5200 masl) as recharge areas, consistent with isotopic evidence from regional studies
2. **Recharge mechanisms**: Accurately describes glacial meltwater and precipitation runoff as recharge pathways
3. **Topographic routing**: Recognizes the role of glacial landforms (artesas, circos) in channeling water toward the basin
4. **Glacial context**: Acknowledges current limited glacial extent with seasonal variability

**Limitations identified:**

1. **Exclusivity of attribution**: Claims Limani as the "principal" source without quantitative support; other volcanic centers also contribute recharge
2. **Lack of water balance**: No quantitative estimates of recharge volumes or relative contributions from different source areas
3. **Missing verification**: No isotopic or hydrochemical data cited to confirm recharge source attribution
4. **Tutupaca omission**: While Figura 9-1 references "Nevado Tutupaca," the text focuses on Limani without discussing Tutupaca's contribution

**Recommendation**: The conceptual model would be strengthened by: (1) quantitative recharge estimates for different source areas, (2) stable isotope data (δ18O, δ2H) comparing precipitation/meltwater from different elevations with groundwater samples from wells and springs, and (3) explicit acknowledgment of multiple contributing recharge sources rather than singular attribution to Limani.
