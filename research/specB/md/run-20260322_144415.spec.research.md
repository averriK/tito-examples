## SLOT 1: Summary of Spectral-Matching Record-Selection Methodologies

Ground-motion record selection methodologies concerned with spectral conformity to a probabilistic seismic hazard analysis (PSHA)-derived target spectrum fall into two conceptually distinct categories: waveform-modification techniques and record-selection-with-weighting techniques. Waveform-modification techniques alter the individual accelerogram itself - either in the time domain or in the frequency domain - to impose compatibility between the computed response spectrum of the modified record and the target. Record-selection-with-weighting techniques, by contrast, preserve the original time-series intact and instead seek a subset of real records, together with a set of scalar multipliers, such that a weighted linear combination of the resulting response spectra approximates the target spectrum. These two families differ fundamentally in their treatment of the ground-motion signal, and the spectral-matching problem framed as a linear regression belongs exclusively to the second family. [DOI:10.1007/s11831-023-10025-y][DOI:10.1007/s10518-022-01393-0]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The distinction between waveform-modification and record-selection-with-weighting approaches is well established in the earthquake engineering literature and is consistent with the two cited sources, which provide comprehensive reviews of both families of methods. No unsupported quantitative claims are made in this paragraph.]

Time-domain spectral matching, introduced by Lilhanand and Tseng (1988) and extended by Abrahamson (1992), adds frequency-localised wavelet pulses to the original accelerogram in an iterative fashion until the response spectrum of the augmented record converges to the target spectrum. The approach of Abrahamson formed the basis of the widely used RspMatch software. [WEB:https://asnr.hal.science/irsn-02123205/document] Although time-domain matching preserves the non-stationary amplitude-modulation structure of the original record better than purely frequency-domain procedures, it inevitably alters the number of sign reversals, the Arias intensity, the strong-motion duration, and the cross-period correlation structure of spectral ordinates. [WEB:https://arxiv.org/pdf/1905.02394] Frequency-domain spectral matching proceeds by iteratively rescaling the Fourier amplitude spectrum of the record while retaining its phase spectrum, thereby achieving compatibility at the cost of modifying the spectral-temporal distribution of energy. Both time-domain and frequency-domain modification techniques produce modified accelerograms whose increased spectral compatibility comes at the expense of the statistical independence and seismological fidelity of the resulting suite. [DOI:10.1007/s11831-023-10025-y]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The descriptions of time-domain and frequency-domain spectral matching methods are well documented in the cited sources. The attribution of the wavelet method to Lilhanand and Tseng (1988) and of RspMatch to Abrahamson (1992) is confirmed by the search results from the ASNR and arXiv sources. The effects on Arias intensity, duration, and cycle count are consistent with the cited review article.]

The linear-combination record-selection methodology avoids the modification of individual accelerograms entirely. In this framework, a candidate library of $K$ real recorded accelerograms is assembled from databases such as the PEER NGA-West2, and non-negative scalar weights $w_k$ are determined so that the weighted sum $\sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k S_{a,k}(T_i)$ closely approximates the target spectral acceleration $S_a^{\mathrm{obj}}(T_i)$ at each period $T_i$ in the mesh $\{T_1, \dots, T_n\}$. The records themselves are never modified; the only degrees of freedom are the components of the weight vector $\mathbf{w} = (w_1, \dots, w_K)^\top$. Early formulations of this concept employed greedy search strategies - most notably the semi-automated procedure of Kottke and Rathje (2008) - and later advances introduced formal optimization algorithms that simultaneously control the mean and standard deviation of the selected suite's spectral shape over the target period range. [WEB:https://peer.berkeley.edu/sites/default/files/baker_et_al_2011_peer_gm_report.pdf][DOI:10.1007/s10518-022-01393-0]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The linear combination record-selection framework and its key early contributors are described in the cited PEER report and the Bulletin of Earthquake Engineering article. The identification of Kottke and Rathje (2008) as contributors is confirmed by search results. The description of the degrees of freedom as the weight vector is consistent with the task notation.]

The choice of target spectrum critically determines the character of the selected suite. The uniform hazard spectrum (UHS) is derived directly from PSHA and represents a specified annual exceedance probability at every period independently, yielding an envelope spectrum that can be overly conservative for multi-period response measures. The Conditional Mean Spectrum (CMS), developed by Baker and Cornell, represents the expected spectral shape at all periods given a specified spectral acceleration at the structural fundamental period $T_1$ and its associated hazard-consistent magnitude, distance, and epsilon disaggregation; selecting records to match the CMS produces suites with appropriate spectral shapes for single-period structural response estimation. [DOI:10.1002/eqe.571] The Generalized Conditional Intensity Measure (GCIM) framework further generalises the CMS to multiple conditioning intensity measures, permitting record selection that targets both the mean and variance of the spectral distribution across a vector of intensity measures. All such target spectra enter the linear-combination methodology as the target vector $\mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ whose components are defined in the logarithmic domain as $y_i = \ln S_a^{\mathrm{obj}}(T_i)$. [WEB:https://peer.berkeley.edu/sites/default/files/baker_et_al_2011_peer_gm_report.pdf]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The UHS, CMS, and GCIM frameworks are described consistently across the cited Baker and Cornell (2006) paper and the PEER report. The definition of $\mathbf{y}$ in log scale is consistent with the task-defined notation and with standard practice in ground motion characterization.]

## SLOT 2: Minimum Number of Records and Code-Based Requirements

From the perspective of the linear-combination optimization problem, the theoretical minimum number of spectra required to achieve an exact spectral match depends on the rank properties of the problem rather than on a fixed integer threshold. If $n$ denotes the number of period ordinates in the target mesh and $K$ denotes the number of candidate spectra in the library, the underdetermined case $K \geq n$ admits infinitely many exact solutions when the target vector $\mathbf{y}$ lies in the column space of the design matrix $\mathbf{X}$. In the practically relevant overdetermined case $n \gg K$, no exact solution generally exists and a least-squares approximation is sought; here the adequacy of the spectral match depends on how well the span of the $K$ candidate log-spectral vectors covers the region of log-spectral-shape space containing $\mathbf{y}$. For a broad-band target spanning several decades of period, adequate coverage typically requires at least five to seven records exhibiting diverse magnitudes, distances, site conditions, and epsilon values, so that no frequency sub-range of the target mesh is systematically underrepresented in the candidate column space of $\mathbf{X}$. [WEB:https://www.nehrp.gov/pdf/nistgcr11-917-15.pdf][WEB:https://pubs.usgs.gov/of/2011/1083/]^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The rank-based argument and the underdetermined versus overdetermined distinction are mathematically correct. The claim that five to seven diverse records are typically needed for adequate broad-band coverage is consistent with the cited NIST and USGS reports, but the exact minimum is problem-dependent and no single authoritative source provides a universal theoretical threshold. The claim is therefore supportable but not precisely verified.]

The provisions of ASCE 7-05 and ASCE 7-10 established a minimum of three ground-motion record pairs for response history analysis, requiring that the structural design be governed by the maximum response across all analyses when fewer than seven records are used, and by the mean response when seven or more records are employed. The seven-record threshold and the transition from envelope to mean acceptance criteria originate primarily from studies by Shome and Cornell in the late 1990s, which demonstrated, using elastic-perfectly plastic and bilinear single-degree-of-freedom oscillator models, that the variance of peak displacement response is sufficiently small with seven randomly selected ground motions that the sample mean provides a reliable estimator of the true conditional mean response. The USGS Open-File Report 2011-1083 examined this threshold in greater detail and concluded that the ASCE 7 scaling procedure applied to seven records produces conservative estimates of median structural response and that the scatter in response estimates decreases monotonically as the suite size increases beyond seven. [WEB:https://pubs.usgs.gov/of/2011/1083/][WEB:https://www.structuremag.org/article/seismic-time-histories-a-practical-approach/]^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The seven-record threshold and its connection to mean-versus-envelope acceptance criteria are supported by the USGS report and the Structure Magazine article. The attribution to Shome and Cornell is consistent with general knowledge of the field but was not confirmed from the primary source paper in this research pass; this introduces moderate uncertainty about the precise origin of the threshold.]

ASCE 7-16 revised the ground-motion selection and scaling requirements in Chapter 16, adopting an eleven-record minimum for the expanded nonlinear response history analysis procedures applicable to Risk Category III and IV buildings and to structures using performance-based design acceptance criteria. The transition from seven to eleven records reflects the more demanding statistical framework of Chapter 16, which evaluates not only the mean of the structural demand quantities from the selected suite but also dispersed response quantities at elevated fractile levels used for limit-state checks on local deformation demands, such as inter-story drift ratios. With eleven records, the sample mean estimate is more stable and the empirical dispersion characterisation is more reliable than with seven records, reducing the sensitivity of the acceptance criteria to the specific selection of individual records within the suite. [WEB:https://www.structuremag.org/article/seismic-time-histories-a-practical-approach/][DOI:10.1007/s11831-023-10025-y]^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The description of ASCE 7-16 Chapter 16 provisions for eleven records is consistent with the Structure Magazine article and the review article citation. The specific rationale linking eleven records to dispersion-based acceptance criteria at elevated fractiles is a reasonable interpretation of the standard's intent, supported by the cited secondary sources, but was not verified from the primary standard text. The confidence is therefore medium.]

## SLOT 3: Classical Linear Regression Formulation (RMSE Minimization)

The spectral-matching problem is cast as a classical ordinary least-squares regression by defining the design matrix $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times K}$ whose $(i,k)$ entry is the log-transformed spectral acceleration of the $k$-th candidate record at the $i$-th period ordinate, $X_{ik} = \ln S_{a,k}(T_i)$, for $i = 1, \dots, n$ and $k = 1, \dots, K$. The target vector $\mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ has components $y_i = \ln S_a^{\mathrm{obj}}(T_i)$, encoding the logarithm of the PSHA-derived target spectral acceleration at each mesh period $T_i$. Working in log-spectral-acceleration space is standard practice because the distribution of $\ln S_a$ conditioned on magnitude and distance is approximately Gaussian, residuals are more homoscedastic in the logarithmic domain than in the original Sa domain, and the linear combination $\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}$ corresponds to a geometrically weighted average of the candidate log-spectra. The regression seeks the weight vector $\mathbf{w} = (w_1, \dots, w_K)^\top \in \mathbb{R}^K$ that best approximates $\mathbf{y}$ in the column space of $\mathbf{X}$. [DOI:10.1002/eqe.571][DOI:10.1007/s11831-023-10025-y]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The design matrix definition and the rationale for working in log-Sa space are consistent with the Baker and Cornell (2006) framework and the review article. The notation is exactly as defined in the task Context section. The Gaussian approximation for ln Sa is a standard result in ground motion characterization.]

The objective function to be minimized is the mean-squared error (MSE) between the model prediction $\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}$ and the target $\mathbf{y}$, expressed as the scaled squared Euclidean norm:^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: This paragraph introduces the MSE objective function. The formulation is a direct application of ordinary least squares to the defined matrix and vector, with no source-dependent claim beyond the notation already established. The statement about convexity and uniqueness follows from standard properties of positive-definite quadratic forms.]

$$\mathrm{MSE}(\mathbf{w}) = \frac{1}{n} \left\| \mathbf{X}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y} \right\|_2^2 = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left( \sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k \ln S_{a,k}(T_i) - y_i \right)^2$$

The root-mean-square error is $\mathrm{RMSE}(\mathbf{w}) = \sqrt{\mathrm{MSE}(\mathbf{w})}$, and minimizing RMSE over $\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^K$ is equivalent to minimizing $\left\| \mathbf{X}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y} \right\|_2^2$, a standard unconstrained convex quadratic program. The objective is strictly convex in $\mathbf{w}$ when $\mathbf{X}$ has full column rank (i.e., when the $K$ candidate log-spectra are linearly independent), which guarantees a unique minimizer.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The equivalence between minimizing RMSE and minimizing the squared norm is a trivial algebraic identity. The convexity and uniqueness arguments are classical properties of positive-definite quadratic forms and are not dependent on any external source.]

The closed-form least-squares solution is obtained by setting the gradient of the objective to zero. The gradient of $\left\| \mathbf{X}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y} \right\|_2^2$ with respect to $\mathbf{w}$ is $2\mathbf{X}^\top(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y})$; setting this to zero yields the normal equations $\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X}\, \mathbf{w} = \mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{y}$. When $\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times K}$ is invertible, the unique ordinary least-squares solution is:^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The normal equations and the closed-form OLS solution are classical results of linear algebra and least-squares theory, with no source-dependent claim. The derivation is standard and does not depend on any external citation.]

$$\mathbf{w}^* = \left( \mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X} \right)^{-1} \mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{y}$$

In practice, the Gram matrix $\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times K}$ may be poorly conditioned when candidate spectra are strongly correlated across periods, a common occurrence when the library contains records from similar seismological environments or with similar magnitudes and distances. In such cases, the normal equations are solved via the QR decomposition or the singular value decomposition (SVD) of $\mathbf{X}$, bypassing the explicit inversion of $\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X}$ to obtain a numerically stable minimum-norm least-squares solution. Regularization strategies, discussed in subsequent sections, also address this ill-conditioning by modifying the objective function itself.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The recommendation to use QR or SVD for numerically stable least-squares in the presence of near-collinear predictors is a standard result of numerical linear algebra. No quantitative claim about condition numbers or specific thresholds is made, and the statement is not dependent on any external source beyond standard numerical analysis practice.]

## SLOT 4: Log-Log Space Reformulation

Response spectra exhibit significantly smoother variation when plotted in log-log coordinates - log-spectral acceleration $\ln S_a$ on the vertical axis against log-period $\ln T$ on the horizontal axis - than in linear-scale coordinates. This smoothness reflects the underlying approximately power-law scaling of spectral content with period in the velocity-controlled and displacement-controlled frequency regimes, as well as the approximately log-normal shape of ground motion attenuation models. When the period mesh $\{T_1, \dots, T_n\}$ is constructed as a uniform grid on the logarithmic period axis, the regression exploits this smoothness property by distributing constraint equations evenly across frequency decades, avoiding the over-weighting of short periods that arises when a linearly spaced mesh assigns many more ordinates per unit frequency to the high-frequency end of the spectrum. [DOI:10.1002/eqe.571][DOI:10.1007/s11831-023-10025-y]^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The smoothness argument for log-log coordinates and the power-law scaling basis are consistent with the cited Baker and Cornell (2006) framework and the review article. The claim about over-weighting of short periods with linear spacing is a qualitative argument that is not explicitly quantified in the cited sources; it is physically motivated and accepted practice but merits a medium confidence designation.]

The log-log reformulation introduces transformed period coordinates $t_i = \ln T_i$ for $i = 1, \dots, n$, so that the period mesh becomes $\{t_1, \dots, t_n\}$ with $T_i = e^{t_i}$, taken as equally spaced in $t$. The transformed design matrix $\widetilde{\mathbf{X}} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times K}$ retains log-spectral acceleration entries indexed over the log-period grid, namely:^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The coordinate transformation $t_i = \ln T_i$ and the equal-spacing interpretation are standard constructions in spectral analysis and regression on log-transformed variables. The notation is consistent with the task Context. No source-dependent quantitative claim is present in this introductory paragraph.]

$$\widetilde{X}_{ik} = \ln S_{a,k}\!\left(e^{t_i}\right) = \ln S_{a,k}(T_i)$$

The transformed target vector $\widetilde{\mathbf{y}} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ retains the components $\widetilde{y}_i = \ln S_a^{\mathrm{obj}}(T_i) = y_i$ as defined in the Context notation. Consequently, the algebraic structure of the regression is identical to that of SLOT 3: $\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}\mathbf{w} \approx \widetilde{\mathbf{y}}$ with the same weight vector $\mathbf{w}$. The fundamental difference lies entirely in the placement of the $n$ period evaluation points: they are now equally spaced in $\ln T$ rather than in $T$, so each frequency decade $[T_i, 10T_i]$ contributes an equal number of constraint equations, and the resulting weight solution $\mathbf{w}^*$ minimises the log-log residuals uniformly across the full frequency range of engineering interest.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The algebraic equivalence of the log-log formulation to SLOT 3 is a direct consequence of the transformation, and the claim about equal constraint-equation density per decade is a straightforward consequence of equal spacing in $\ln T$. Both statements are mathematically self-evident and consistent with the task notation.]

The objective function in the fully log-log parameterisation is:^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: This paragraph introduces the log-log objective. The formulation is a direct substitution of the transformed notation into the MSE structure of SLOT 3. No source-dependent claim is introduced beyond the established notation.]

$$\mathrm{MSE}_{\mathrm{LL}}(\mathbf{w}) = \frac{1}{n} \left\| \widetilde{\mathbf{X}}\mathbf{w} - \widetilde{\mathbf{y}} \right\|_2^2 = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left( \sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k \ln S_{a,k}(T_i) - \ln S_a^{\mathrm{obj}}(T_i) \right)^2$$

where the summation is over $n$ log-uniformly spaced period ordinates. The closed-form solution is structurally identical to the SLOT 3 result, $\mathbf{w}^* = (\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}^\top \widetilde{\mathbf{X}})^{-1} \widetilde{\mathbf{X}}^\top \widetilde{\mathbf{y}}$, provided $\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}$ has full column rank. The numerical conditioning of $\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}^\top \widetilde{\mathbf{X}}$ is generally improved relative to the linearly spaced formulation because the log-uniform sampling reduces the amplitude disparity among spectral features evaluated at widely separated periods, leading to a more balanced Gram matrix and a smaller effective condition number.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The algebraic identity of the log-log closed-form solution with the SLOT 3 formula is a direct consequence of the structural equivalence established above. The conditioning improvement argument follows from the smoothness of spectral functions in log-log coordinates, which is consistent with standard numerical linear algebra arguments. No source-dependent quantitative claim about specific condition numbers is made.]

## SLOT 5: Non-Negative Weight Constraint and R Implementation

The unconstrained least-squares solution $\mathbf{w}^*$ obtained in SLOT 3 or SLOT 4 may contain negative components. A negative weight $w_k < 0$ would imply subtracting a scaled version of record $k$ from the spectral combination, which has no physically admissible interpretation in ground-motion seismic hazard analysis: a seismic ground-motion record cannot contribute negatively to the expected spectral demand at a site, and no anti-record exists in any strong-motion database. Physical consistency therefore requires $w_k \geq 0$ for all $k = 1, \dots, K$, transforming the unconstrained regression of SLOT 3 into a non-negative least-squares (NNLS) problem. An additional practical consequence of the non-negativity constraint is that it promotes sparse solutions - many $w_k$ will be driven to exactly zero at the optimum, effectively performing record selection (identifying a small active subset of the library) simultaneously with weight assignment. [WEB:https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-negative_least_squares][WEB:https://cran.r-project.org/package=nnls]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The physical motivation for non-negative weights and the sparsity-promoting property of NNLS are well established and consistent with the cited sources. The Wikipedia NNLS article and CRAN nnls package documentation confirm both the algorithmic framework and the sparsity property. No unsupported quantitative claims are made.]

The NNLS problem is stated as:^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: This paragraph presents the formal NNLS problem statement. The convexity of the feasible set (non-negative orthant) and the uniqueness of the minimizer under full column rank are standard results in convex optimization, with no source-dependent claim beyond established mathematical facts.]

$$\mathbf{w}^* = \underset{\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^K}{\arg\min} \left\| \mathbf{X}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y} \right\|_2^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad w_k \geq 0 \; \text{for all} \; k = 1, \dots, K$$

The feasible region $\{\mathbf{w} : w_k \geq 0\}$ is the non-negative orthant, a closed convex cone. Because the objective is a convex quadratic and the feasible set is convex, the NNLS problem is a convex quadratic program with a global minimiser that is unique when $\mathbf{X}$ has full column rank. The canonical algorithm for solving this problem is the active-set method of Lawson and Hanson (1974), which iteratively partitions the index set $\{1, \dots, K\}$ into an active set $\mathcal{A}$ (indices with $w_k = 0$) and a passive set $\mathcal{P}$ (indices with $w_k > 0$), solving an unconstrained sub-problem restricted to $\mathcal{P}$ at each iteration and updating the partition until the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker optimality conditions are satisfied. [WEB:https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-negative_least_squares]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The Lawson-Hanson active-set algorithm and its KKT convergence criterion are well-established results confirmed by the Wikipedia NNLS article, which explicitly references the 1974 Lawson and Hanson text. The description of the active/passive partition is consistent with the documented algorithm.]

Ridge regression, which minimises the penalised objective $\|\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}\|_2^2 + \lambda \|\mathbf{w}\|_2^2$ for a regularisation parameter $\lambda > 0$, does not directly enforce non-negativity constraints. Its closed-form solution is $\mathbf{w}^*_{\mathrm{Ridge}} = (\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X} + \lambda \mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}$, which can yield negative entries for any value of $\lambda > 0$; the $\ell_2$ penalty shrinks the coefficient vector globally toward zero but does not constrain the sign of individual components. Lasso regression, which minimises $\|\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}\|_2^2 + \lambda \|\mathbf{w}\|_1$, promotes sparsity through the $\ell_1$ penalty and drives some weights to exactly zero, but it also cannot guarantee $w_k \geq 0$ for all $k$: the subgradient optimality conditions for the Lasso permit negative coefficients whenever the inner product of a predictor column $\mathbf{x}_k$ with the residual exceeds $\lambda/2$. Consequently, standard Ridge and Lasso formulations cannot serve as direct substitutes for the NNLS constraint; enforcing non-negativity requires either a dedicated NNLS solver or a general convex programming framework. [WEB:https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ridge_regression][WEB:https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-negative_least_squares]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The Ridge closed-form solution and the argument that it does not enforce non-negativity are standard results of penalized regression theory. The Lasso subgradient optimality argument is consistent with well-established $\ell_1$-regularization theory. Both claims are confirmed by the cited sources and are fundamental properties of these estimators.]

The following R code implements the NNLS formulation using the `nnls` package, which provides an R interface to the Lawson-Hanson algorithm. In this example, $\mathbf{X}$ is the $n \times K$ design matrix with entries $X_{ik} = \ln S_{a,k}(T_i)$ and $\mathbf{y}$ is the $n$-vector with entries $y_i = \ln S_a^{\mathrm{obj}}(T_i)$. [WEB:https://cran.r-project.org/package=nnls]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The code uses the documented interface of the nnls CRAN package as confirmed by the package page and the search results referencing the Lawson-Hanson algorithm implementation. The X and y definitions are consistent with the notation of the preceding sections.]

```r
# install.packages("nnls")
library(nnls)

# X: n x K design matrix,  X[i, k] = log(Sa_k(T_i))
# y: n-vector,              y[i]    = log(Sa_obj(T_i))

# Solve: minimize  ||X %*% w - y||_2^2  subject to  w >= 0
result <- nnls(A = X, b = y)

# Extract the non-negative weight vector
w_nnls    <- result$x              # K-vector of non-negative weights
residuals <- result$residuals      # n-vector: X %*% w_nnls - y
rmse      <- sqrt(mean(residuals^2))

cat("NNLS weights:", round(w_nnls, 4), "\n")
cat("RMSE (log-Sa):", round(rmse, 6), "\n")
```

## SLOT 6: Bounded Weights with Ridge/Lasso Feasibility

The NNLS formulation of SLOT 5 enforces a lower bound of zero on each weight but imposes no upper bound. An excessively large weight assigned to a single record effectively reduces the spectral combination to a single-record computation, negating the statistical rationale for assembling a multi-record suite and introducing sensitivity to the peculiarities of one event. Conversely, setting a positive lower bound $w_{\min} > 0$ ensures that every record in the library contributes at least minimally to the combination, which may be desirable when the library has been pre-screened and every selected record is deemed relevant. The box-constrained formulation extends SLOT 5 by imposing $w_{\min} \leq w_k \leq w_{\max}$ simultaneously for all $k = 1, \dots, K$, where $w_{\min} \geq 0$ and $w_{\max} < \infty$:^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The physical motivation for upper and lower bounds on individual weights is grounded in practical considerations of multi-record analysis and is consistent with the general principles described in the NIST and PEER references cited in earlier sections. No unsupported quantitative claim is made in this introductory paragraph.]

$$\mathbf{w}^* = \underset{\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^K}{\arg\min} \left\| \mathbf{X}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y} \right\|_2^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad w_{\min} \leq w_k \leq w_{\max} \; \text{for all} \; k = 1, \dots, K$$

This is a bounded-variable least-squares (BVLS) problem, a generalisation of NNLS in which each variable has both a finite lower bound and a finite upper bound. The feasible set $\{\mathbf{w} : w_{\min}\mathbf{1} \leq \mathbf{w} \leq w_{\max}\mathbf{1}\}$ is a compact convex polytope (a hyperrectangle in $\mathbb{R}^K$), and the convex quadratic objective guarantees a unique global minimiser within this set. Algorithmically, BVLS is solved by active-set methods analogous to the Lawson-Hanson procedure, or equivalently by casting the problem as a general quadratic program with $2K$ linear inequality constraints. [WEB:https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/quadprog/quadprog.pdf]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The BVLS formulation, its relationship to NNLS, and the characterisation of the feasible set as a compact convex polytope are standard results in constrained optimisation. The equivalence to a QP with $2K$ inequality constraints is a direct algebraic observation. The quadprog citation confirms the availability of this QP formulation in R.]

Ridge regression and Lasso regularisation cannot enforce box constraints of the form $w_{\min} \leq w_k \leq w_{\max}$ with arbitrary finite bounds. Ridge provides no mechanism to impose an upper bound on individual coefficients; its $\ell_2$ penalty shrinks the entire coefficient vector globally toward zero but cannot prevent any individual $w_k$ from exceeding $w_{\max}$ or from falling below a positive $w_{\min}$. Lasso's $\ell_1$ penalty can effectively set coefficients to zero (a special case of a lower bound at zero) but cannot impose a non-zero lower bound $w_{\min} > 0$ nor an explicit upper bound $w_{\max}$. The elastic net, which combines both $\ell_1$ and $\ell_2$ penalties, inherits both limitations: it provides shrinkage and sparsity promotion but no bounded-variable guarantee. Enforcing general box constraints therefore requires a dedicated BVLS solver or a general quadratic programming formulation, as provided by the `quadprog` or `bvls` packages in R. [WEB:https://cran.r-project.org/package=quadprog][WEB:https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ridge_regression]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The limitations of Ridge, Lasso, and Elastic Net for enforcing box constraints are fundamental properties of $\ell_1/\ell_2$ regularisation, well established in the penalised regression literature. The identification of quadprog and bvls as appropriate R tools for box-constrained QP is confirmed by the cited CRAN documentation. No source-dependent quantitative claim is made.]

The following R code demonstrates the box-constrained formulation using the `quadprog` package, which implements the Goldfarb-Idnani dual active-set algorithm for convex QPs. The QP standard form requires minimising $\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{D}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{d}^\top\mathbf{w}$; the spectral-matching objective $\frac{1}{n}\|\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}\|_2^2$ is mapped to this form by setting $\mathbf{D} = \frac{2}{n}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}$ and $\mathbf{d} = \frac{2}{n}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}$ (the constant $\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{y}^\top\mathbf{y}$ is dropped as it does not affect the optimiser). Box constraints are expressed in the Goldfarb-Idnani inequality form $\mathbf{A}^\top\mathbf{w} \geq \mathbf{b}_0$. [WEB:https://cran.r-project.org/package=quadprog]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The mapping from the least-squares objective to the quadprog QP form is a straightforward algebraic identity. The Goldfarb-Idnani algorithm attribution is confirmed by the quadprog CRAN documentation. The code below uses the documented API. No unsupported claim is made.]

```r
# install.packages("quadprog")
library(quadprog)

n     <- nrow(X)   # number of period ordinates
K     <- ncol(X)   # number of candidate spectra
w_min <- 0.0       # lower bound (non-negative)
w_max <- 2.0       # upper bound (example: weight cannot exceed 2)

# Objective: (1/n) * ||X %*% w - y||^2
# QP form:   min  0.5 * w' D w - d' w
Dmat <- (2 / n) * crossprod(X)     # K x K positive-definite matrix
dvec <- (2 / n) * crossprod(X, y)  # K-vector

# Box constraints: w >= w_min  AND  w <= w_max
# Goldfarb-Idnani form:  A' w >= b0
# Stack: [I | -I]' w >= [w_min * 1 | -w_max * 1]
Amat <- cbind(diag(K), -diag(K))           # K x 2K
bvec <- c(rep(w_min, K), rep(-w_max, K))   # 2K-vector

result   <- solve.QP(Dmat = Dmat, dvec = dvec,
                     Amat = Amat, bvec = bvec)
w_bounded <- result$solution   # K-vector satisfying box constraints
rmse_bvls <- sqrt(mean((X %*% w_bounded - y)^2))

cat("Box-constrained weights:", round(w_bounded, 4), "\n")
cat("RMSE (log-Sa):", round(rmse_bvls, 6), "\n")
```

## SLOT 7: Convex Optimization Generalization with R Libraries

The formulations in SLOTS 3 through 6 are all instances of a single parametric convex optimisation problem that subsumes unconstrained least squares, NNLS, box-constrained least squares, Ridge, Lasso, and elastic-net regression as special cases. Letting $\lambda_1 \geq 0$ and $\lambda_2 \geq 0$ denote $\ell_1$ and $\ell_2$ regularisation intensities respectively, and letting $w_{\min}$ and $w_{\max}$ define the component-wise bounds (with $w_{\min} = 0$ for non-negativity and $w_{\max} = +\infty$ for no upper bound), the general spectral-matching convex optimisation problem is:^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: This paragraph introduces the general parametric family and lists special cases. The identification of all listed formulations as special cases of the combined objective is a standard result in convex optimisation and regularised regression theory, with no source-dependent quantitative claim.]

$$\mathbf{w}^* = \underset{\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^K}{\arg\min} \; \frac{1}{n}\left\|\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}\right\|_2^2 + \lambda_1 \left\|\mathbf{w}\right\|_1 + \lambda_2 \left\|\mathbf{w}\right\|_2^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad w_{\min} \leq w_k \leq w_{\max} \; \forall\, k$$

The combined objective is a sum of the convex quadratic data-fidelity term $\frac{1}{n}\|\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{y}\|_2^2$, the convex $\ell_1$ penalty $\lambda_1\|\mathbf{w}\|_1$ (which promotes sparsity in the weight vector, effectively selecting a small active subset of records from the library), and the strictly convex $\ell_2$ penalty $\lambda_2\|\mathbf{w}\|_2^2$ (which penalises excessively large individual weights and improves conditioning in the presence of highly correlated candidate spectra). The feasible set defined by the box constraints is a closed convex polytope. The combined problem is therefore a convex optimisation problem over a convex feasible set; when $\lambda_2 > 0$ the objective is strictly convex in $\mathbf{w}$ and the global minimiser is unique; when $\lambda_2 = 0$ a unique minimiser still exists if $\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}$ is positive definite. [ARXIV:1711.07582]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The convexity argument, the sparsity interpretation of L1 penalty, and the uniqueness conditions under strict convexity are standard results in convex optimisation confirmed by the CVXR reference (arXiv 1711.07582), which provides the disciplined convex programming framework within which such problems are formalised. No quantitative claim requiring specific empirical evidence is made.]

The special cases recovered from this general form are as follows. Plain least squares (SLOT 3) corresponds to $\lambda_1 = \lambda_2 = 0$ with no box constraints. NNLS (SLOT 5) corresponds to $\lambda_1 = \lambda_2 = 0$ with $w_{\min} = 0$ and $w_{\max} = +\infty$. Box-constrained least squares (SLOT 6) corresponds to $\lambda_1 = \lambda_2 = 0$ with finite $w_{\min}$ and $w_{\max}$. Ridge regression corresponds to $\lambda_1 = 0$, $\lambda_2 > 0$, and no box constraints. Lasso corresponds to $\lambda_1 > 0$, $\lambda_2 = 0$, and no box constraints. Elastic net with box constraints uses $\lambda_1, \lambda_2 > 0$ simultaneously and adds the polytope feasibility constraint. The $\ell_1$ penalty can be incorporated into a QP only after a linear reformulation that introduces auxiliary variables $\mathbf{u} \geq \mathbf{0}$ satisfying $-\mathbf{u} \leq \mathbf{w} \leq \mathbf{u}$; for this reason, solvers that handle second-order cone programs or disciplined convex programs (such as CVXR) are preferred when $\lambda_1 > 0$. [ARXIV:1711.07582][WEB:https://cran.r-project.org/package=quadprog]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The enumeration of special cases and the LP reformulation of the L1 norm via auxiliary variables are standard results of convex optimisation theory. The citation of CVXR as a suitable solver for the combined problem is confirmed by the arXiv paper. The quadprog limitation to $\lambda_1 = 0$ cases follows from its quadratic programming scope.]

The following R code implements the general convex formulation using the `CVXR` package, which provides a disciplined convex programming interface. The problem is expressed in mathematical form using CVXR's symbolic variable and constraint objects and solved via an embedded conic solver (CLARABEL or SCS). [ARXIV:1711.07582]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The CVXR code uses the documented API as confirmed by the arXiv CVXR paper. The use of sum_squares for the data-fidelity term, norm1 for the L1 penalty, and sum_squares for the L2 penalty is consistent with the CVXR function naming documented in the package. The internal solver chain (CLARABEL/SCS) is confirmed by the package description.]

```r
# install.packages("CVXR")
library(CVXR)

n       <- nrow(X)    # number of period ordinates
K       <- ncol(X)    # number of candidate spectra

# Penalty parameters and bounds
lambda1 <- 0.0        # L1 (Lasso) weight; set > 0 to promote sparsity
lambda2 <- 0.01       # L2 (Ridge) weight; set > 0 for shrinkage / conditioning
w_min   <- 0.0        # lower bound (0 for non-negativity)
w_max   <- 2.0        # upper bound (finite to prevent single-record dominance)

# Define CVXR symbolic variable
w <- Variable(K)

# Objective components
data_fidelity <- (1 / n) * sum_squares(X %*% w - y)
l1_penalty    <- lambda1 * norm1(w)
l2_penalty    <- lambda2 * sum_squares(w)
objective     <- Minimize(data_fidelity + l1_penalty + l2_penalty)

# Box constraints
constraints <- list(w >= w_min, w <= w_max)

# Assemble and solve the problem
problem <- Problem(objective, constraints)
result  <- solve(problem)

w_opt  <- result$getValue(w)
mse    <- result$value   # optimal objective value (MSE + penalties)
rmse   <- sqrt((1 / n) * sum((X %*% w_opt - y)^2))

cat("Optimal weights (CVXR):", round(w_opt, 4), "\n")
cat("RMSE (log-Sa):", round(rmse, 6), "\n")
```

When $\lambda_1 = 0$, the general objective reduces to a pure quadratic program and the `quadprog` package provides an efficient alternative. The following code demonstrates the pure QP formulation with Ridge-type regularisation and box constraints, mapping the spectral-matching objective into the standard Goldfarb-Idnani form. The $\ell_2$ regularisation term $\lambda_2\|\mathbf{w}\|_2^2$ is absorbed into the Hessian matrix as $\mathbf{D} = \frac{2}{n}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X} + 2\lambda_2 \mathbf{I}$, which ensures positive definiteness and hence strict convexity even when $\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}$ is singular. [WEB:https://cran.r-project.org/package=quadprog]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The absorption of the Ridge penalty into the QP Hessian is a standard algebraic manipulation. The statement that adding $2\lambda_2\mathbf{I}$ ensures positive definiteness for $\lambda_2 > 0$ follows directly from the positive definiteness of the identity matrix. The quadprog CRAN documentation confirms the Goldfarb-Idnani form and the solve.QP interface.]

```r
library(quadprog)

n       <- nrow(X)
K       <- ncol(X)
lambda2 <- 0.01    # Ridge regularisation (L2 penalty weight)
w_min   <- 0.0
w_max   <- 2.0

# QP objective: (1/n)||Xw - y||^2 + lambda2 * ||w||^2
# = (1/2) w' D w - d' w  (constant term omitted)
Dmat <- (2 / n) * crossprod(X) + 2 * lambda2 * diag(K)  # K x K, PD
dvec <- (2 / n) * crossprod(X, y)                         # K-vector

# Box constraints in Goldfarb-Idnani form: A' w >= b0
# Stack identity (w >= w_min) and negated identity (-w >= -w_max)
Amat <- cbind(diag(K), -diag(K))           # K x 2K
bvec <- c(rep(w_min, K), rep(-w_max, K))   # 2K-vector

result    <- solve.QP(Dmat = Dmat, dvec = dvec,
                      Amat = Amat, bvec = bvec)
w_qp      <- result$solution
rmse_qp   <- sqrt(mean((X %*% w_qp - y)^2))

cat("Optimal weights (quadprog):", round(w_qp, 4), "\n")
cat("RMSE (log-Sa):", round(rmse_qp, 6), "\n")
```
