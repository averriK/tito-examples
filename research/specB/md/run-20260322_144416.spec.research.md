# Spectral Matching as Linear Regression: Methodology, Formulation, and Optimization

## SLOT 1: Summary of Spectral-Matching Record-Selection Methodologies

Ground-motion record-selection methodologies aimed at achieving conformity between the spectral properties of a selected suite and a PSHA-derived target spectrum fall into two broad and conceptually distinct families, differentiated by whether the physical time histories of the candidate accelerograms are altered before analysis. The first family encompasses waveform-modification techniques, in which the time history of a recorded accelerogram is directly altered either in the time domain through additive wavelet injection - as implemented in programs such as RSPMatch and similar iterative algorithms - or in the frequency domain through iterative Fourier amplitude rescaling followed by inverse transformation. These methods can achieve period-by-period spectral conformity for individual records, but they systematically distort the non-stationary amplitude envelope, alter Arias intensity, modify the sign-reversal count of the acceleration time series, and change the input energy spectrum of the record. Each of these modifications compromises the accuracy of structural response estimates for systems whose behavior depends on cumulative damage accumulation, liquefaction potential, or effective cycle count, because those response quantities are sensitive not only to spectral amplitude but also to the duration and sequence of loading cycles. [WEB:https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10518-022-01393-0]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The characterization of time-domain and frequency-domain waveform-modification methods and their documented limitations with respect to Arias intensity, sign reversals, and non-stationary content is consistent with the cited Bulletin of Earthquake Engineering source on selection and spectral matching of recorded ground motions, and is well-established in the earthquake engineering record-selection literature.]

The second family - to which the linear-combination formulation belongs - preserves the physical waveforms of the candidate accelerograms intact or applies only a single global amplitude scale factor to each record, and achieves spectral conformity at the suite level rather than at the individual-record level. In its most direct form the method assigns a weight $w_k$ to each of $K$ candidate records and requires that the weighted linear combination of spectral ordinates $\sum_{k=1}^K w_k S_{a,k}(T_i)$ approximates the target value $S_a^{\mathrm{obj}}(T_i)$ across the full period mesh $\{T_1, \dots, T_n\}$. Early implementations of this concept were essentially manual or semi-automated scaling exercises constrained to a small candidate set, with scale factors chosen to minimize the sum of squared errors between the scaled individual spectra and the target over a designated period range [WEB:https://www.nehrp.gov/pdf/nistgcr11-917-15.pdf]. The defining advantage over waveform modification is that the original time histories - including their non-stationary amplitude content, phase structure, duration characteristics, and frequency variation over time - remain unchanged, making the selected records suitable for analyses sensitive to cumulative inelastic demands, ground-motion duration, and effective number of load cycles.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The description of the linear-combination suite-level approach and its distinction from waveform modification is well-supported by the cited NIST GCR 11-917-15 report, which is the primary NEHRP reference document on selecting and scaling earthquake ground motions. The advantage of preserving non-stationary waveform characteristics is a widely recognized property of this family of methods in the earthquake engineering literature.]

Probabilistic record-selection frameworks refined the suite-level matching objective by anchoring the target spectrum explicitly to probabilistic seismic hazard analysis. Baker and Cornell (2006) demonstrated that records selected to match the conditional mean spectrum (CMS) - defined as the expected value of $\ln S_a(T)$ at all periods conditioned on a target hazard level and the spectral shape parameter $\varepsilon$ - produce structural response estimates that are unbiased relative to hazard-consistent benchmarks, whereas records chosen only to envelope the uniform-hazard spectrum (UHS) introduce systematic conservatism [WEB:https://web.stanford.edu/~bakerjw/Publications/Baker_Cornell_(2006)_EESD_record_selection.pdf]. Subsequent work by Jayaram, Lin, and Baker (2011) generalized the target to include both the conditional mean and variance of $\ln S_a(T)$ across all periods, formulating record selection as a greedy iterative optimization in which records are swapped into and out of the suite to reduce the total squared deviation between the suite log-mean spectrum and the target [WEB:https://peer.berkeley.edu/sites/default/files/baker_et_al_2011_peer_gm_report.pdf]. Energy-compatible selection methods extended the target criterion further to include the input energy equivalent velocity spectrum alongside the acceleration spectrum, reducing bias in structural response for long-duration records and records from stiff-soil sites [WEB:https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/built-environment/articles/10.3389/fbuil.2019.00140/full]. Multi-objective optimization algorithms - based on harmony search, genetic algorithms, or stochastic meta-heuristics - address the selection problem with multiple simultaneous fitness criteria (spectral fit, magnitude, source-to-site distance, site condition) without requiring a scalar weighting between competing objectives [WEB:https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10518-022-01450-8]. The linear-regression formulation described in the subsequent sections provides a unified algebraic framework for the suite-level matching problem in its most direct form.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The probabilistic CMS framework and its attribution to Baker and Cornell (2006), the Jayaram-Lin-Baker (2011) greedy algorithm, the energy-compatible approach, and multi-objective meta-heuristic methods are all cited with specific references to peer-reviewed publications and PEER technical reports that are primary and authoritative sources in the earthquake engineering record-selection literature.]

## SLOT 2: Minimum Number of Records and Code-Based Requirements

In the linear-combination framework, the minimum number of spectra $K$ required to obtain a feasible spectral match can be analyzed from two perspectives: the algebraic structure of the optimization problem and the statistical quality of the resulting ensemble. From the algebraic standpoint, the design matrix $\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times K}$ has $n$ rows corresponding to period ordinates and $K$ columns corresponding to candidate records. When $K < n$ the system is overdetermined and the ordinary least-squares solution minimizes the residual norm $\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{b}\|_2$ without reducing it to zero in general, with the residual decreasing monotonically as $K$ increases provided the additional records contribute linearly independent spectral shapes. When $K = n$ and $\mathbf{A}$ has full rank, an exact zero-residual solution exists; when $K > n$ the system is underdetermined and additional constraints or regularization are required for uniqueness. In the spectral-matching context, the period mesh typically contains $n = 50$ to $100$ points while $K$ rarely exceeds 20, so the relevant regime is always overdetermined, and the theoretical algebraic minimum for a valid regression is $K = 1$ [WEB:https://pubs.usgs.gov/of/2011/1083/].^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The algebraic analysis of overdetermined versus underdetermined regimes is standard linear algebra applied correctly to the spectral-matching design matrix. The claim that n is typically 50-100 and K rarely exceeds 20 is representative of standard engineering practice but relies on domain knowledge not directly cited. The USGS source cited provides context for the 7-record statistical threshold rather than the algebraic minimum specifically.]

The minimum number of records in engineering practice is governed not by algebraic feasibility but by the statistical requirement that the mean engineering demand parameter (EDP) from the suite adequately represent the hazard-consistent expected value. The seven-record threshold in ASCE/SEI 7-10 Chapter 16 was originally established based on engineering experience rather than a systematic statistical study, as explicitly acknowledged in the USGS open-file report that subsequently provided empirical justification [WEB:https://pubs.usgs.gov/of/2011/1083/]. Jayaram and Baker (2010) demonstrated through Monte Carlo experiments assembling 480 ground-motion sets by random sampling from a large database that suites of fewer than seven records yield EDP estimates that are statistically conservative relative to benchmark values computed from large ensembles, and that utilizing seven or more randomly selected records produces suite-mean EDPs that converge to within acceptable bounds of the benchmark [WEB:https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70042370]. Selecting records on the basis of spectral shape and design spectral acceleration rather than randomly further improves efficiency, allowing equivalent accuracy with fewer records. The underlying statistical rationale for the seven-record rule is therefore a sample-mean convergence criterion: the standard error of the mean EDP scales as $\sigma/\sqrt{K}$, and seven records represent an empirically calibrated balance between computational cost and the reliability of the suite mean as a proxy for the hazard-consistent expected response.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The origin of the 7-record threshold in engineering experience and its subsequent statistical validation by Jayaram and Baker through Monte Carlo analysis of 480 record sets are directly stated in the two cited USGS sources, which are the primary references for this specific question. The sample-mean convergence argument ($\sigma/\sqrt{K}$) is standard statistical reasoning that explains the threshold in mathematical terms.]

The eleven-record minimum was introduced in the comprehensively revised Chapter 16 of ASCE/SEI 7-16, which superseded the three- and seven-record provisions of prior editions with a single, tighter threshold tailored to three-dimensional nonlinear response history analysis [WEB:https://www.structuremag.org/article/seismic-time-histories-a-practical-approach/]. Under ASCE/SEI 7-16, three-dimensional NRHA requires a minimum of eleven ground-motion pairs (two horizontal components per pair) for the engineer to use the mean EDP from the suite as the design value without defaulting to the maximum. The increase from seven to eleven reflects both the tighter spectral conformance requirements imposed on the selected suite relative to the risk-targeted maximum considered earthquake (MCER) spectrum and the need for three-dimensional analysis to sample both horizontal ground-motion components coherently across a sufficient range of incident azimuths [WEB:https://www.researchgate.net/publication/337886040_ASCE_7-16_Ground-motion_Scaling_Procedure_For_Nonlinear_Analysis_Of_Buildings_In_Comparison_With_ASCE_7-10_And_ASCE_7-05]. The eleven-record minimum is thus a regulatory threshold calibrated to ensure that mean-based design is statistically defensible under the revised acceptance criteria of ASCE/SEI 7-16, rather than a number derived analytically from the linear-combination optimization theory itself.^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The eleven-record minimum and its connection to ASCE/SEI 7-16 Chapter 16 requirements for three-dimensional NRHA are supported by the two cited sources. The specific calibration process that produced eleven (rather than ten or twelve) and the detailed statistical derivation are not directly documented in the available citations; the rationale described is consistent with the cited sources but involves engineering inference about the intent of the code revision.]

## SLOT 3: Classical Linear Regression Formulation (RMSE Minimization)

The spectral-matching problem in the linear (arithmetic) spectral domain is cast as a classical linear regression by assembling the spectral ordinates of the $K$ candidate records into a design matrix $\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times K}$ whose columns correspond to individual record spectra evaluated at the $n$ period points of the mesh $\{T_1, \dots, T_n\}$.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The designation of the problem as a classical linear regression through assembly of a design matrix is a straightforward algebraic framing consistent with the notation established in the task Context. No external citation is required for this definitional lead-in; the formulation is internally consistent with standard linear least-squares theory.]

$$A_{ik} = S_{a,k}(T_i), \quad i = 1, \dots, n, \quad k = 1, \dots, K.$$

Each column $\mathbf{A}_{\cdot k} = (S_{a,k}(T_1), \dots, S_{a,k}(T_n))^\top$ contains the discretized response spectrum of the $k$-th candidate record, and each row $\mathbf{A}_{i\cdot}$ contains the spectral accelerations of all $K$ records at the single period $T_i$. The target spectrum in the linear domain is represented by the vector $\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ with entries $b_i = S_a^{\mathrm{obj}}(T_i) = \exp(y_i)$, where $\mathbf{y}$ is the log-domain target vector defined in the Context with components $y_i = \ln S_a^{\mathrm{obj}}(T_i)$. [WEB:https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10518-022-01393-0]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The definition of each column and row of the design matrix, and the relationship $b_i = \exp(y_i)$ between the linear-domain target and the log-domain target defined in the Context, are internally consistent definitional statements. The cited source on selection and spectral matching confirms the use of such design matrix structure in spectral-matching regression.]

The objective of the spectral-matching regression is to find the weight vector $\mathbf{w} = (w_1, \dots, w_K)^\top$ that minimizes the global root-mean-square error (RMSE) between the weighted linear combination of candidate spectra and the target spectrum across all $n$ period ordinates.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The goal of minimizing the global RMSE as the spectral-matching objective is stated directly in the task specification and is consistent with the weighted sum-of-squared-errors criterion widely used in the earthquake engineering literature on spectral matching.]

$$\mathrm{RMSE}(\mathbf{w}) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left(\sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k S_{a,k}(T_i) - b_i\right)^2} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}} \|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{b}\|_2.$$

Minimizing $\mathrm{RMSE}(\mathbf{w})$ over $\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^K$ is equivalent to minimizing the squared Euclidean norm $\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{b}\|_2^2$, since the factor $1/\sqrt{n}$ is a positive constant that does not alter the location of the minimizer. The resulting problem is the unconstrained ordinary least-squares (OLS) regression problem, which is the canonical formulation for minimizing the sum of squared residuals over an affinely parameterized model.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The equivalence between RMSE minimization and squared-norm minimization through the monotone square-root is elementary algebra. The identification of the problem as OLS is standard linear regression theory.]

When the design matrix $\mathbf{A}$ has full column rank, meaning $\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{A}) = K$ (which requires $n \geq K$ and no pair of candidate spectra to be linearly dependent over the period mesh), the gradient of $\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{b}\|_2^2$ with respect to $\mathbf{w}$ is $2\mathbf{A}^\top(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{b})$, and setting this to zero yields the normal equations $\mathbf{A}^\top \mathbf{A}\, \mathbf{w} = \mathbf{A}^\top \mathbf{b}$, whose unique solution is the closed-form OLS estimator.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The normal equations and the full-column-rank condition for their unique solvability are standard results in linear algebra and OLS regression theory. The gradient computation and the resulting normal equations are mathematically rigorous.]

$$\mathbf{w}^* = (\mathbf{A}^\top \mathbf{A})^{-1} \mathbf{A}^\top \mathbf{b}.$$

The matrix $\mathbf{A}^\top \mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times K}$ is the Gram matrix of the candidate spectra, with $(j,k)$-th entry $\sum_{i=1}^n S_{a,j}(T_i)\,S_{a,k}(T_i)$ measuring the spectral similarity between records $j$ and $k$ over the period mesh. The vector $\mathbf{A}^\top \mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^K$ contains the cross-correlations between each candidate spectrum and the target. When $\mathbf{A}$ lacks full column rank - for instance when two candidate records share nearly identical spectral shapes - the Gram matrix is singular or ill-conditioned, and the minimum-norm solution is obtained via the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse $\mathbf{w}^* = \mathbf{A}^+ \mathbf{b} = (\mathbf{A}^\top \mathbf{A})^{+} \mathbf{A}^\top \mathbf{b}$. In practice, even mild near-collinearity in the candidate set can yield weight magnitudes with large alternating signs, motivating the regularized and constrained extensions developed in subsequent sections. [WEB:https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10518-022-01393-0]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The interpretation of the Gram matrix entries as spectral inner products, the pseudoinverse solution for rank-deficient cases, and the practical observation that near-collinear candidate spectra lead to unstable large-magnitude weights are standard results in numerical linear algebra, consistent with the cited source on spectral-matching regression and widely corroborated in the broader regression literature.]

## SLOT 4: Log-Log Space Reformulation

Spectral acceleration as a function of vibration period exhibits approximately power-law behavior across the engineering period range: beyond the spectral plateau, $S_a(T)$ decays roughly as $T^{-\alpha}$ for some positive exponent $\alpha$, so that $\ln S_a$ varies nearly linearly with $\ln T$. This power-law structure means that spectral ordinates span several orders of magnitude when periods range from $0.01$ s to $10$ s, and in a regression performed in the linear domain the large absolute values at short periods exert disproportionate leverage on the fit, distorting the match at longer periods where inelastic displacement demands are typically most relevant. Working in log-log coordinates compresses the dynamic range uniformly across the period axis, renders spectral shapes smoother and better approximated by linear models in the transformed space, and aligns with the standard probabilistic seismic hazard analysis convention of treating $\ln S_a(T)$ as normally distributed [WEB:https://web.stanford.edu/~bakerjw/Publications/Baker_Cornell_(2006)_EESD_record_selection.pdf].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The power-law decay of spectral acceleration with period and the consequent motivation for log-log regression (dynamic range compression, reduced leverage of short-period ordinates, alignment with PSHA log-normality) are well-established properties of ground-motion response spectra, directly supported by the Baker-Cornell (2006) paper on spectral shape and record selection, which is a primary and widely cited reference for this methodological choice.]

The log-log reformulation is obtained by applying the natural logarithm to all spectral ordinates. The transformed design matrix $\tilde{\mathbf{A}} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times K}$ retains the same row and column structure as $\mathbf{A}$ but replaces each entry with its logarithm.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The definition of the transformed design matrix by element-wise logarithm is a straightforward application of the natural log transformation and is internally consistent with the established notation. No external citation is required for this definitional statement.]

$$\tilde{A}_{ik} = \ln S_{a,k}(T_i), \quad i = 1, \dots, n, \quad k = 1, \dots, K.$$

The corresponding transformed target is the vector $\mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ already defined in the Context with components $y_i = \ln S_a^{\mathrm{obj}}(T_i)$. The weight vector $\mathbf{w} = (w_1, \dots, w_K)^\top$ retains the same definition as in SLOT 3 and remains the unknown to be estimated. The objective function in the log-log reformulation is the mean squared log-spectral error, which measures the average squared discrepancy in log spectral amplitude units between the weighted log-spectral combination of the candidate records and the log-target.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The reuse of $\mathbf{y}$ from the Context as the log-domain target and the definition of the log-log objective in terms of squared log-spectral discrepancies are direct consequences of the log transformation and are internally consistent with the established notation.]

$$\mathrm{RMSE}_{\log}(\mathbf{w}) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}} \|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}\|_2 = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left(\sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k \ln S_{a,k}(T_i) - y_i\right)^2}.$$

Minimizing $\mathrm{RMSE}_{\log}(\mathbf{w})$ over $\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^K$ is again an OLS problem on the transformed quantities $(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}, \mathbf{y})$, and the closed-form solution retains the same structural form as in SLOT 3 with $\tilde{\mathbf{A}}$ replacing $\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{y}$ replacing $\mathbf{b}$.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The reduction of the log-log minimization to an OLS problem in the transformed variables is a direct consequence of the linearity of the model in $\mathbf{w}$ even after the log transformation, and follows immediately from the analysis of SLOT 3 by substitution. The derivation is internally consistent.]

$$\mathbf{w}^* = (\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top \tilde{\mathbf{A}})^{-1} \tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top \mathbf{y},$$

provided $\tilde{\mathbf{A}}$ has full column rank. The fitted log-spectral value at period $T_i$ under this model is $\hat{y}_i = \sum_{k=1}^K w_k \ln S_{a,k}(T_i)$, which in the original spectral domain corresponds to a geometric weighted combination of the candidate spectra.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The closed-form solution and the algebraic relationship between the log-domain fitted value and the geometric weighted combination in the original domain are mathematically exact consequences of the log transformation. The derivation is rigorous and internally consistent.]

$$\hat{S}_a(T_i) = \exp(\hat{y}_i) = \prod_{k=1}^{K} S_{a,k}(T_i)^{w_k}.$$

The reconstructed spectrum is therefore a geometric weighted mean of the candidate spectra in which the weights $w_k$ serve as exponents. This structure is consistent with the log-normal parametrization of spectral variability used in PSHA: the weights represent the relative log-spectral contributions of each candidate record, and the minimized $\mathrm{RMSE}_{\log}$ measures the mean squared error in log spectral amplitude units - the natural units for PSHA-derived targets [WEB:https://peer.berkeley.edu/sites/default/files/baker_et_al_2011_peer_gm_report.pdf]. Compared to the linear-domain formulation of SLOT 3, the log-log regression assigns equal relative weight to errors across the entire period range rather than being dominated by the absolute magnitudes of short-period spectral peaks, generally producing a more uniform and engineering-relevant spectral fit.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The geometric-mean interpretation and its connection to PSHA log-normality are mathematically exact and consistent with the cited Baker et al. PEER report, which treats ground-motion selection in terms of log-spectral distributions. The comparative advantage of log-log regression over linear-domain regression in terms of period-range uniformity is a well-established property supported by the cited source.]

## SLOT 5: Non-Negative Weight Constraint and R Implementation

In the spectral-matching context each weight $w_k$ represents the contribution of the $k$-th candidate record's (log-)spectrum to the ensemble match. A negative value of $w_k$ implies that the record's spectral contribution is subtracted from the combination, which has no physical interpretation in the context of ground-motion scaling: structural analysis cannot apply a negative scale factor to an accelerogram. Negative weights also risk producing a fitted composite spectrum that deviates arbitrarily from the individual candidate spectra and assumes shapes that no physical ground motion could produce. The physical requirement therefore restricts the weight vector to the non-negative orthant, yielding the non-negative least-squares (NNLS) problem.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The physical motivation for non-negativity (no subtraction of a ground motion record's spectral contribution, no negative scale factor) is a well-recognized engineering constraint in ground-motion selection practice, consistent with the NNLS literature and the general earthquake engineering context of the problem.]

$$\min_{\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^K} \;\|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}\|_2^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad w_k \geq 0, \quad k = 1, \dots, K.$$

The NNLS problem is a strictly convex quadratic program with $K$ non-negativity constraints, and its solution satisfies the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions for constrained quadratic minimization. Records for which the constraint is active at the solution (i.e., $w_k^* = 0$) are effectively excluded from the ensemble, so the NNLS solution inherently produces a sparse subset of records drawn from the original candidate set. [WEB:https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-negative_least_squares]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The characterization of NNLS as a convex QP with KKT conditions and the sparsity of the solution (some $w_k^* = 0$) are standard results in convex optimization theory and NNLS analysis, consistent with the cited source and the broader NNLS literature.]

Ridge regression adds an L2 penalty $\lambda \|\mathbf{w}\|_2^2$ to the OLS objective, yielding the modified normal equations $(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top \tilde{\mathbf{A}} + \lambda \mathbf{I})\mathbf{w} = \tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top \mathbf{y}$, whose unique solution exists for all $\lambda > 0$. However, this solution can contain negative components because the L2 regularization shrinks coefficient magnitudes toward zero without altering their sign: geometrically, the feasible region of the Ridge problem is the interior of a Euclidean ball centered at the origin in $\mathbb{R}^K$, which includes all $2^K$ orthants, and the constrained optimum lies wherever the elliptical OLS contour first intersects the ball with no mechanism to exclude negative orthants [WEB:https://www.sthda.com/english/articles/37-model-selection-essentials-in-r/153-penalized-regression-essentials-ridge-lasso-elastic-net/]. Standard Lasso regularization (L1 penalty $\lambda \|\mathbf{w}\|_1$) induces sparsity at the corners of the L1 constraint polytope, but the non-zero coefficients on the Lasso solution path are not constrained in sign; a non-negative variant of the Lasso (nnlasso) exists in R but is a distinct algorithm from the standard Ridge or Lasso solvers [WEB:https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/nnlasso/].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The statement that standard Ridge regression cannot enforce non-negativity due to the geometry of the L2 ball, and that standard Lasso does not prevent negative non-zero coefficients, are well-established properties of penalized regression supported by the cited STHDA source and the general statistical learning literature. The existence of a separate nnlasso package for non-negative Lasso is confirmed by the cited CRAN resource.]

The NNLS problem is solved efficiently by the Lawson-Hanson active-set algorithm, originally described by Lawson and Hanson (1974), which partitions the index set $\{1, \dots, K\}$ into an active set $\mathcal{Z}$ (indices where $w_k = 0$ is enforced) and a passive set $\mathcal{P}$ (indices free to be positive), solves the unconstrained OLS sub-problem restricted to $\mathcal{P}$, and iterates until the KKT conditions for non-negativity are satisfied [WEB:https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/nnls/]. In R the `nnls` package exposes this algorithm directly through a single function call.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The Lawson-Hanson active-set algorithm is the classical and most widely implemented solution for NNLS problems. Its description in terms of active/passive set partitioning and KKT termination is accurate and consistent with the cited CRAN documentation for the nnls package.]

```r
library(nnls)

# A_tilde : n-by-K design matrix of log-transformed spectral ordinates
# y       : length-n target vector of log-transformed target spectral values

fit  <- nnls(A = A_tilde, b = y)
w_nn <- fit$x          # non-negative weight vector, length K
res  <- fit$residuals  # vector y - A_tilde %*% w_nn
rmse <- sqrt(mean(res^2))
cat("NNLS RMSE (log-spectral units):", rmse, "\n")
```

^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The R code is consistent with the documented interface of the nnls package on CRAN. The function nnls(A, b) returns an object with components x (solution) and residuals (y - A %*% x), as documented. The code is syntactically correct for R and implements the NNLS problem formulated above.]

The object `fit$x` contains the optimal non-negative weight vector $\mathbf{w}^*$ satisfying $w_k^* \geq 0$ for all $k$. Indices where $w_k^* = 0$ correspond to records eliminated from the ensemble by the active-set constraint, so the output of the NNLS solver simultaneously provides a spectral fit and a record selection, with the degree of sparsity increasing as the candidate spectra in $\tilde{\mathbf{A}}$ become more correlated or as the target $\mathbf{y}$ lies within the cone spanned by fewer records. [WEB:https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/nnls/]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The interpretation of zero-weight indices as eliminated records and the connection between spectral correlation and solution sparsity are direct consequences of the Lawson-Hanson active-set construction, consistent with the cited CRAN documentation and the convex geometry of the NNLS problem.]

## SLOT 6: Bounded Weights with Ridge/Lasso Feasibility

Box constraints extend the non-negativity restriction by imposing simultaneous lower and upper bounds on each weight component: $w_{\min} \leq w_k \leq w_{\max}$ for all $k = 1, \dots, K$. A strictly positive lower bound $w_{\min} > 0$ ensures that every candidate record in a preselected suite retains at least a minimum spectral contribution to the ensemble, which may be required when the set of records is specified by regulatory or engineering criteria and the analyst cannot discard any of them. An upper bound $w_{\max}$ prevents the optimizer from assigning an extreme scale factor to a single dominant record, which would reduce the ensemble effectively to a near-single-record analysis and negate the statistical benefit of suite-based response averaging.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The physical motivation for box constraints in the spectral-matching context (minimum contribution per record for regulatory compliance, maximum scale factor to prevent single-record dominance) follows directly from standard earthquake engineering practice and the engineering requirements of code-conforming ground-motion selection. No external citation is required for this physical reasoning.]

$$\min_{\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^K} \;\|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}\|_2^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad w_{\min} \leq w_k \leq w_{\max}, \quad k = 1, \dots, K.$$

The bounded-weight spectral-matching problem is a strictly convex quadratic program when $w_{\min} < w_{\max}$ and the feasible set is non-empty. The constraint set $\{w_{\min} \leq w_k \leq w_{\max}\}$ is a box (axis-aligned hyperrectangle) in $\mathbb{R}^K$, which is a closed bounded convex set. Setting $w_{\min} = 0$ and $w_{\max} = +\infty$ recovers the NNLS problem of SLOT 5 as a special case.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The characterization of the bounded LS problem as a strictly convex QP with a box constraint set, and the reduction to NNLS when $w_{\min} = 0$ and $w_{\max} = +\infty$, are mathematically exact and follow from standard convex quadratic programming theory.]

Neither Ridge nor Lasso regularization can substitute for hard box constraints on the weight coefficients. Ridge regression produces the closed-form solution $\mathbf{w}^*_{\mathrm{Ridge}} = (\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top \tilde{\mathbf{A}} + \lambda \mathbf{I})^{-1} \tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top \mathbf{y}$, which is an unconstrained solution on $\mathbb{R}^K$; while large $\lambda$ shrinks all components toward zero, this does not enforce a specific per-component upper bound $w_{\max}$, and the shrinkage is uniform across all components rather than component-specific. Lasso induces exact sparsity by setting some coefficients to zero at the corners of the L1 constraint polytope, which would violate a non-trivial requirement $w_{\min} > 0$ for those zeroed components. Both Ridge and Lasso operate through penalty terms added to the objective - soft discouragements - rather than through hard feasibility constraints, and neither can enforce simultaneous lower and upper bounds on individual coefficients [WEB:https://www.sthda.com/english/articles/37-model-selection-essentials-in-r/153-penalized-regression-essentials-ridge-lasso-elastic-net/].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The claim that Ridge and Lasso cannot enforce hard box constraints is a correct and fundamental distinction between penalized regression and constrained optimization. Ridge produces an unconstrained closed-form solution, and Lasso zeroes coefficients rather than bounding them. These properties are consistent with the cited source on penalized regression and with standard statistical learning theory.]

The bounded-weight LS problem is expressed as a quadratic program (QP) suitable for `quadprog::solve.QP`, which minimizes $\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{D}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{d}^\top \mathbf{w}$ subject to $\mathbf{C}^\top \mathbf{w} \geq \mathbf{b}_c$. ## SLOT 7: Convex Optimization Generalization with R Libraries

The spectral-matching problem admits a unified convex formulation that subsumes the unconstrained OLS, the NNLS, and the bounded-weight LS problems as special cases and accommodates additional convex penalty terms. The general loss function combines a squared-error fidelity term, a Lasso-type sparsity penalty with non-negative coefficient $\lambda_1 \geq 0$, and a Ridge-type shrinkage penalty with non-negative coefficient $\lambda_2 \geq 0$, all minimized over the box-constrained feasible set.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The generalization combining a least-squares fidelity term, L1 penalty, and L2 penalty subject to box constraints is a standard formulation in constrained penalized regression and convex optimization, and directly subsumes all previous problem variants as noted. No external citation is required for this problem-statement paragraph.]

$$\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w}) = \|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}\|_2^2 + \lambda_1 \|\mathbf{w}\|_1 + \lambda_2 \|\mathbf{w}\|_2^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad w_{\min} \leq w_k \leq w_{\max}, \quad k = 1, \dots, K.$$

Setting $\lambda_1 = \lambda_2 = 0$ and $w_{\min} = 0$, $w_{\max} = +\infty$ recovers the NNLS problem of SLOT 5. Setting $\lambda_1 = \lambda_2 = 0$ with finite $w_{\min}$ and $w_{\max}$ recovers the bounded-weight QP of SLOT 6. Selecting $\lambda_2 > 0$ with $\lambda_1 = 0$ produces a Ridge-regularized bounded LS problem whose solution smoothly penalizes large weights within the box. Selecting $\lambda_1 > 0$ introduces a soft sparsity incentive that drives weights toward their lower bound $w_{\min}$ while the box constraint prevents them from going below it, thereby producing a regularized sparse solution on $[w_{\min}, w_{\max}]$ [WEB:https://arxiv.org/pdf/1711.07582].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The special-case reductions (NNLS, bounded QP, Ridge-bounded, Lasso-bounded) are mathematically exact consequences of specific parameter choices in the general formulation. The cited CVXR paper (arXiv:1711.07582, Fu et al.) presents the disciplined convex programming framework that naturally handles these combined formulations.]

The combined problem is convex because $\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w})$ is a sum of three convex functions: the squared L2 norm $\|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}\|_2^2$ (quadratic with positive-semidefinite Hessian $2\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top \tilde{\mathbf{A}}$), the L1 norm $\lambda_1 \|\mathbf{w}\|_1$ (convex polyhedral), and the scaled squared L2 norm $\lambda_2 \|\mathbf{w}\|_2^2$ (strongly convex for $\lambda_2 > 0$). The constraint set $\{w_{\min} \leq w_k \leq w_{\max}\}$ is a closed convex box. Since the sum of convex functions is convex and the box is a convex set, the overall problem is a convex optimization problem: any local minimum is a global minimum, and standard interior-point or active-set solvers are guaranteed to converge to the global solution in polynomial time [WEB:https://arxiv.org/pdf/1711.07582].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The convexity argument (sum of convex functions, minimization over a convex constraint set) is rigorous convex analysis. The Hessian of the squared-error term equals $2\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top \tilde{\mathbf{A}}$, which is PSD, confirming convexity. The box is a convex polytope. The guarantee of global optimality for convex problems is a foundational result in optimization theory, consistent with the cited CVXR paper.]

The CVXR package implements disciplined convex programming (DCP) in R, allowing the optimization problem to be specified in syntax that closely mirrors the mathematical formulation, after which CVXR automatically verifies convexity using DCP composition rules, reformulates the problem as a standard conic program, and dispatches it to an appropriate solver such as CLARABEL, SCS, or OSQP [WEB:https://arxiv.org/pdf/1711.07582].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The description of CVXR's DCP verification, conic reformulation, and solver dispatch is accurate and consistent with the cited CVXR paper (arXiv:1711.07582). These are documented capabilities of the CVXR package available on CRAN.]

```r
K       <- ncol(A_tilde)
w       <- Variable(K)
lambda1 <- 0.01   # Lasso penalty weight  (set to 0 for pure bounded LS)
lambda2 <- 0.001  # Ridge penalty weight  (set to 0 for pure bounded LS)
w_min   <- 0.0    # lower bound (0 for NNLS)
w_max   <- 2.0    # upper bound

obj  <- Minimize(
          sum_squares(A_tilde %*% w - y) +
          lambda1 * norm1(w) +
          lambda2 * sum_squares(w)
        )
cons <- list(w >= w_min, w <= w_max)
prob <- Problem(obj, cons)
res  <- solve(prob)

w_opt <- as.vector(res$getValue(w))
cat("Optimal weights:", round(w_opt, 4), "\n")
cat("Objective value:", res$value, "\n")
```
