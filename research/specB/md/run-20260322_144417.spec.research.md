# Spectral Matching as Linear Regression: Methodologies, Formulations, and Computational Approaches

## Context

Traditional spectral matching methods modify recorded accelerograms to achieve conformity with target response spectra derived from probabilistic seismic hazard analysis (PSHA), but in doing so they alter the frequency content, duration characteristics, and number of sign reversals present in the original ground-motion records. Spectral matching in the time or frequency domain frequently distorts the non-stationary characteristics of ground motion and can alter scalar parameters such as Arias intensity. Phenomena including stiffness degradation and liquefaction depend on the number of load reversals, and the seismic response of systems that accumulate damage under cyclic loading depends not only on peak amplitude but also on duration and effective cycle count.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: This paragraph reproduces the context statement from the task specification verbatim, which is a well-established characterization of the trade-offs in spectral modification techniques; the claims about load reversals and Arias intensity are consistent with the broad earthquake-engineering literature.]

Given a candidate set of $m$ spectra $S_a(T_n)$ and a period mesh of $n$ points $\{T_1, \dots, T_n\}$, each spectrum $k = 1, \dots, K$ is defined by spectral ordinates $S_{a,k}(T_i)$ for $i = 1, \dots, n$. The PSHA-derived target spectrum is encoded as the vector $\mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ with components $y_i = \ln S_a^{\mathrm{obj}}(T_i)$. The objective is to find weights $w_k$ such that the weighted linear combination of the candidate spectra minimizes the discrepancy with respect to the target spectrum.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: Notation reproduced from the task specification context section; the log-normal encoding of the target spectrum is standard in GMPE-based hazard analysis.]

## SLOT 1: Summary of Spectral-Matching Record-Selection Methodologies

Ground-motion record selection for nonlinear dynamic analysis encompasses a spectrum of approaches whose common objective is to assemble a suite of accelerograms whose spectral content is compatible with a PSHA-derived target. The principal distinction in the literature separates methods that modify original waveforms from those that select unmodified or minimally scaled records on the basis of spectral compatibility.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The distinction between waveform-modification and record-selection approaches is well established in earthquake engineering literature and is confirmed by multiple sources [WEB:https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10518-022-01393-0][WEB:https://seismosoft.com/products/seismomatch/].]

Amplitude scaling is the simplest record-selection strategy: each candidate accelerogram is multiplied by a single constant factor chosen so that a representative spectral ordinate, or a geometric mean over a period range, approaches the target value. The ASCE 7 procedure requires that the average of the square-root-of-sum-of-squares (SRSS) spectra from all selected horizontal-component pairs does not fall below the corresponding ordinate of the design response spectrum over the period range $[0.2T, 1.5T]$ [WEB:https://www.structuremag.org/article/seismic-time-histories-a-practical-approach/]. While amplitude scaling preserves the original waveform intact, large scale factors can bias nonlinear structural response, a concern documented by Luco and Bazzurro (2007) [WEB:https://www.researchgate.net/publication/275600580_How_many_records_should_be_used_in_ASCESEI-7_ground_motion_scaling_procedure].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: Amplitude scaling and the ASCE 7 period-range requirement are well-documented code provisions; the concern about large scale factors biasing nonlinear response is reported in the literature cited.]

Waveform-modification techniques operate directly on the time series rather than applying a global scale factor. Time-domain spectral matching adds wavelet components to the original record iteratively until the computed response spectrum satisfies the target within prescribed tolerances; the methods of Abrahamson and Hancock (1992, 2006) and Atik and Abrahamson (2010) are widely implemented in tools such as SeismoMatch [WEB:https://seismosoft.com/products/seismomatch/]. Frequency-domain approaches iteratively adjust the Fourier amplitude spectrum of the record so that each Fourier ordinate is scaled by the ratio of target to computed spectral acceleration at the corresponding frequency. Both waveform-modification families alter the non-stationary energy envelope, cycle count, and Arias intensity of the original recording, which is the principal objection raised against their use when accumulated damage under cyclic loading is of concern [WEB:https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10518-022-01393-0].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: Wavelet-based and Fourier-domain spectral matching are standard techniques; the tools and authors cited (Abrahamson, Hancock, Atik) are widely referenced in the earthquake engineering community. The concern about Arias intensity modification is substantiated by the cited Springer source.]

Record selection by spectral compatibility, in contrast to waveform modification, selects recorded accelerograms from strong-motion databases without altering their waveform content, identifying subsets or weighted combinations of records whose collective spectral representation matches the target. Three principal variants are recognized. The first is conditional-spectrum-based selection, developed by Baker (2011), which defines the target as the conditional mean spectrum (CMS) rather than the uniform hazard spectrum, thereby avoiding the artificial simultaneous maximization of spectral ordinates at all periods that characterizes the UHS [WEB:https://ascelibrary.org/doi/abs/10.1061/%28ASCE%29ST.1943-541X.0000215]. The second is the Jayaram-Baker algorithm (2011), which generates simulated acceleration response spectra that match the mean, variance, and multivariate distribution of the target, then selects recorded ground motions whose spectra closely match each simulated spectrum [WEB:https://www.researchgate.net/publication/312930866_Conditional_spectrum-based_ground_motion_record_selection_using_average_spectral_acceleration]. The third and most directly relevant to the present formulation is linear-combination record selection: given $K$ candidate spectra $\{S_{a,k}(T_i)\}$ and a target $S_a^{\mathrm{obj}}(T_i)$, a weight vector $\mathbf{w} = (w_1, \dots, w_K)^\top$ is sought such that $\sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k S_{a,k}(T_i) \approx S_a^{\mathrm{obj}}(T_i)$ for all $i$ [WEB:https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/built-environment/articles/10.3389/fbuil.2019.00140/full]. This formulation does not modify the original records and preserves their non-stationary characteristics, while producing a composite target-compatible representation through the weighted superposition of response spectra.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: Baker's CMS paper (2011, ASCE JSE) and the Jayaram-Baker algorithm are canonical references in record selection literature; the linear-combination approach is also documented in the Frontiers built-environment source. No KB is available so all evidence is from web sources, which are authoritative peer-reviewed outlets.]

Stochastic search methods, including genetic algorithms (Naeim et al. 2004) and harmony search algorithms, extend record selection by optimizing simultaneously over both the choice of records from a database and the scale factors applied to each, minimizing objective functions based on median spectral shape and dispersion relative to the target [WEB:https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10518-022-01450-8][WEB:https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/13632469.2017.1342302]. These multi-objective formulations are computationally more demanding than direct linear regression but can account for second-order statistics (spectral variability across the suite) in addition to the mean spectral match.^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: Genetic algorithm and harmony-search approaches for record selection are documented in the cited Bulletin of Earthquake Engineering and Taylor & Francis sources. The claim about simultaneous optimization of scale factors and record choice is consistent with the abstracts available, though full text was not retrievable for detailed verification.]

## SLOT 2: Minimum Number of Records and Code-Based Requirements

The theoretical minimum number of candidate spectra required to achieve an exact spectral match through linear combination depends on the number of period points $n$ in the target mesh and the constraints imposed on the weight vector. In the unconstrained case, if $K = n$ records are available, the linear system $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{y}$ is square and a unique solution exists provided $\mathbf{A}$ is full rank, where $\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times K}$ is the design matrix of spectral ordinates. If $K > n$, the system is underdetermined and infinitely many solutions achieve zero residual; the least-squares formulation selects the minimum-norm solution. If $K < n$, the system is overdetermined and an exact match cannot generally be achieved; the least-squares weight vector then minimizes the RMSE over the period mesh. In practice, the minimum number of records necessary to achieve an adequate - not necessarily exact - spectral match depends on the spectral variability of the candidate set, the tolerance specified for spectral compatibility, and whether additional physical constraints (such as non-negativity or bounded weights) are imposed. A single well-chosen record with appropriate scaling can satisfy simple mean-spectrum compatibility criteria, but this provides no information about spectral variability and is statistically unreliable for structural response estimation.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The relationship between $K$, $n$, and the solvability of the linear system is elementary linear algebra and requires no external citation. The practical observation that a single record is insufficient for statistical reliability is consistent with the statistical studies cited below.]

The minimum-record thresholds embedded in modern seismic design codes originate from statistical and engineering-judgment studies on the variability of structural response estimates as a function of suite size. Early editions of ASCE/SEI 7 (through ASCE 7-05 and ASCE 7-10) required a minimum of three ground-motion pairs for nonlinear response history analysis, with an important conditional: if fewer than seven ground motions are analyzed, the design engineer must base the design on the maximum structural response among all records in the suite rather than the average, whereas if seven or more records are used, the average response is permitted [WEB:https://www.structuremag.org/article/seismic-time-histories-a-practical-approach/]. The seven-record threshold was introduced because statistical research demonstrated that the ASCE/SEI 7 scaling procedure is conservative and biased when fewer than seven ground motions are employed, and that seven or more randomly selected and appropriately scaled records provide more accurate and efficient estimates of the true structural response [WEB:https://pubs.usgs.gov/of/2011/1083/]. The study applied the ASCE/SEI 7 procedure to 480 different ground motion sets ranging from 3 to 10 records, comparing results against benchmark responses of single-degree-of-freedom systems, and found that the original code-prescribed limits had been based on engineering experience rather than rigorous statistical analysis [WEB:https://www.academia.edu/31967927/How_Many_Records_Should_Be_Used_in_an_ASCE_SEI_7_Ground_Motion_Scaling_Procedure].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The 7-record threshold and the average-vs-maximum rule are confirmed by the Structure Magazine article and the USGS Open-File Report (Reyes & Kalkan 2011). The 480-set statistical study is documented in the Academia.edu source referencing the same Reyes & Kalkan paper.]

ASCE/SEI 7-16 introduced a substantially revised Chapter 16 for nonlinear response history analysis, increasing the minimum suite size to eleven ground-motion pairs for buildings subject to site-specific maximum considered earthquake (MCER) hazard analysis [WEB:https://www.researchgate.net/publication/337886040_ASCE_7-16_Ground-motion_Scaling_Procedure_For_Nonlinear_Analysis_Of_Buildings_In_Comparison_With_ASCE_7-10_And_ASCE_7-05]. This requirement was retained in ASCE/SEI 7-22 [WEB:https://amplify.asce.org/content/standard/9780784415788/part/provisions/standard-chapter/s11]. The engineering rationale for the increase from seven to eleven records reflects the recognition that eleven records provide a statistically more robust basis for computing structural response statistics, particularly for the displacement-controlled limit states and collapse margin assessments that underlie the ASCE 7-16 Chapter 16 framework. With eleven records, the coefficient of variation of the estimated mean response is sufficiently small that no additional conservatism factor analogous to the max-over-suite rule is required for the mean of structural response metrics. The shift also aligns with the recommendations of NIST GCR 11-917-15 (Selecting and Scaling Earthquake Ground Motions), which advocated for larger suite sizes to improve the fidelity of nonlinear analysis outcomes [WEB:https://www.nehrp.gov/pdf/nistgcr11-917-15.pdf].^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The ASCE 7-16 Chapter 16 eleven-record requirement is confirmed by multiple sources (ResearchGate comparison paper, ASCE Amplify standard page). The statistical rationale connecting eleven records to improved coefficient of variation is consistent with the general statistical arguments in the literature but was not directly quantified in the retrieved web excerpts; hence confidence is MEDIUM rather than HIGH.]

## SLOT 3: Classical Linear Regression Formulation (RMSE Minimization)

The spectral-matching problem is cast as an ordinary least-squares (OLS) regression by assembling the candidate spectral ordinates into a design matrix and the target spectral ordinates into a response vector. Define the design matrix $\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times K}$ with entries^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The OLS formulation of spectral matching is a direct application of standard linear regression; the design matrix definition is notational and requires no external citation.]

$$A_{ik} = S_{a,k}(T_i), \quad i = 1, \dots, n, \quad k = 1, \dots, K,$$

where $S_{a,k}(T_i)$ is the pseudo-spectral acceleration of the $k$-th candidate record at period $T_i$. Define the target vector $\mathbf{y}^{\mathrm{lin}} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ with components $y_i^{\mathrm{lin}} = S_a^{\mathrm{obj}}(T_i)$, the spectral ordinate of the PSHA-derived target at period $T_i$ in physical units. The predicted spectral combination is $\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{w}$, and the residual vector is $\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}^{\mathrm{lin}}$.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The construction of the design matrix from spectral ordinates and the linear model $\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{w}$ are standard applications of ordinary least squares; no external citation is needed for the algebraic setup.]

The RMSE objective is^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: Definitional sentence introducing the RMSE formula; the equation follows directly from the residual vector definition and requires no external citation.]

$$\mathrm{RMSE}(\mathbf{w}) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_k S_{a,k}(T_i) - S_a^{\mathrm{obj}}(T_i)\right)^2} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}^{\mathrm{lin}}\|_2.$$

Since $n$ is a fixed positive constant, minimizing RMSE is equivalent to minimizing the residual sum of squares (RSS):^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The equivalence follows from $\mathrm{RMSE} = \sqrt{\mathrm{RSS}/n}$ and monotonicity of the square root; this is elementary algebra requiring no citation.]

$$\min_{\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^K} \|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}^{\mathrm{lin}}\|_2^2.$$

Expanding and differentiating with respect to $\mathbf{w}$ yields the normal equations^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The normal equations follow from setting $\nabla_\mathbf{w}\|\mathbf{Aw}-\mathbf{y}\|^2 = 2\mathbf{A}^\top(\mathbf{Aw}-\mathbf{y}) = \mathbf{0}$; this is a standard derivation in linear algebra.]

$$\mathbf{A}^\top \mathbf{A}\,\hat{\mathbf{w}} = \mathbf{A}^\top \mathbf{y}^{\mathrm{lin}}.$$

Provided $\mathbf{A}$ has full column rank (i.e., $\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{A}) = K$, which requires $n \geq K$ and the candidate spectra to be linearly independent), the unique closed-form least-squares solution is^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The invertibility of $\mathbf{A}^\top\mathbf{A}$ under full column rank is a standard result; the closed-form OLS estimator is textbook linear algebra requiring no external citation.]

$$\hat{\mathbf{w}} = \left(\mathbf{A}^\top \mathbf{A}\right)^{-1} \mathbf{A}^\top \mathbf{y}^{\mathrm{lin}}.$$

When $K > n$ (more records than period points) the normal equation matrix $\mathbf{A}^\top\mathbf{A}$ is singular; in that case the minimum-norm solution $\hat{\mathbf{w}} = \mathbf{A}^\top(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^\top)^{-1}\mathbf{y}^{\mathrm{lin}}$ is obtained via the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse. The closed-form OLS solution does not impose any constraint on the sign or magnitude of the components of $\hat{\mathbf{w}}$; negative weights, which imply subtraction of a scaled accelerogram from the combination, can appear and are physically meaningless for real ground-motion records [WEB:https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-negative_least_squares].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The normal equations, the closed-form OLS solution, and the conditions for its existence (full column rank) are textbook linear algebra. The observation that unconstrained OLS permits negative weights is a fundamental property of the unconstrained problem, confirmed by standard optimization and NNLS literature.]

## SLOT 4: Log-Log Space Reformulation

Pseudo-spectral acceleration values span several orders of magnitude across the period range of engineering interest, and the scatter of recorded spectra about a median curve is well described by a log-normal distribution - the distributional model underlying all modern ground-motion prediction equations. Representing both spectral ordinates and periods on logarithmic scales produces a space in which spectral curves are approximately linear (log-log linearity), more smoothly varying, and more homoscedastic. Regression in this transformed space therefore yields better-conditioned normal equations and residuals that are more nearly homogeneous across the period axis.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The log-normality of spectral ordinates and the smoothing effect of log-log representation are foundational principles of seismological statistics, consistent with GMPE literature and confirmed qualitatively by [WEB:https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10518-022-01393-0][WEB:https://blog.prosig.com/2002/07/15/smoothing-spectral-data/].]

The log-log reformulation replaces each spectral ordinate and each period value by its natural logarithm. Define the transformed design matrix $\tilde{\mathbf{A}} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times K}$ with entries^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The variable substitution defining $\tilde{\mathbf{A}}$ is a notational definition; the log transformation of spectral ordinates follows from the distributional model for spectral accelerations.]

$$\tilde{A}_{ik} = \ln S_{a,k}(T_i), \quad i = 1, \dots, n, \quad k = 1, \dots, K,$$

and the transformed target vector $\tilde{\mathbf{y}} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ with components^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The transformed target vector is a notational definition; its components equal the log-space target already established in the Context section.]

$$\tilde{y}_i = \ln S_a^{\mathrm{obj}}(T_i) = y_i,$$

which is identical to the log-space target $\mathbf{y}$ defined in the Context section. The transformed regression model is $\tilde{\mathbf{y}} \approx \tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w}$, and the objective function becomes^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The equivalence $\tilde{y}_i = y_i$ follows directly from the Context section definition; the regression model formulation is a standard linear algebra statement requiring no external citation.]

$$\min_{\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^K}\|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w} - \tilde{\mathbf{y}}\|_2^2 = \min_{\mathbf{w}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_k \ln S_{a,k}(T_i) - \ln S_a^{\mathrm{obj}}(T_i)\right)^2.$$

The closed-form solution retains the same algebraic structure as in SLOT 3 but is expressed in terms of the log-transformed matrices:^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The log-log OLS estimator is structurally identical to the linear-space estimator of SLOT 3 with $\tilde{\mathbf{A}}$ substituted for $\mathbf{A}$ and $\tilde{\mathbf{y}}$ for $\mathbf{y}^{\mathrm{lin}}$; the derivation follows the same normal-equations argument.]

$$\hat{\mathbf{w}} = \left(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top \tilde{\mathbf{A}}\right)^{-1} \tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top \tilde{\mathbf{y}}.$$

The period mesh $\{T_1, \dots, T_n\}$ is typically chosen to be uniformly spaced on the log scale (i.e., $\ln T_i$ are equally spaced), which ensures that each decade of the period axis contributes equally to the objective function. This choice further motivates the log-log representation: equal spacing in $\ln T$ combined with a log-space spectral target yields a regression problem that is geometrically uniform across the broad period range $[0.01 \mathrm{s}, 10 \mathrm{s}]$ commonly adopted in engineering practice. The weight vector $\mathbf{w}$ has the same dimension and interpretation in both the linear and log-log formulations; however, the residuals now measure discrepancy in log-spectral-acceleration units, so a unit residual corresponds approximately to a factor-of-$e$ error in spectral acceleration, consistent with the fractional error metrics used in GMPE validation [WEB:https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10518-022-01393-0].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The transformation to log-log space and the resulting OLS structure follow directly from substitution of variables. The choice of log-uniform period meshes is standard in spectral engineering. The interpretation of log-space residuals is consistent with seismological practice but is stated as an approximation; confidence is HIGH because the mathematical derivation is rigorous.]

## SLOT 5: Non-Negative Weight Constraint and R Implementation

The unconstrained OLS solution $\hat{\mathbf{w}} = (\mathbf{A}^\top\mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{A}^\top\mathbf{y}$ admits negative components whenever two or more candidate spectra are highly correlated. A negative weight $w_k < 0$ implies subtraction of the $k$-th spectral contribution from the combination, which corresponds to adding an accelerogram with negated amplitude - a construct with no physical meaning in the context of seismic hazard. Physical plausibility therefore requires that all weights satisfy $w_k \geq 0$ for $k = 1, \dots, K$ [WEB:https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-negative_least_squares].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The physical argument against negative weights in the ground-motion context is straightforward and consistent with NNLS literature. The Wikipedia NNLS article confirms the constraint formulation.]

Incorporating this constraint yields the non-negative least-squares (NNLS) problem:^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The NNLS formulation is a direct extension of the OLS objective with the addition of the non-negativity constraint; the problem statement is definitional and follows from the physical argument in the preceding paragraph.]

$$\min_{\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^K} \|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}\|_2^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad w_k \geq 0, \quad k = 1, \dots, K.$$

The NNLS problem is a strictly convex quadratic program with a half-space feasible region and a unique global minimizer. The Lawson-Hanson active-set algorithm solves NNLS exactly in a finite number of steps by maintaining a partition of the indices $\{1, \dots, K\}$ into an active set (indices at which $w_k = 0$) and a passive set (indices that are unconstrained), iteratively moving indices between the two sets until the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker optimality conditions are satisfied [WEB:https://cran.r-project.org/package=nnls].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The Lawson-Hanson algorithm for NNLS is the canonical algorithm for this problem and is implemented in the R nnls package; the KKT conditions characterization of NNLS optimality is standard convex optimization theory.]

Ridge regression, defined by the penalized objective $\min_\mathbf{w} \|\mathbf{Aw}-\mathbf{y}\|_2^2 + \lambda\|\mathbf{w}\|_2^2$, yields the closed-form solution $\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{\mathrm{Ridge}} = (\mathbf{A}^\top\mathbf{A} + \lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{A}^\top\mathbf{y}$. This solution is the result of an unconstrained differentiable optimization problem and imposes no bound on the sign of any component of $\mathbf{w}$; adding an $\ell_2$ penalty shrinks the coefficient magnitudes toward zero but cannot enforce $w_k \geq 0$ because the Euclidean ball $\{\mathbf{w} : \|\mathbf{w}\|_2 \leq c\}$ is symmetric about the origin and does not exclude the negative orthant. Lasso regression, which replaces the $\ell_2$ penalty with $\lambda\|\mathbf{w}\|_1$, can drive individual coefficients to exactly zero through the geometry of the $\ell_1$ ball, but it too does not prevent negative coefficients [WEB:https://www.stat.berkeley.edu/~stark/Preprints/bvls.pdf]. Consequently, neither Ridge nor Lasso regularization can serve as a direct substitute for the NNLS constraint; a dedicated inequality-constrained solver is required.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The inability of Ridge and Lasso to enforce non-negativity follows from their mathematical formulations as unconstrained (penalized) rather than constrained problems. This is consistent with regularized least squares literature [WEB:https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regularized_least_squares][WEB:https://www.stat.berkeley.edu/~stark/Preprints/bvls.pdf].]

The following R code solves the NNLS problem for spectral matching using the `nnls` package (Lawson-Hanson implementation) [WEB:https://cran.r-project.org/package=nnls]:^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The nnls package is a well-documented CRAN package implementing the Lawson-Hanson algorithm; the lead-in accurately describes the function call below.]

```r
# Non-negative least squares for spectral matching
# A : design matrix (n x K), rows = period points, cols = candidate spectra
# y : target spectral ordinates (length n)

library(nnls)

# Solve: minimize ||A %*% w - y||^2 subject to w >= 0
fit_nnls <- nnls(A = A, b = y)
w_hat    <- fit_nnls$x          # non-negative weight vector (length K)
residuals <- fit_nnls$residuals # n-vector of residuals A %*% w_hat - y
rmse     <- sqrt(mean(residuals^2))
```

^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The nnls() function signature and its correspondence to the NNLS problem are documented in the CRAN package page [WEB:https://cran.r-project.org/package=nnls] and confirmed by the R-Bloggers article [WEB:https://www.r-bloggers.com/2019/11/non-negative-least-squares/]. The code is straightforward and follows the package documentation.]

An alternative implementation using the `glmnet` package enforces non-negativity by setting `lower.limits = 0` with `lambda = 0` (no regularization penalty), which recovers the NNLS solution:^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The glmnet lower.limits = 0 approach for NNLS is documented in [WEB:https://www.r-bloggers.com/2019/11/non-negative-least-squares/]; the lead-in accurately describes the code block below.]

```r
library(glmnet)

fit_glmnet <- glmnet(x = A, y = y, lambda = 0,
                     lower.limits = 0, intercept = FALSE)
w_hat_glmnet <- as.numeric(coef(fit_glmnet))[-1]  # drop intercept slot
```

^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The glmnet approach to NNLS via lower.limits = 0 is documented in [WEB:https://www.r-bloggers.com/2019/11/non-negative-least-squares/] and is a known capability of the glmnet package.]

## SLOT 6: Bounded Weights with Ridge/Lasso Feasibility

In practical ground-motion suites the spectral contribution of any single record should remain within physically and statistically defensible bounds. A weight near zero implies that the corresponding record contributes negligibly to the combination and might as well be excluded; a very large weight concentrates the entire spectral representation on a single record and defeats the purpose of assembling a diverse suite. Box constraints of the form^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The physical motivation for bounding individual weights is engineering judgment consistent with spectral matching practice; no specific quantitative claim is made here that requires external verification.]

$$w_{\min} \leq w_k \leq w_{\max}, \quad k = 1, \dots, K,$$

where $w_{\min} \geq 0$ and $w_{\max} < \infty$, formalize these requirements. Substituting $w_{\min} = 0$ recovers the NNLS constraint from SLOT 5.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The motivation for box constraints on spectral matching weights is physically grounded and consistent with engineering practice; the mathematical connection to NNLS via $w_{\min} = 0$ follows directly from the constraint definition.]

Ridge regression is fundamentally incompatible with box constraints. The Ridge penalty $\lambda\|\mathbf{w}\|_2^2$ modifies the curvature of the objective function but does not alter its domain; the unconstrained minimizer $(\mathbf{A}^\top\mathbf{A}+\lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{A}^\top\mathbf{y}$ can lie anywhere in $\mathbb{R}^K$ and may violate either the lower or the upper bound. Lasso regression introduces sparsity through the $\ell_1$ penalty, which tends to concentrate solutions on the faces and vertices of the $\ell_1$ ball, but the constraint boundary of the Lasso is $\|\mathbf{w}\|_1 \leq t$ - a symmetric convex set that neither enforces non-negativity nor imposes an upper bound on any individual coefficient. A constrained Lasso formulation exists (the "constrained Lasso" of James, Paulson, and Rusmevichientong), but it requires explicit constraint matrices beyond those of standard Lasso and is solved by dedicated algorithms rather than the standard coordinate-descent Lasso [WEB:https://arxiv.org/pdf/1611.01511]. In summary, neither standard Ridge nor standard Lasso regression can enforce box constraints $w_{\min} \leq w_k \leq w_{\max}$ without reformulation as a constrained optimization problem.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The mathematical argument that Ridge and Lasso cannot enforce box constraints follows from the definitions of their respective penalty and feasible-region structures. The constrained Lasso reference [WEB:https://arxiv.org/pdf/1611.01511] confirms that enforcing arbitrary linear constraints on Lasso requires dedicated algorithms.]

The bounded-variable least-squares (BVLS) formulation places the problem in the framework of quadratic programming with simple bound constraints:^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: BVLS is a standard QP formulation with explicit lower and upper bounds on each decision variable; this is a definitional classification statement.]

$$\min_{\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^K} \|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}\|_2^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad w_{\min} \leq w_k \leq w_{\max}, \quad k = 1, \dots, K.$$

The Stark-Parker algorithm, implemented in the R `bvls` package, solves this problem using an active-set strategy that handles both the lower and upper bounds simultaneously [WEB:https://www.rdocumentation.org/packages/bvls/versions/1.4/topics/bvls][WEB:https://www.stat.berkeley.edu/~stark/Preprints/bvls.pdf]. The following R code demonstrates the bounded-weight formulation:^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The Stark-Parker BVLS algorithm and its R implementation in the bvls package are well documented. The problem formulation as QP with simple bounds is mathematically standard.]

```r
# Bounded-variable least squares for spectral matching
# A       : design matrix (n x K)
# y       : target vector (length n)
# w_min   : lower bound on each weight (scalar or vector of length K)
# w_max   : upper bound on each weight (scalar or vector of length K)

library(bvls)

K <- ncol(A)
bl <- rep(w_min, K)   # lower-bound vector
bu <- rep(w_max, K)   # upper-bound vector

fit_bvls <- bvls(A = A, b = y, bl = bl, bu = bu)
w_hat    <- fit_bvls$x          # bounded weight vector
rmse     <- sqrt(mean(fit_bvls$residuals^2))
```

## SLOT 7: Convex Optimization Generalization with R Libraries

The spectral-matching problem as formulated in SLOTS 3-6 belongs to the class of convex quadratic programs (QPs) because its objective is a convex quadratic function of $\mathbf{w}$ and all constraints are linear inequalities. This structure permits a unified loss function that subsumes both the non-negativity requirement of SLOT 5 and the box-constraint requirement of SLOT 6, and that can accommodate additional convex penalty terms such as Ridge or Lasso regularization while preserving the convexity of the problem.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: A convex quadratic objective with linear constraints is a QP by definition; this classification is standard in convex optimization. The ability to add convex penalties (Ridge, Lasso) while preserving convexity follows from closure properties of convex functions.]

The generalized loss function for spectral matching is^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: This sentence introduces the unified loss function combining residual, Ridge, and Lasso terms; it is a definitional statement and the equation follows from standard regularized regression notation.]

$$\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w}) = \|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}\|_2^2 + \lambda_2 \|\mathbf{w}\|_2^2 + \lambda_1 \|\mathbf{w}\|_1,$$

subject to^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The box constraint is carried over from SLOT 6; this lead-in sentence bridges the loss function definition to its feasible region.]

$$w_{\min} \leq w_k \leq w_{\max}, \quad k = 1, \dots, K.$$

Setting $\lambda_1 = \lambda_2 = 0$ with $w_{\min} = 0$, $w_{\max} = \infty$ recovers the NNLS problem. Setting $\lambda_1 = \lambda_2 = 0$ with finite $w_{\min}$ and $w_{\max}$ recovers the BVLS problem. Setting $\lambda_2 > 0$, $\lambda_1 = 0$ with box constraints produces a Ridge-regularized bounded-weight formulation (a strictly convex QP). Setting $\lambda_1 > 0$, $\lambda_2 = 0$ with box constraints yields a Lasso-regularized bounded formulation (a non-smooth but still convex QP solvable by interior-point or ADMM methods). All these variants satisfy the DCP (Disciplined Convex Programming) rules and are therefore solvable by the CVXR framework [WEB:https://arxiv.org/pdf/1711.07582].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The decomposition of the generalized loss into special cases and the convexity of each term are elementary; DCP compliance of squared-norm, $\ell_1$-norm, and linear inequality constraints is standard in convex programming. The CVXR paper [WEB:https://arxiv.org/pdf/1711.07582] confirms DCP coverage of these problem types.]

The following R code formulates and solves the generalized spectral-matching problem using CVXR [WEB:https://cvxr.rbind.io/]:^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: CVXR is a well-documented DCP-based R package; the lead-in accurately describes the code block that follows, which implements the generalized loss function with box constraints.]

```r
# Generalized spectral matching via CVXR
# Subsumes NNLS (w_min=0, w_max=Inf, lambda1=lambda2=0)
# and BVLS (finite w_min, w_max, lambda1=lambda2=0)

library(CVXR)

K      <- ncol(A)
w      <- Variable(K)                 # decision variable: weight vector
lambda1 <- 0                          # Lasso penalty (set > 0 for sparsity)
lambda2 <- 0                          # Ridge penalty (set > 0 for shrinkage)

# Generalized loss function
objective <- Minimize(
  sum_squares(A %*% w - y) +
  lambda2 * sum_squares(w) +
  lambda1 * norm1(w)
)

# Box constraints (non-negativity: w_min = 0; set w_max = Inf to omit upper bound)
w_min <- 0
w_max <- Inf   # replace with a finite value to enforce upper bound

constraints <- list(w >= w_min)
if (is.finite(w_max)) {
  constraints <- c(constraints, list(w <= w_max))
}

problem <- Problem(objective, constraints)
result  <- psolve(problem, solver = "CLARABEL")
w_hat   <- value(w)
```

^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The CVXR syntax and problem-construction approach shown are consistent with the official CVXR documentation and the introductory vignette retrieved from [WEB:https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/CVXR/vignettes/cvxr_intro.html]. The CLARABEL solver is the default recommended solver in current CVXR versions.]

An equivalent formulation using the `quadprog` package is available for the pure QP case ($\lambda_1 = 0$, finite $w_{\min}$ and $w_{\max}$). The `quadprog::solve.QP` function minimizes $\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{D}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{d}^\top\mathbf{w}$ subject to $\mathbf{C}^\top\mathbf{w} \geq \mathbf{b}_0$ [WEB:https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/quadprog/index.html]. Expanding the BVLS objective,^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The quadprog canonical form and the solve.QP function signature are documented at [WEB:https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/quadprog/index.html] and [WEB:https://henrywang.nl/quadratic-programming-with-r/]; the lead-in is consistent with these sources.]

$$\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}\|_2^2 = \mathbf{w}^\top(\mathbf{A}^\top\mathbf{A})\mathbf{w} - 2(\mathbf{A}^\top\mathbf{y})^\top\mathbf{w} + \|\mathbf{y}\|_2^2,$$

so $\mathbf{D} = 2\mathbf{A}^\top\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{d} = 2\mathbf{A}^\top\mathbf{y}$.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The algebraic expansion of the BVLS objective into the quadprog canonical form $\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{D}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{d}^\top\mathbf{w}$ is elementary; the quadprog function signature is documented at [WEB:https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/quadprog/index.html] and confirmed by [WEB:https://henrywang.nl/quadratic-programming-with-r/].]

```r
# Quadprog formulation of the BVLS spectral-matching problem
# lambda2 > 0 supported by adding lambda2 * I to Dmat

library(quadprog)

K      <- ncol(A)
Dmat   <- 2 * t(A) %*% A                    # K x K positive semi-definite matrix
dvec   <- 2 * t(A) %*% y                    # K-vector

# Constraint matrix: lower bounds w >= w_min and upper bounds w <= w_max
# Reformulate w <= w_max as -w >= -w_max for the >= convention of solve.QP
Amat   <- cbind(diag(K), -diag(K))          # 2K constraints
bvec   <- c(rep(w_min, K), rep(-w_max, K))  # right-hand side

result <- solve.QP(Dmat = Dmat + 1e-8 * diag(K),  # small ridge for numerics
                   dvec = dvec,
                   Amat = Amat,
                   bvec = bvec)
w_hat  <- result$solution
```

^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The quadprog solve.QP code is consistent with the documented function signature [WEB:https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/quadprog/index.html][WEB:https://henrywang.nl/quadratic-programming-with-r/]. The small ridge regularization added for numerical stability is standard practice when the design matrix is nearly rank-deficient.]

The CVXR formulation is preferred when $\lambda_1 > 0$ (Lasso penalty) is required, because the $\ell_1$ term renders the objective non-differentiable and `quadprog` no longer applies. CVXR's disciplined convex programming layer automatically selects an appropriate solver (CLARABEL, SCS, or ECOS) depending on the problem structure, making it the most general computational tool for the spectral-matching optimization among standard R libraries [WEB:https://arxiv.org/pdf/1711.07582][WEB:https://cvxr.rbind.io/].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The DCP framework in CVXR and its ability to handle non-differentiable convex objectives such as the $\ell_1$ norm are core features documented in the CVXR paper [WEB:https://arxiv.org/pdf/1711.07582] and the package website [WEB:https://cvxr.rbind.io/].]
