# Pore Pressure Increment Proxy Model for Saturated Fine-Grained Soils Under Seismic Loading

---

## SLOT 1: Numerical Strategies for Estimating Maximum Shear Strain During a Seismic Event

In a one-dimensional site-response framework, the instantaneous shear strain history $\gamma(z,t)$ is the primary mechanical variable driving pore pressure generation in a saturated fine-grained stratum. For a stratum of thickness $H_s$ with power-law shear modulus $G(\eta) = G_o(\eta/H_s)^m$, where $\eta = H_s - z$ denotes depth from the free surface and $G_o = \rho c_o^2$ is the base shear modulus, the exact model-independent relationship between the acceleration field and the shear strain is obtained by integrating the equation of motion vertically from the zero-stress free surface [@Ref001][@RovithisEtAl2011]:

$$\gamma(\eta,t) = \frac{\rho}{G(\eta)} \int_0^{\eta} a(\eta',t)\,d\eta'$$

The shear stress $\tau = G\gamma$ at any level equals the total inertial force of the overlying soil column and is independent of the specific form of $G(z)$, which is an exact consequence of the equation of motion [@Ref001][@RovithisEtAl2011]. The acceleration field $a(\eta',t)$ is itself obtained by multiplying the base acceleration spectrum $A_g(\omega)$ by the closed-form transfer function $H_a(z,\omega)$ appropriate to the stiffness profile -- trigonometric for the uniform stratum and Bessel-function-based for the general power-law family -- and recovering the time domain via the inverse discrete Fourier transform [@Ref002][@Ref001][@Ref001][@Gazetas1982].

Once the full instantaneous strain history $\gamma(z,t)$ is available at any depth, a representative scalar amplitude must be extracted to drive a pore pressure accumulation model. The max-max strategy defines this representative amplitude as the absolute peak of the instantaneous history over the entire event duration:

$$\gamma_{max}(z) = \max_{t \in [0,T]} |\gamma(z,t)|$$

This approach is conceptually direct and requires no further processing of the time series beyond identifying its maximum absolute value [@Ref003]. Its primary advantage is that it quantifies the single largest transient demand imposed on the soil, providing a well-defined and conservative scalar for deterministic design. The principal limitation is that an isolated large-amplitude spike may be unrepresentative of the cumulative loading imposed by a long, multi-cycle motion; for records with a pronounced impulsive character, the max-max estimate can overstate the effective strain amplitude relative to the energy content of the bulk of the cycles [@Ref003][@Ref004].

The root-mean-square (RMS) strategy defines the representative strain amplitude in terms of the energy content of the full history:

$$\gamma_{RMS}(z) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{T}\int_0^T \gamma^2(z,t)\,dt}$$

This quantity is proportional to the specific energy density of the strain time series and provides a measure of average cyclic intensity over the entire event rather than a single extreme value [@Ref005]. The RMS strain is directly related to the energy dissipated per unit volume per cycle in a hysteretically damped material and therefore reflects the cumulative fatigue loading more accurately than the single-peak maximum for records with many cycles of moderate amplitude. A long-duration Mw 8 motion with 20 cycles of moderate strain accumulates pore pressures quite differently from an Mw 6 pulse with a single large transient, even if the peak strain is comparable; the RMS metric captures this distinction [@Ref005][@Ref006]. The main disadvantage is sensitivity to record windowing: including low-amplitude pre- and post-event coda reduces the RMS without affecting the actual damage potential, and the result depends on the record duration selected. A frequency-content-weighted variant of the strain ratio, applied to 110 ground motions at 15 KiK-net stations, produced approximately 54% reduction in peak horizontal acceleration prediction error relative to the fixed 0.65 peak-fraction convention [@Ref005].

The equivalent uniform strain approach, introduced in the SHAKE program by Schnabel et al. (1972), defines the representative cyclic strain as a fixed fraction of the maximum strain:

$$\gamma_{eff}(z) = 0.65\,\gamma_{max}(z)$$

This fraction was adopted as an empirical approximation of the amplitude of a uniform sinusoidal history that produces the same pore pressure buildup as the actual irregular record for a motion representative of the western United States seismic environment [@Ref007][@SchnabelEtAl1972]. The advantage of this convention is simplicity: it requires only the peak strain and a single scalar multiplier, making it directly compatible with the iterative equivalent linear procedure implemented in SHAKE-family codes and in pyStrata [@Ref008]. The limitation is that the 0.65 ratio is fixed and physically unjustified for arbitrary records: the appropriate fraction depends on the frequency content, the number of cycles, and the irregularity coefficient of the specific motion, and systematic errors of both signs arise when the ratio is applied universally [@Ref005][@Ref004].

Beyond these three scalar reduction strategies, two additional approaches are recognized in earthquake geotechnical engineering practice. The Dobry et al. (1982) cyclic strain method identifies a threshold shear strain $\gamma_{th}$ -- approximately 0.01% for clean sands -- below which excess pore pressures do not develop regardless of the number of applied cycles; only strain excursions above $\gamma_{th}$ contribute to pore pressure generation, so the threshold-based approach naturally filters out small-amplitude portions of the record that carry no damage potential [@Ref009]. A cycle-counting approach based on rainflow or peak-valley enumeration of the strain time series extracts the number and amplitude distribution of significant half-cycles from the irregular record, which can then be used as input to pore pressure accumulation models in a cycle-by-cycle manner without reducing the history to a single scalar; this approach preserves the distributional information about cycle amplitudes and is the most physically rigorous entry-level method short of full time-stepping constitutive analysis [@Ref006]. Nonlinear effective-stress time-domain analyses apply the constitutive pore pressure model at every time step directly to the instantaneous strain increment, eliminating the need for any a priori scalar reduction and preserving the full path dependence of pore pressure generation and dissipation [@Ref010].

## SLOT 2: Empirical Methods for Pore Pressure Increment from PGA and Earthquake Magnitude

Empirical methods for estimating pore pressure increment from PGA and earthquake magnitude provide a first-level screening capability in geotechnical earthquake engineering practice. These methods bypass detailed constitutive modeling in favor of simplified correlations calibrated against case history databases, laboratory cyclic test results, and analytical models. The central chain of inference is: PGA and Mw determine the cyclic stress ratio (CSR) applied to the soil; the CSR, compared with the cyclic resistance ratio (CRR) of the material at the in situ state, determines whether liquefaction triggering occurs and, for pre-triggering conditions, the degree of excess pore pressure accumulation [@Ref011][@Ref012].

Seed, Martin, and Lysmer (1976) established one of the earliest analytical frameworks for tracking pore pressure buildup and dissipation under cyclic earthquake loading [@Ref013]. Their formulation expresses the excess pore pressure ratio $r_u = \Delta u / \sigma'_{v0}$ as a continuous function of the cycle ratio $N/N_L$, where $N$ is the number of applied uniform cyclic stress cycles and $N_L$ is the number of cycles required to trigger liquefaction at the same CSR. The functional form is:

$$r_u = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{\pi}\arcsin\!\left[2\!\left(\frac{N}{N_L}\right)^{1/\alpha} - 1\right]$$

The shape parameter $\alpha \approx 0.7$ was calibrated against undrained cyclic triaxial and simple shear test data on clean sands [@Ref013]. Earthquake magnitude enters through $N_L$, which depends on the CSR, and through the number of equivalent uniform cycles $N_{eq}$, which scales with Mw through empirical correlations. The advantage of this model is that it provides a continuous pore pressure trajectory rather than a binary trigger criterion; its main limitation is that specification of $N_L$ at each depth requires a CRR estimate from in situ testing and calibrated liquefaction resistance correlations [@Ref011].

Dobry et al. (1982) proposed a cyclic-strain-based approach in which the driving variable for pore pressure generation is the cyclic shear strain amplitude $\gamma_c$ rather than the cyclic stress ratio, thereby decoupling the pore pressure model from in situ resistance parameters [@Ref009]. Below a threshold shear strain $\gamma_{th} \approx 0.01\%$ for clean sands, excess pore pressures do not develop regardless of the number of cycles applied; above $\gamma_{th}$, the excess pore pressure ratio after $N$ uniform cycles follows a power-law relationship in $\gamma_c - \gamma_{th}$ [@Ref009]. The connection to PGA is indirect: PGA and Mw determine the induced cyclic stress, which, divided by the initial shear modulus, yields the cyclic strain; the pore pressure response is then evaluated against the threshold and the strain-based accumulation model. The advantage of this formulation is that it does not require separate liquefaction resistance characterization; threshold strain and power-law parameters can be obtained from standard cyclic laboratory tests. Its limitation in the proxy model context is that PGA alone is an imprecise predictor of $\gamma_c$ at depth, because site amplification, depth-reduction factors, and frequency content all modulate the actual strain independently of PGA [@Ref009].

Tokimatsu and Seed (1987) developed a simplified procedure linking CSR, normalized SPT blow count, and earthquake magnitude to volumetric strain and implicitly to the degree of pore pressure generation [@Ref014]. A key contribution of this framework is the explicit quantification of the number of equivalent uniform cycles $N_{eq}$ as a function of Mw: approximately 2-3 cycles for Mw 5.5 events, increasing to approximately 15-26 cycles for Mw 8 earthquakes. This magnitude-cycle scaling is the primary mechanism through which Mw enters the pore pressure estimation within stress-based simplified procedures. The CSR is expressed as:

$$\mathrm{CSR} = 0.65\,\frac{a_{max}}{g}\,\frac{\sigma_{v0}}{\sigma'_{v0}}\,r_d$$

where $a_{max}$ is the PGA, $\sigma_{v0}$ and $\sigma'_{v0}$ are the total and effective vertical stresses, and $r_d$ is the depth-reduction factor [@Ref014][@Ref011]. The advantage of magnitude scaling through $N_{eq}$ is that it captures the duration dependence of pore pressure accumulation: longer, larger-magnitude events impose more loading cycles at comparable PGA, resulting in higher $r_u$ values for otherwise identical site and soil conditions [@Ref011].

The Idriss and Boulanger (2008) framework represents the current state of North American engineering practice for simplified liquefaction triggering and pore pressure evaluation [@Ref011]. The approach defines CSR (demand) as a function of PGA, total and effective overburden stresses, and the depth-reduction factor $r_d$, and CRR (capacity) as a function of normalized in situ index parameters (SPT blow count or CPT tip resistance), effective overburden stress, and fines content. The magnitude scaling factor:

$$MSF = \frac{10^{2.24}}{M_w^{2.56}}$$

adjusts CRR from the reference magnitude 7.5 to the actual event magnitude, so that Mw enters the triggering assessment as a multiplicative correction to the resistance [@Ref011]. The factor of safety $FS = \mathrm{CRR} \cdot MSF / \mathrm{CSR}$ provides a continuous measure of the margin against triggering; $FS < 1$ implies $r_u = 1$, while $FS > 1$ indicates partial pore pressure generation that can be quantified through the Seed-Martin-Lysmer cycle ratio model or the Dobry strain-based model [@Ref011][@Ref012].

NCHRP Research Report 1092 (2024) provides the most recent authoritative synthesis of empirical pore pressure methods, establishing guidelines for seismic site response analysis with pore water pressure generation [@Ref012]. The report classifies methods into three tiers: Tier 1 comprises simplified empirical correlations linking PGA and Mw directly to $r_u$ through the CSR-CRR framework; Tier 2 comprises semi-empirical constitutive models requiring laboratory-derived pore pressure generation parameters; and Tier 3 comprises advanced plasticity models such as PM4Sand that resolve pore pressure generation and dissipation at each time step. For the specific objective of a proxy model from PGA and Mw, Tier 1 methods are the appropriate entry point, with the Idriss-Boulanger (2008) triggering procedure as the current Tier 1 state of practice supplemented by Tokimatsu-Seed magnitude scaling for duration effects [@Ref012][@Ref011].

## SLOT 3: Numerical Implementation Strategies for Pore Pressure Estimation from Instantaneous Shear Strain

Given a base acceleration history $a_g(t)$ and a one-dimensional stratum model, two broadly distinct implementation strategies permit estimation of excess pore pressure at a point within a saturated fine-grained stratum. The first is a two-stage approach that computes the full instantaneous shear strain history analytically and then applies a scalar or cycle-based pore pressure model to the resulting strain field. The second is a direct time-stepping approach that advances the pore pressure state at each time step as the coupled stress-strain-pore-pressure system evolves. Both strategies require the instantaneous shear strain $\gamma(z,t)$ as the primary driving variable; the analytical closed-form route for computing this strain is documented in the KB through frequency-domain transfer functions and their R implementation [@Ref002][@Ref001][@Ref001][@RovithisEtAl2011].

The closed-form analytical route proceeds through two computational stages as implemented in the R algorithm of the KB [@Ref002][@Ref001]. In the first stage, `computeAccelField` computes the depth-varying acceleration matrix $a(\eta_k, t_i)$ by multiplying the base acceleration spectrum $A_g(\omega)$ by the appropriate transfer function $H_a(z,\omega)$ at each depth and frequency, and recovering the time domain via the inverse DFT. In the second stage, `computeStrainHistory` integrates the acceleration field vertically by cumulative trapezoidal quadrature to form the integral $\mathcal{I}(\eta_k,t_i) = \int_0^{\eta_k} a(\eta',t_i)\,d\eta'$, then divides by $G(\eta)/\rho$ to produce the instantaneous shear strain at each requested depth [@Ref002][@Ref001]. The approach is exact within the linear viscoelastic framework; its domain of validity is limited to strains below approximately 0.1% for fine-grained soils where modulus degradation and strain-path dependence become significant [@Ref001][@SchnabelEtAl1972]. Once the full history $\gamma(z,t)$ is available, pore pressure is estimated by applying a scalar model -- such as the Dobry et al. (1982) strain-based relationship or the Seed-Martin-Lysmer cycle ratio model -- to the representative strain amplitude extracted from the time series [@Ref009][@Ref013].

An intermediate strategy couples the equivalent linear site response procedure with a simplified pore pressure generation model. The frequency-domain transfer functions of the KB [@Ref001][@Gazetas1982] are used iteratively: at each iteration, the effective shear strain (typically $0.65\gamma_{max}$ per the SHAKE convention) updates the strain-compatible shear modulus and damping ratio from modulus reduction and damping curves; after convergence, the final strain profile is passed to a pore pressure accumulation model [@Ref010]. This strategy preserves the computational simplicity of the frequency-domain approach while approximately accounting for strain-dependent soil behavior. Its limitations are the inability to model path-dependent pore pressure accumulation and the assumption that the strain amplitude is stationary over the event, which is appropriate for stationary ground motions but not for records with pronounced temporal non-stationarity [@Ref006].

Nonlinear effective-stress site response analysis advances the full stress-strain-pore pressure state at each time step, resolving pore pressure generation and dissipation as the strain history evolves [@Ref010][@Ref012]. At each time step and each depth, the constitutive model receives the current strain increment, the current effective stress state, and the accumulated excess pore pressure, and returns the updated stress and pore pressure. This approach naturally captures the coupling between stiffness degradation, pore pressure generation, and wave propagation: as excess pore pressures build up and effective stress decreases, the computed shear modulus and wave velocity decrease in real time, producing a fully coupled dynamic response that the frequency-domain approach cannot reproduce [@Ref010]. The computational cost is substantially higher than the closed-form frequency-domain route because the constitutive model must be evaluated at each grid point and each time step; for a stratum discretized at 200 depth increments and a 40-second record sampled at 0.01 s, this implies on the order of 800,000 constitutive model evaluations per analysis, excluding internal constitutive sub-iterations [@Ref006].

For implementations intermediate between fully analytical and fully coupled constitutive analysis, the volumetric strain model of Martin, Finn, and Seed (1975) and its simplification by Byrne (1991) provide tractable semi-empirical relationships between the shear strain increment and the plastic volumetric reconsolidation strain from which excess pore pressure at constant volume is computed [@Ref006]. The Martin-Finn-Seed formulation expresses the plastic volumetric strain increment per half-cycle as $\Delta\varepsilon_v = C_1(\gamma - C_2\varepsilon_v)^2 / (\gamma + C_3\varepsilon_v)$ for material constants $C_1, C_2, C_3$; excess pore pressure equals the product of the volumetric strain and the one-dimensional rebound modulus. The Byrne (1991) simplification reduces this to a two-parameter model of the form $\Delta\varepsilon_v / \gamma = C_1 \exp(-C_2\varepsilon_v/\gamma)$, which is numerically robust and is widely implemented in FLAC and DEEPSOIL [@Ref006]. These models are directly compatible with the instantaneous-strain-based pipeline of the KB: the shear strain history $\gamma(z,t)$ from `computeStrainHistory` [@Ref002][@Ref001] provides the input to per-half-cycle volumetric strain accumulation, which is integrated over the duration of shaking to yield total excess pore pressure as a function of depth and time [@Ref006].

## SLOT 4: Available R and Python Libraries for Advanced Constitutive Pore Pressure Models and 1D Site Response Integration

Within the R ecosystem, the analytical framework documented in the KB provides a complete and operational implementation of 1D linear viscoelastic site response for power-law shear modulus profiles [@Ref002][@Ref001]. The two public functions `computeAccelField` and `computeStrainHistory` compute the depth-varying acceleration matrix and the instantaneous shear strain history, respectively, using closed-form frequency-domain transfer functions and trapezoidal depth integration. The implementation supports the uniform stratum ($m = 0$), the Gibson deposit ($m = 1$), and the full power-law family ($0 < m < 2$) through a unified calling interface, with near-surface singularities for $m > 0$ regularized by clamping $\eta$ to $10^{-8}H_s$, returning $\gamma = 0$ at the free surface consistently with the zero-stress boundary condition [@Ref002][@Ref001]. As a linear viscoelastic frequency-domain implementation, this R code produces the input strain history for a downstream pore pressure model but does not itself generate pore pressures; coupling it with a pore pressure accumulation model requires additional R code that processes the long-format `data.table` output of `computeStrainHistory` and applies the chosen pore pressure constitutive relationship at each depth and time step [@Ref002][@Ref001].

No dedicated R package for advanced constitutive pore pressure modeling -- including PM4Sand or equivalent formulations -- was identified in the software repositories surveyed for this study [@Ref006]. Geotechnical effective-stress analysis in R remains limited to post-processing of results generated by dedicated computational engines such as OpenSees, DEEPSOIL, or FLAC, or to simplified scalar pore pressure models implemented directly by the analyst. The R ecosystem is well-suited for statistical post-processing, probabilistic hazard integration, and visualization of site response outputs, but the computational kernels for advanced soil constitutive models are written in C, C++, or Fortran and are interfaced through Python, MATLAB, or native GUI applications rather than R [@Ref006].

Within the Python ecosystem, the pyStrata library -- formerly known as pySRA -- by Kottke and Millen implements one-dimensional site response analysis using both equivalent linear and random vibration theory (RVT) methods [@Ref015][@Ref016]. The library provides frequency-domain transfer functions for layered profiles, strain-compatible modulus and damping updates following the standard equivalent linear iterative procedure, and probabilistic response assessment through RVT. pyStrata is the Python functional equivalent of the KB R implementation for equivalent linear analyses; it produces the effective shear strain profile at each iteration, which the analyst passes to a separately implemented pore pressure model, since pyStrata does not include pore pressure generation constitutive functions [@Ref016]. The library is open-source under the MIT license, maintained on GitHub, and distributed via PyPI.

PySeismoSoil, developed by the Geoquake Research Group at Caltech (Shi, Asimaki, and collaborators), is an open-source Python library for nonlinear total-stress 1D seismic site response analysis [@Ref017]. The library implements the hybrid hyperbolic nonlinear soil model of Shi and Asimaki (2017), which transitions smoothly from a stiffness-controlled small-strain response to a strength-controlled large-strain response without requiring modulus reduction and damping curves as separate input [@Ref017]. PySeismoSoil operates in total stress and does not include an integrated effective-stress pore pressure generation model; its output is the nonlinear shear strain time history at each depth, which can be passed to a separately coded pore pressure constitutive model. The library is particularly suited for probabilistic site response studies through its interface to randomized shear wave velocity profiles and ground motion selection tools [@Ref017].

The most complete Python-based environment for advanced constitutive pore pressure modeling within a 1D site response workflow is OpenSeesPy coupled with the PM4Sand effective-stress constitutive model [@Ref018][@Ref019]. PM4Sand, developed by Boulanger and Ziotopoulou (2015, 2017), is a stress-ratio-controlled, fabric-dilatancy plasticity model calibrated to reproduce the undrained cyclic shear strength and post-liquefaction volumetric reconsolidation behavior of clean sands [@Ref020]. Its primary calibration parameters are the relative density $D_R$, the atmospheric-pressure-normalized shear modulus coefficient $G_o$, and the contraction rate parameter $h_{po}$; secondary parameters control fabric evolution and dilatancy [@Ref020]. In OpenSeesPy, PM4Sand is available as `nDMaterial PM4Sand` and is embedded in a 1D effective-stress site response model by assigning the material to a column of `SSPquad` or `quad` elements with undrained boundary conditions; the resulting time history of excess pore pressure ratio $r_u(z,t)$ is available for post-processing at each node [@Ref021]. Chen and Arduino (2021) documented the verification and validation of PM4Sand in OpenSees against laboratory and centrifuge datasets, confirming the adequacy of the implementation for engineering applications [@Ref019].

Integration of the closed-form strain pipeline from the KB [@Ref002][@Ref001] into the Python-based pore pressure workflow can proceed along two paths. In the direct input path, the analytical acceleration field from `computeAccelField` defines the input motion at each soil node in the OpenSeesPy model, and PM4Sand processes the resulting strain increments to produce the pore pressure response at each depth. In the post-processing path, `computeStrainHistory` produces the full $\gamma(z,t)$ matrix; the instantaneous strain increments are extracted time step by time step and supplied to the Byrne or Martin-Finn-Seed volumetric strain model implemented in Python, accumulating excess pore pressure at each depth. For the RMS-based approach, the scalar $\gamma_{RMS}(z)$ extracted from the history provides input to the Dobry (1982) strain-based pore pressure model, returning the cycle count to liquefaction and the pore pressure ratio for a specified cycle count [@Ref009][@Ref006].

## SLOT 5: Failure Extent Criteria and Global Failure Definition in 1D Liquefaction Analysis

### (a) Criteria for the Spatial Extent of Zones Where Excess Pore Pressures Exceed Critical Thresholds

In a one-dimensional liquefaction analysis driven by instantaneous shear strains, the spatial extent of failure is identified by computing the excess pore pressure ratio $r_u = \Delta u / \sigma'_{v0}$ at each depth and comparing it against a critical threshold. The standard engineering criterion for liquefaction triggering is $r_u \geq 1.0$, corresponding to the condition in which effective vertical stress has been reduced to zero and the soil has lost its shear strength entirely [@Ref011][@Ref012]. For partial pore pressure generation prior to triggering, the factor of safety against liquefaction $FS = \mathrm{CRR} \cdot MSF / \mathrm{CSR}$ provides the depth-by-depth criterion: $FS < 1$ identifies the liquefiable zone, and the thickness of the zone where $FS < 1$ is a primary descriptor of the spatial extent of failure [@Ref011]. For fine-grained materials susceptible to cyclic softening rather than full liquefaction, a lower threshold such as $r_u \geq 0.5$ is sometimes applied in practice to identify zones of significant strength loss, though the specific threshold value varies by jurisdiction and project performance objective [@Ref011][@Ref012].

### (b) Point Failure versus Failure of a Proportion of the Stratum Thickness

The question of whether failure at a single depth is equivalent to failure across a proportion of the stratum depends on the engineering performance objective and on empirical evidence regarding the conditions required for surface manifestation of liquefaction. Point failure ($FS < 1$ at a single depth) is a necessary but not sufficient condition for observable ground surface damage; theoretical and field evidence establishes that a liquefiable zone must achieve a minimum thickness to propagate its effects to the surface [@Ref006]. Ishihara (1985) formalized this concept through the H1-H2 boundary curves, which define the minimum thickness $H_2$ of a liquefiable layer, as a function of PGA and the overlying non-liquefiable crust thickness $H_1$, required to produce surface manifestations; layers thinner than the H1-H2 boundary for the applicable PGA level may liquefy internally without surface expression [@Ref022]. Field observations from the Canterbury earthquake sequence (2010-2011), compiled from approximately 7,500 CPT investigations, confirmed that thin liquefied sublayers within a competent crust rarely produced surface damage, while thicker zones beneath a thin crust consistently caused settlement, lateral spreading, and sand ejecta [@Ref022]. The specific threshold of 30% of stratum thickness as a failure criterion does not correspond to a single universally cited primary standard; it appears in practice guidelines as a heuristic for defining a globally significant liquefied zone relative to the total stratum but has not been codified in a DOI-bearing primary reference identified in this review [@Ref006].

### (c) Engineering Significance of Deep versus Shallow Failure Zones

Failure at depth does not carry the same engineering significance as failure at shallow depths; this asymmetry is a foundational design principle embedded in all depth-weighted liquefaction assessment indices. The physical rationale is that liquefied zones close to the surface directly control bearing capacity failure of shallow foundations, the driving mechanism for lateral spreading, and the volumetric reconsolidation that produces settlement [@Ref022][@Ref023]. A liquefied zone at 20 m depth may be hydraulically isolated from the surface by an overlying competent crust; under these conditions surface damage may not occur even if $r_u = 1$ at depth, provided the crust thickness $H_1$ exceeds the Ishihara H1-H2 limit for the applicable PGA [@Ref022]. The Liquefaction Potential Index (LPI) introduced by Iwasaki et al. (1978, 1982) quantifies this depth dependence through the linear weighting function $w(z) = 10 - 0.5z$ for $z$ in meters (zero at 20 m depth), integrated over the top 20 m of the profile [@Ref011][@Ref023]:

$$LPI = \int_0^{20\,\mathrm{m}} F(z)\,w(z)\,dz$$

where $F(z) = 1 - FS(z)$ when $FS(z) < 1$ and $F(z) = 0$ otherwise. This formulation assigns ten times more weight to the near-surface meter than to the zone at 18 m depth, capturing the empirically observed primacy of shallow failure in producing surface damage [@Ref011].

### (d) Design and Analysis Criteria Defining Global Failure

An alternative depth-weighting scheme with hyperbolic rather than linear decay is provided by the Liquefaction Severity Number (LSN), developed from the Canterbury earthquake sequence field database [@Ref022][@Ref024]:

$$LSN = \int_0^{z_{max}} \frac{\varepsilon_v(z)}{z}\,dz$$

where $\varepsilon_v(z)$ is the volumetric reconsolidation strain at depth $z$ and the $1/z$ weighting is hyperbolic [@Ref022]. This weighting assigns more aggressive penalization to shallow failure zones than LPI; the hyperbolic kernel approaches infinity as $z \to 0$, producing an index that is highly sensitive to near-surface pore pressure generation. Van Ballegooy et al. (2014) validated LSN against observed residential land damage from the Canterbury events and found LSN thresholds of approximately 15-30 corresponding to minor damage, 30-50 to moderate damage, and above 50 to severe damage [@Ref025]. A comparative evaluation by Maurer et al. (2015) using a global case history database found that neither LPI nor LSN is universally superior, with relative performance depending on site depth profiles, earthquake characteristics, and the specific damage reference level [@Ref023].

Global failure of a stratum is conventionally defined through threshold values of the depth-integrated index rather than through any single depth criterion. For LPI, the Iwasaki et al. (1982) calibration against Japanese field damage established the following severity thresholds: $LPI > 15$ corresponds to very high liquefaction risk with a high probability of surface manifestation; $5 < LPI \leq 15$ to high risk; $0 < LPI \leq 5$ to low risk; and $LPI = 0$ to negligible risk [@Ref011][@Ref023]. These thresholds apply to the stratum as a whole and constitute the primary engineering design criteria for global liquefaction potential assessment. In design practice a stratum is considered globally failed when the applicable index exceeds the threshold for the performance objective, not merely when $FS < 1$ at any isolated depth. A proxy model that predicts $r_u = 1$ at a single thin sublayer at 18 m depth may produce $LPI < 5$ when integrated over the full profile with the Iwasaki weighting, indicating low global failure risk despite local triggering. The NCHRP 1092 (2024) guidelines recommend that site-specific 1D effective-stress analyses always report depth-integrated indices in addition to point-by-point $r_u$ profiles, providing a globally consistent failure metric for comparison with performance objectives [@Ref012].
