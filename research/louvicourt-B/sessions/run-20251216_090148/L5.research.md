## SLOT 5: Summarize spectral matching methodologies

State-of-practice spectral matching methodologies have evolved significantly over the past decade to provide more accurate representations of seismic ground motions for structural analysis. Spectral matching involves modifying recorded earthquake acceleration time histories to closely align with target response spectra across a defined period range, contrasting with simpler amplitude scaling methods that apply uniform scale factors without altering frequency content [WEB:https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10518-022-01366-0]. The two primary technical approaches include time-domain methods that iteratively add wavelet functions to original accelerograms (implemented in widely-used software such as RspMatch and SeismoMatch) and frequency-domain techniques that adjust Fourier amplitude spectra while preserving phase characteristics [WEB:https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/13632460609350644][WEB:https://seismosoft.com/]. Research demonstrates that spectral matching generally produces more stable results with lower bias and reduced dispersion in spectral ordinates compared to amplitude scaling alone, though it does not necessarily reduce dispersion in nonlinear structural response, meaning close spectral matching cannot justify using fewer ground motion records [WEB:https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11831-022-09844-3].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: This paragraph synthesizes information from multiple peer-reviewed publications on spectral matching methodologies. The description of time-domain wavelet methods and frequency-domain approaches is supported by technical literature citations. The statement about reduced dispersion in spectral ordinates but not necessarily in nonlinear response is from recent research literature (2023).]

Current building codes have incorporated spectral matching as an accepted practice alongside amplitude scaling methods. ASCE 7-16 introduced formal spectral matching provisions in Chapter 16 (Nonlinear Response History Analysis), requiring that each pair of ground motions be matched such that the average of maximum-direction spectra equals or exceeds 110% of the target spectrum over the period range of interest, a more stringent requirement than amplitude scaling to prevent under-prediction of mean structural response [WEB:https://ascelibrary.org/doi/book/10.1061/9780784414248][WEB:https://escholarship.org/uc/item/89f4p2bc]. ASCE 7-22 maintained these provisions while introducing refinements including restrictions on spectral matching for near-fault sites unless pulse characteristics are retained and updating from the two-point design spectrum to a smooth 22-point curve [WEB:https://ascelibrary.org/doi/book/10.1061/9780784415788]. The PEER 2011 report by Baker and colleagues established influential ground motion selection procedures emphasizing spectral shape compatibility through conditional spectrum methods, which remain widely cited in contemporary practice [WEB:https://peer.berkeley.edu/sites/default/files/webpeer-2011-03-jack_w._baker.pdf]. Professional practice typically employs at least seven ground motion records as recommended by current codes to adequately characterize nonlinear structural behavior, with selection focusing on magnitude-distance scenarios identified through disaggregation analysis and consideration of secondary intensity measures such as Arias intensity, cumulative absolute velocity, and significant duration [KB:output.md].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The description of ASCE 7-16 and ASCE 7-22 provisions is taken from official ASCE publications and authoritative technical sources. The reference to PEER 2011 report by Baker et al. is a seminal and widely-cited work in ground motion selection. The KB document confirms the use of at least seven records and secondary intensity measures in the Louvicourt project.]

## SLOT 6: Investigate PEER NGA spectral matching methodology

The PEER (Pacific Earthquake Engineering Research Center) online applications for the NGA-East and NGA-West2 databases provide comprehensive ground motion record selection tools but employ amplitude scaling rather than spectral matching as their primary methodology. The NGA-West2 database, which contains records from shallow crustal earthquakes in active tectonic regions worldwide, and the NGA-East database, which includes over 29,000 records from 81 earthquake events in stable continental regions, both offer web-based interfaces where users can search, select, and download ground motion time series based on various seismological parameters [WEB:https://peer.berkeley.edu/][WEB:https://peer.berkeley.edu/nga-west-2-database]. Critically, all downloaded records from these databases are provided as unscaled and as-recorded (unrotated) accelerograms, with an integrated scaling tool available to determine amplitude scale factors that users apply within their simulation platforms, and these scale factors are included in the record metadata accompanying downloads [WEB:https://ngawest2.berkeley.edu/].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The description of PEER NGA-East and NGA-West2 databases and their web interfaces is from the official PEER Berkeley website. The statement that records are provided as-recorded (unscaled, unrotated) with separate scaling factors is consistent with PEER database documentation and standard practice in ground motion database management.]

The PEER online tools do not perform spectral matching directly within their interfaces, instead providing amplitude scaling functionality where individual records can be scaled to minimize mean squared error relative to a target spectrum or pinned at a single period [WEB:https://peer.berkeley.edu/]. This design philosophy aligns with the databases' core purpose of preserving the integrity of recorded ground motions while providing engineers flexibility in application-specific scaling approaches [WEB:https://peer.berkeley.edu/]. For the Louvicourt project specifically, engineers selected candidate ground motions from the PEER NGA-West2 database and applied amplitude scaling with scale factors generally maintained within the range of 0.5 to 4.0, focusing on achieving geometric mean response spectra between 90% and 130% of the target uniform hazard spectrum within the period range of interest [KB:output.md]. The NGA-East database was consulted but proved less suitable due to a paucity of records with magnitudes exceeding $M_w$ 5.85, whereas the target scenario required $M_w$ 7.3 events [KB:output.md]. When spectral matching is required for specific engineering applications, practitioners typically export selected records from PEER databases and process them using specialized third-party software such as RspMatch, SeismoMatch, or similar wavelet-based spectral matching tools rather than performing matching within the PEER online interface [WEB:https://seismosoft.com/][WEB:https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/13632460609350644].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The statement that PEER tools use amplitude scaling rather than spectral matching is confirmed by PEER documentation. The Louvicourt project details (scale factors 0.5-4.0, target spectrum 90-130%, NGA-East limitations for large magnitudes, target $M_w$ 7.3) are all from the KB document. The workflow of exporting PEER records and using third-party spectral matching software reflects standard professional practice and is supported by software documentation.]
