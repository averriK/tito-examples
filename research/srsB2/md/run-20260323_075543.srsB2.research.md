## SLOT 1: Extension of the Error-Minimization Formulation to the Unit-Sum Constraint

The weight calibration problem formulated in the knowledge base (SLOT 5) is a non-negative least squares (NNLS) problem. Let $\tilde{\mathbf{A}} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times N}$ denote the design matrix whose $(i,k)$-th entry encodes the $k$-th ground-motion model (GMM) prediction at intensity level $i$, and let $\mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ denote the target vector of observed or PSHA-derived reference values. The weight vector $\mathbf{w} = (w_1, \dots, w_N)^\top \in \mathbb{R}^N$ assigns a scalar contribution to each of the $N$ candidate GMMs. The KB formulation minimises the squared $\ell_2$ residual subject exclusively to component-wise non-negativity [KB:spec.compile.research.md]:^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: This lead-in paragraph establishes notation and introduces the KB's NNLS problem. The definitions of the design matrix, target vector, and weight vector are taken directly from KB SLOT 5. The description of the objective and feasible region accurately represents the KB formulation.]

$$\min_{\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^N} \;\|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}\|_2^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad w_k \geq 0, \quad k = 1, \dots, N.$$

The feasible region is the non-negative orthant $\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^N$, a closed convex cone. When $\tilde{\mathbf{A}}$ has full column rank, the objective is strictly convex and the unique global minimiser $\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{\mathrm{NNLS}}$ is computed by the active-set algorithm of Lawson and Hanson (1974), which partitions the index set $\{1,\dots,N\}$ into an active set $\mathcal{Z}$ (components held at zero) and a passive set $\mathcal{P}$ (components free to be positive), iteratively solving unconstrained least-squares sub-problems on $\mathcal{P}$ until the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions for non-negativity are satisfied [KB:spec.compile.research.md][WEB:https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-negative_least_squares].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: This paragraph accurately describes the NNLS formulation and Lawson-Hanson active-set algorithm as explicitly presented in SLOT 5 of the KB document. The characterization of the feasible region as the non-negative orthant, the strict convexity under full column rank, and the Lawson-Hanson algorithm are all stated or implied directly in the KB and are consistent with standard constrained optimization references.]

The normalization condition $\sum_{k=1}^{N} w_k = 1$ requires the weight vector to lie on the standard probability simplex $\Delta^{N-1} = \{\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^N : w_k \geq 0\; \forall k,\; \mathbf{1}^\top\mathbf{w} = 1\}$. The simplex is a compact convex polytope: it is bounded (all components lie in $[0,1]$ under non-negativity and sum-to-one), closed, and has $N$ vertices $\mathbf{e}_1, \dots, \mathbf{e}_N$ (unit coordinate vectors) and $N$ facets. The introduction of the equality constraint $\mathbf{1}^\top\mathbf{w} = 1$ as an additional conditioning step converts the KB formulation into a quadratic program (QP) over the simplex [WEB:https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_programming]:^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: This paragraph introduces the standard simplex and the extended problem formulation. The simplex topology (compactness, polytope structure, number of vertices) is a standard result in convex geometry. The identification of the additional equality constraint as converting the NNLS cone problem into a QP over the simplex follows directly from the definition of the feasible set.]

$$\text{(P)} \quad \min_{\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^N} \;\|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}\|_2^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad w_k \geq 0, \quad k = 1, \dots, N, \quad \mathbf{1}^\top\mathbf{w} = 1.$$

Because the feasible set $\Delta^{N-1}$ is compact and the objective is continuous and strictly convex (under full column rank of $\tilde{\mathbf{A}}$), problem (P) has a unique global minimiser $\mathbf{w}^*$ [WEB:https://optimization.mccormick.northwestern.edu/index.php/Quadratic_programming].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The definition of the standard simplex, its topological properties, and the existence-uniqueness result for a strictly convex objective over a compact convex set are standard results in convex analysis. The formulation of (P) follows directly from adding the equality constraint to the KB's NNLS problem. No numerical claims are made that require external empirical support.]

Expanding the squared residual, problem (P) is equivalently written as:^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: This sentence introduces the algebraic expansion of the squared residual into standard QP form. The expansion is a routine identity and the prose serves only as a structural lead-in to the displayed equation; the substantive mathematical content and citation are in the following paragraph.]

$$\min_{\mathbf{w}} \;\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{w} - 2\mathbf{c}^\top\mathbf{w} \quad \text{subject to} \quad \mathbf{w} \geq \mathbf{0}, \;\; \mathbf{1}^\top\mathbf{w} = 1,$$

where $\mathbf{Q} = \tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\tilde{\mathbf{A}} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$ is the positive semidefinite Gram matrix (positive definite when $\tilde{\mathbf{A}}$ has full column rank) and $\mathbf{c} = \tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^N$ is the cross-correlation vector between each GMM column and the target. The constant $\|\mathbf{y}\|_2^2$ is dropped as it does not affect the argmin. The $(j,k)$-th entry of $\mathbf{Q}$, equal to $\sum_{i=1}^n \tilde{A}_{ij}\tilde{A}_{ik}$, quantifies the spectral (or hazard-curve) similarity between GMMs $j$ and $k$ over the evaluation grid, and the $k$-th entry of $\mathbf{c}$, equal to $\sum_{i=1}^n \tilde{A}_{ik} y_i$, measures the alignment of GMM $k$ with the target [KB:spec.compile.research.md].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The expansion of the squared residual into the quadratic form involving the Gram matrix Q and cross-correlation vector c is a standard algebraic identity. The interpretation of Q entries as spectral similarity and c entries as cross-correlations with the target follows directly from the definitions in SLOT 3 and SLOT 5 of the KB document.]

The optimality conditions for problem (P) are derived from the Lagrangian. Introducing a scalar multiplier $\mu \in \mathbb{R}$ for the equality constraint and a non-negative multiplier vector $\boldsymbol{\nu} = (\nu_1, \dots, \nu_N)^\top \geq \mathbf{0}$ for the inequality constraints, the Lagrangian is:^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: This lead-in paragraph introduces the Lagrangian construction for the mixed-constraint QP. The identification of a scalar equality multiplier and a vector of non-negative inequality multipliers is the standard Lagrangian formulation for problems of this type, following directly from the constraint structure of problem (P).]

$$\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w}, \mu, \boldsymbol{\nu}) = \|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}\|_2^2 + \mu\left(\mathbf{1}^\top\mathbf{w} - 1\right) - \boldsymbol{\nu}^\top\mathbf{w}.$$

Since the objective is convex and differentiable and the constraints are affine, the KKT conditions are both necessary and sufficient for global optimality [WEB:https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karush%E2%80%93Kuhn%E2%80%93Tucker_conditions].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The Lagrangian construction with a scalar multiplier for the equality constraint and non-negative multipliers for the inequality constraints is standard for mixed-constraint QPs. The statement that KKT conditions are necessary and sufficient for convex problems with affine constraints follows from standard convex optimization theory (Slater's condition is trivially satisfied for the simplex, which has a relative interior).]

The four KKT conditions are stated as follows. Stationarity requires:^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: This lead-in sentence introduces the KKT stationarity condition displayed below. The KKT framework applies because the objective is convex, differentiable, and the constraints are affine, satisfying the regularity conditions established in the preceding paragraph.]

$$\nabla_{\mathbf{w}} \mathcal{L} = 2\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\!\left(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w}^* - \mathbf{y}\right) + \mu\mathbf{1} - \boldsymbol{\nu}^* = \mathbf{0}.$$

Primal feasibility requires $w_k^* \geq 0$ for all $k$ and $\mathbf{1}^\top\mathbf{w}^* = 1$. Dual feasibility requires $\nu_k^* \geq 0$ for all $k$. Complementary slackness requires $\nu_k^* w_k^* = 0$ for all $k$.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: These four KKT conditions are the standard necessary and sufficient conditions for a convex QP with mixed equality and inequality constraints. The stationarity condition follows by differentiating the Lagrangian with respect to w and setting the result to zero. The other three conditions are standard feasibility and complementarity requirements.]

Defining the objective gradient at $\mathbf{w}^*$ as $g_k = 2[\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w}^* - \mathbf{y})]_k$ for each $k$, the stationarity condition and complementary slackness reduce to a compact characterisation of the optimal partition. For any index $k$ in the passive set $\mathcal{P}$ (where $w_k^* > 0$), complementary slackness requires $\nu_k^* = 0$, and stationarity then gives $g_k = \mu$. For any index $k$ in the active set $\mathcal{Z}$ (where $w_k^* = 0$), stationarity gives $\nu_k^* = \mu - g_k$, and dual feasibility then requires $g_k \leq \mu$. The scalar $\mu$ is the common value of the objective gradient across all GMMs that receive positive weight at the optimum, and it is determined uniquely by the equality constraint. Comparing with the KB's NNLS KKT conditions -- which require $g_k = 0$ for $k \in \mathcal{P}$ and $g_k \geq 0$ for $k \in \mathcal{Z}$ -- the role of $\mu$ is to shift the stationarity threshold away from zero: the simplex constraint replaces zero-gradient stationarity with equal-gradient stationarity across the passive set [KB:spec.compile.research.md][WEB:https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-negative_least_squares].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The derivation of the compact KKT characterisation (g_k = mu for passive set, g_k <= mu for active set) follows algebraically from substituting the complementary slackness conditions into the stationarity condition. The comparison with the NNLS KKT conditions is direct from the KB (SLOT 5) and is consistent with the mathematical structure of the two problems.]

The solution methodology for problem (P) proceeds by an active-set iteration that extends the Lawson-Hanson algorithm to accommodate the equality constraint. At each iteration, with the active set $\mathcal{Z}$ fixed (so that $w_k = 0$ for $k \in \mathcal{Z}$), the constraint $\mathbf{1}^\top\mathbf{w} = 1$ reduces to $\mathbf{1}_\mathcal{P}^\top\mathbf{w}_\mathcal{P} = 1$ on the passive set. The sub-problem restricted to $\mathcal{P}$ is a least-squares problem with a single equality constraint and no inequality constraints:^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: This lead-in describes the reduction of the full simplex-constrained problem to a passive-set sub-problem with a single equality constraint. The reduction follows because all active-set variables are fixed at zero, so the equality constraint involves only the passive-set variables. This is a standard active-set step consistent with the Lawson-Hanson framework from KB SLOT 5.]

$$\min_{\mathbf{w}_\mathcal{P} \in \mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{P}|}} \;\|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}_\mathcal{P}\mathbf{w}_\mathcal{P} - \mathbf{y}\|_2^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad \mathbf{1}_\mathcal{P}^\top\mathbf{w}_\mathcal{P} = 1,$$

where $\tilde{\mathbf{A}}_\mathcal{P} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times |\mathcal{P}|}$ is the sub-matrix of $\tilde{\mathbf{A}}$ restricted to the passive-set columns. This sub-problem has a unique closed-form solution, obtained by introducing the Lagrangian for the equality constraint alone and solving the resulting linear system [WEB:http://www.seas.ucla.edu/~vandenbe/133A/lectures/cls.pdf].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The reduction of the full problem to a passive-set sub-problem with a single equality constraint is a standard step in active-set algorithms for QPs. The stated structure (equality-constrained LS) and the existence of a closed-form solution follow from standard constrained least-squares theory. The Vandenberghe lecture notes confirm this formulation.]

Let $\mathbf{Q}_\mathcal{P} = \tilde{\mathbf{A}}_\mathcal{P}^\top\tilde{\mathbf{A}}_\mathcal{P} \in \mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{P}| \times |\mathcal{P}|}$ and $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_\mathcal{P} = \mathbf{Q}_\mathcal{P}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}_\mathcal{P}^\top\mathbf{y}$ be the unconstrained least-squares solution on the passive set. Applying Lagrange optimality conditions to the sub-problem and invoking the equality constraint to determine the multiplier $\mu$, the closed-form constrained solution on $\mathcal{P}$ is [WEB:http://www.seas.ucla.edu/~vandenbe/133A/lectures/cls.pdf]:^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: This lead-in sentence introduces the closed-form equality-constrained LS result displayed below. The result follows from standard Lagrange multiplier theory for equality-constrained quadratic programs, as documented in the Vandenberghe lecture notes (cls.pdf).]

$$\mathbf{w}_\mathcal{P}^* = \hat{\mathbf{w}}_\mathcal{P} - \frac{\mathbf{1}_\mathcal{P}^\top\hat{\mathbf{w}}_\mathcal{P} - 1}{\mathbf{1}_\mathcal{P}^\top\mathbf{Q}_\mathcal{P}^{-1}\mathbf{1}_\mathcal{P}}\;\mathbf{Q}_\mathcal{P}^{-1}\mathbf{1}_\mathcal{P},$$

with corresponding Lagrange multiplier:^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: This lead-in phrase introduces the Lagrange multiplier expression derived by substituting the constrained solution back into the equality constraint. The derivation is purely algebraic and follows from rearranging the KKT stationarity system for the passive-set sub-problem.]

$$\mu = \frac{2\left(\mathbf{1}_\mathcal{P}^\top\hat{\mathbf{w}}_\mathcal{P} - 1\right)}{\mathbf{1}_\mathcal{P}^\top\mathbf{Q}_\mathcal{P}^{-1}\mathbf{1}_\mathcal{P}}.$$

The term $\mathbf{1}_\mathcal{P}^\top\hat{\mathbf{w}}_\mathcal{P} - 1$ measures the surplus (or deficit) of the unconstrained passive-set solution relative to the required unit sum, and the denominator $\mathbf{1}_\mathcal{P}^\top\mathbf{Q}_\mathcal{P}^{-1}\mathbf{1}_\mathcal{P}$ is a positive scalar when $\mathbf{Q}_\mathcal{P}$ is positive definite. The adjustment redistributes this surplus proportionally across the passive-set components, with the proportion for each component determined by the corresponding row of $\mathbf{Q}_\mathcal{P}^{-1}\mathbf{1}_\mathcal{P}$ [WEB:http://www.seas.ucla.edu/~vandenbe/133A/lectures/cls.pdf].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The closed-form solution for equality-constrained least squares follows from standard Lagrange multiplier theory applied to the quadratic sub-problem on the passive set. The formula matches the result derived in the Vandenberghe constrained least-squares lecture notes and is consistent with the general theory of equality-constrained QPs. The interpretation of the numerator as a sum surplus and the denominator as a positive normalisation constant is algebraically correct.]

The full active-set iteration for problem (P) proceeds as follows. Beginning from a feasible starting point on $\Delta^{N-1}$ (for example, the equal-weight vector $\mathbf{w} = N^{-1}\mathbf{1}$), the algorithm alternates between solving the equality-constrained sub-problem on the current passive set and updating the partition. If the sub-problem solution $\mathbf{w}_\mathcal{P}^*$ has any negative components, those indices are moved from $\mathcal{P}$ to $\mathcal{Z}$ (using the same inner-loop interpolation step as Lawson-Hanson to recover feasibility), and the sub-problem is re-solved. Once $\mathbf{w}_\mathcal{P}^* \geq \mathbf{0}$, the algorithm checks the KKT active-set condition: for each $k \in \mathcal{Z}$, it evaluates $g_k = 2[\tilde{\mathbf{A}}_\mathcal{P}^\top(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}_\mathcal{P}\mathbf{w}_\mathcal{P}^* - \mathbf{y})]_k$ and compares with $\mu$. If $g_k \leq \mu$ for all $k \in \mathcal{Z}$, the current point satisfies all KKT conditions and is the global solution. Otherwise, the index $k^* = \arg\min_k g_k$ (the most violated active-set condition) is moved from $\mathcal{Z}$ to $\mathcal{P}$, and the iteration continues. Because the objective is strictly convex and the feasible set is compact, this procedure terminates in a finite number of steps [WEB:https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active-set_method][WEB:https://rdrr.io/cran/lsei/man/nnls.html].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The described active-set iteration is a standard extension of the Lawson-Hanson algorithm to problems with an additional equality constraint, consistent with the algorithm implemented in the R lsei package's pnnls function. The finite termination property follows from strict convexity and compactness of the feasible set. The description is consistent with the active-set literature cited.]

This formulation is implemented in practice by the `pnnls` function in the R `lsei` package, which solves NNLS with an additional fixed-sum constraint by transforming the problem into an equivalent unconstrained formulation and applying a modified active-set solver. For general convex programming environments, problem (P) can be dispatched to solvers such as `quadprog` (augmenting the inequality matrix with an equality row), to `CVXR` with an explicit `sum(w) == 1` constraint, or to any QP solver that accepts mixed equality-inequality linear constraints alongside a convex quadratic objective, all of which are already described in the KB for the bounded-weight extension [KB:spec.compile.research.md][WEB:https://rdrr.io/cran/lsei/man/nnls.html].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The description of pnnls in the R lsei package as a solver for NNLS with a fixed-sum constraint is directly supported by the lsei package documentation (rdrr.io source). The mention of quadprog and CVXR as alternative solvers is consistent with SLOT 6 and SLOT 7 of the KB, which use exactly these libraries for bounded-weight extensions.]

## SLOT 2: Validity of Post-Hoc Normalization of Positive Weights

The post-hoc normalization procedure is defined as follows. First, solve the KB's NNLS problem (non-negativity constraint only, no unit-sum constraint) to obtain $\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{\mathrm{NNLS}} \in \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^N$. Second, compute the sum $s = \mathbf{1}^\top\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{\mathrm{NNLS}} = \sum_{k=1}^N \hat{w}_k$. Third, define the normalized vector $\tilde{\mathbf{w}} = \hat{\mathbf{w}}^{\mathrm{NNLS}} / s$, which is non-negative and satisfies $\mathbf{1}^\top\tilde{\mathbf{w}} = 1$ by construction, so $\tilde{\mathbf{w}} \in \Delta^{N-1}$. The question is whether $\tilde{\mathbf{w}}$ is the global minimiser of problem (P), i.e., whether $\tilde{\mathbf{w}} = \mathbf{w}^*$ [KB:spec.compile.research.md].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The setup of the post-hoc normalization procedure follows directly from the NNLS formulation in KB SLOT 5 and the simplex-constrained extension in SLOT 1 above. The definition of the normalized vector is algebraically elementary, and the question of whether it coincides with the simplex optimum is well-posed under the given assumptions.]

To determine whether $\tilde{\mathbf{w}}$ satisfies the KKT conditions for problem (P), the gradient of the objective at $\tilde{\mathbf{w}}$ must be evaluated. For any $k$ in the NNLS passive set $\mathcal{P}$ (where $\hat{w}_k > 0$), the NNLS stationarity condition requires $[\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\hat{\mathbf{w}}]_k = [\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\mathbf{y}]_k$. Since $\tilde{\mathbf{w}} = \hat{\mathbf{w}}/s$, one has $\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\tilde{\mathbf{w}} = \tilde{\mathbf{A}}\hat{\mathbf{w}}/s$, and the $k$-th component of the objective gradient at $\tilde{\mathbf{w}}$ evaluates to:^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: This lead-in introduces the gradient expression at the normalized point, derived from the definitions of the normalized solution and the objective gradient. The NNLS stationarity condition cited here is taken directly from KB SLOT 5.]

$$g_k(\tilde{\mathbf{w}}) = 2\left[\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\!\left(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\tilde{\mathbf{w}} - \mathbf{y}\right)\right]_k = \frac{2}{s}\left[\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\hat{\mathbf{w}}\right]_k - 2\left[\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\mathbf{y}\right]_k.$$

Substituting the NNLS stationarity condition $[\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\hat{\mathbf{w}}]_k = [\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\mathbf{y}]_k$ for all $k \in \mathcal{P}$ into the expression above yields the simplified form:^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: This lead-in introduces the substitution step that eliminates $[\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\hat{\mathbf{w}}]_k$ in favour of $[\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\mathbf{y}]_k$. The substitution is valid for all passive-set indices by the NNLS stationarity condition from KB SLOT 5, and the algebra is elementary.]

$$g_k(\tilde{\mathbf{w}}) = \frac{2}{s}\left[\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\mathbf{y}\right]_k - 2\left[\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\mathbf{y}\right]_k = 2\!\left(\frac{1}{s} - 1\right)\!\left[\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\mathbf{y}\right]_k, \quad k \in \mathcal{P}.$$

This expression shows that the gradient at the normalized point is proportional to the cross-correlation $[\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\mathbf{y}]_k$ with proportionality constant $2(1/s - 1)$, which is nonzero whenever $s \neq 1$.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The interpretation follows directly from the displayed equation. The observation that the proportionality constant is nonzero for $s \neq 1$ is elementary. No external sources are required for this algebraic observation.]

The simplex KKT conditions require the existence of a scalar $\mu$ such that $g_k(\tilde{\mathbf{w}}) = \mu$ for all $k \in \mathcal{P}$ (constant gradient across positive-weight components). Substituting the expression derived above, this requires:^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: This lead-in identifies the simplex KKT stationarity requirement -- constant gradient across all passive-set components -- and connects it to the gradient expression derived in the preceding paragraph. The requirement follows from the KKT conditions established in SLOT 1.]

$$2\!\left(\frac{1}{s} - 1\right)\!\left[\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\mathbf{y}\right]_k = \mu \quad \text{for all } k \in \mathcal{P}.$$

When $s \neq 1$, the factor $2(1/s - 1)$ is a nonzero constant, so this condition reduces to the requirement that $[\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\mathbf{y}]_k$ takes the same value for every index $k$ in the passive set $\mathcal{P}$. The quantity $[\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\mathbf{y}]_k = \sum_{i=1}^n \tilde{A}_{ik} y_i$ is the inner product of the $k$-th GMM prediction vector with the target; it varies across GMMs whenever the GMM predictions are non-proportional to each other relative to the target. Equal cross-correlations across all passive-set GMMs is a degenerate condition that depends on the specific data and target and is not satisfied in general [WEB:https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karush%E2%80%93Kuhn%E2%80%93Tucker_conditions].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The algebraic condition for the simplex KKT to be satisfied at the normalized point is derived rigorously from the gradient expression. The observation that equal cross-correlations [A'y]_k across the passive set is required is a direct consequence of the algebra and is not a claim about specific data values. The statement that this is a degenerate condition in general is correct because it requires a very specific relationship between the GMM prediction vectors and the target that depends on the particular dataset.]

The failure of normalization to preserve optimality is demonstrated by a concrete two-GMPE counterexample. Let $N = 2$, $n = 2$, $\tilde{\mathbf{A}} = \mathbf{I}_2$ (the $2 \times 2$ identity matrix), and $\mathbf{y} = (3, 1)^\top$. The NNLS objective is $(w_1 - 3)^2 + (w_2 - 1)^2$; both unconstrained minimisers $w_1 = 3$ and $w_2 = 1$ are non-negative, so the NNLS solution is $\hat{\mathbf{w}} = (3, 1)^\top$ with sum $s = 4$. Post-hoc normalization yields $\tilde{\mathbf{w}} = (3/4,\, 1/4)^\top$. To find the simplex-constrained optimum, one substitutes $w_2 = 1 - w_1$ into the objective, obtaining $f(w_1) = (w_1 - 3)^2 + w_1^2 = 2w_1^2 - 6w_1 + 9$, which achieves its unconstrained minimum at $w_1 = 3/2 > 1$. Since $w_1 \leq 1$ is required by the simplex, the constrained minimum lies at the boundary $w_1^* = 1$, $w_2^* = 0$. The objective values confirm the suboptimality of the normalized solution: $f(\tilde{\mathbf{w}}) = (3/4 - 3)^2 + (1/4 - 1)^2 = 81/16 + 9/16 = 90/16 = 5.625$, whereas $f(\mathbf{w}^*) = (1-3)^2 + 0^2 = 4 + 1 = 5 < 5.625$. The post-hoc normalized vector is therefore strictly suboptimal on the simplex in this instance.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: This is a fully self-contained algebraic counterexample. All arithmetic steps are elementary and verifiable: the NNLS solution at (3,1) follows from non-negativity of the unconstrained minimiser; the normalized vector (3/4,1/4) follows from dividing by s=4; the simplex optimum at (1,0) follows from minimising the constrained quadratic; and the objective values 5.625 and 5 are exact fractions. No citation is required as the counterexample is constructed from first principles.]

The conditions under which post-hoc normalization is equivalent to direct simplex optimization are now characterised precisely. The first and most straightforward condition is $s = 1$: when the NNLS solution already satisfies the unit-sum constraint, no normalization is performed, and $\tilde{\mathbf{w}} = \hat{\mathbf{w}}$. In this case, $\hat{\mathbf{w}}$ satisfies the NNLS KKT conditions with $g_k = 0$ for $k \in \mathcal{P}$ and $g_k \geq 0$ for $k \in \mathcal{Z}$; setting $\mu = 0$ and $\nu_k^* = g_k$ for $k \in \mathcal{Z}$ and $\nu_k^* = 0$ for $k \in \mathcal{P}$, all simplex KKT conditions are satisfied, so $\hat{\mathbf{w}}$ is simultaneously the NNLS and the simplex-constrained optimum [KB:spec.compile.research.md].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The equivalence when s=1 is demonstrated rigorously by showing that the NNLS KKT conditions imply the simplex KKT conditions with mu=0. This follows by direct substitution and is consistent with the general principle that when a feasibility constraint is not active at the optimum (i.e., the NNLS solution already lies on the simplex), the constrained and unconstrained solutions coincide.]

The second equivalence condition is the equal cross-correlation condition: $[\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\mathbf{y}]_k = \rho$ for all $k \in \mathcal{P}$, where $\rho$ is a common scalar. When this holds and $s \neq 1$, the gradient at $\tilde{\mathbf{w}}$ is $g_k(\tilde{\mathbf{w}}) = 2(1/s - 1)\rho$ for all $k \in \mathcal{P}$, which is a constant (call it $\mu$), and the active-set gradient condition requires $g_k(\tilde{\mathbf{w}}) \leq \mu$ for $k \in \mathcal{Z}$. Whether the latter holds depends on the active-set cross-correlations $[\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\mathbf{y}]_k$ for $k \in \mathcal{Z}$, but given that the NNLS active-set condition $[\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\hat{\mathbf{w}} - \mathbf{y})]_k \geq 0$ holds and that $[\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\hat{\mathbf{w}}]_k \geq [\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\mathbf{y}]_k$ for $k \in \mathcal{Z}$, one can verify that $g_k(\tilde{\mathbf{w}}) = 2(1/s)[\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\hat{\mathbf{w}}]_k - 2[\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\mathbf{y}]_k \geq 2(1/s - 1)[\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\mathbf{y}]_k = \mu$ when $[\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\mathbf{y}]_k \leq \rho$ and $s > 1$, or the inequality is reversed when $s < 1$. The direction of the inequality depends jointly on the sign of $(1/s - 1)$ and the relative cross-correlations, so even the equal cross-correlation condition does not generically guarantee that the active-set KKT condition is satisfied at $\tilde{\mathbf{w}}$. In the most common operational setting for PSHA logic trees, the $N$ GMMs have distinct spectral shapes and different alignment with the target hazard curve, so the cross-correlations $[\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\mathbf{y}]_k$ differ across GMMs and neither equivalence condition is satisfied [KB:spec.compile.research.md].^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The analysis of the equal cross-correlation condition and the active-set check involves conditional reasoning that depends on signs of quantities determined by the data. The main claim -- that equal cross-correlations are necessary but not always sufficient -- is rigorously derived from the gradient expression. The operational observation about distinct GMM spectral shapes is qualitative and supported by the KB's description of GMPE diversity in SLOT 1, but the specific claim about cross-correlation inequality direction requires additional case-by-case verification beyond what the KB and cited external sources provide directly.]

The rigorous conclusion is that post-hoc normalization of the NNLS weight vector does not, in general, yield the solution to the unit-sum constrained problem (P). The unit-sum constraint introduces a non-zero Lagrange multiplier $\mu$ that shifts the stationarity threshold uniformly across all positive-weight GMMs: all passive-set gradient components must equal $\mu$, not zero. Post-hoc normalization, by contrast, rescales $\mathbf{w}$ globally by the factor $1/s$, which alters the residual $\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}$ in a manner that depends non-uniformly on the cross-correlations $[\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\mathbf{y}]_k$. Unless these cross-correlations are all equal (a condition with no geometric or physical basis in the general PSHA setting), the gradient at $\tilde{\mathbf{w}}$ is non-constant across the passive set, violating the simplex KKT stationarity condition.
