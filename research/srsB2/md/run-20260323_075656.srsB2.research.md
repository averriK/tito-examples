# Epistemic Logic-Tree Weight Calibration Under Unit-Sum Constraint

## SLOT 1: Extension of the Error-Minimization Formulation to the Unit-Sum Constraint

The non-negative least-squares (NNLS) formulation of the knowledge base casts the weight-calibration problem as minimization of the squared log-spectral residual $\|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}\|_2^2$ subject to the component-wise non-negativity constraint $w_k \geq 0$ for $k = 1, \ldots, N$, where $\tilde{\mathbf{A}} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times N}$ is the log-domain design matrix whose $k$-th column contains the log-spectral predictions of the $k$-th candidate GMPE at $n$ spectral ordinates, and $\mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is the corresponding log-target vector [KB:spec.compile.research.md]. The unit-sum condition $\sum_{k=1}^{N} w_k = 1$, necessary for the branch weights to define a proper discrete probability distribution over the $N$ candidate models, is not enforced within that formulation. Incorporating this condition as an explicit equality constraint changes the feasible set from the non-negative orthant $\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^N$ to the standard $(N-1)$-dimensional probability simplex and fundamentally modifies the structure of the optimality conditions.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The KB (spec.compile.research.md SLOT 5) explicitly defines the NNLS problem with only the non-negativity constraint and no unit-sum condition. The claim that adding an equality constraint modifies the feasible set and the KKT system is a foundational result of constrained optimization theory, well grounded and without contested elements.]

The fully constrained weight-calibration problem appends the linear equality $\mathbf{1}^\top \mathbf{w} = 1$ to the NNLS non-negativity requirements, where $\mathbf{1} \in \mathbb{R}^N$ is the all-ones vector; the resulting quadratic program is [KB:spec.compile.research.md][DOI:10.1137/1.9781611971217]:^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The formulation is a direct extension of the KB NNLS problem (spec.compile.research.md SLOT 5) by appending the unit-sum equality constraint. Lawson and Hanson (1995) DOI:10.1137/1.9781611971217 covers the LSEI class, of which this is an instance. The problem statement is unambiguous and correctly derived.]

$$\min_{\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^N} \;\|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}\|_2^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad \mathbf{1}^\top \mathbf{w} = 1, \quad w_k \geq 0, \quad k = 1, \ldots, N$$

The feasible set is the probability simplex $\Delta^{N-1} = \{\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^N : \mathbf{1}^\top \mathbf{w} = 1,\, \mathbf{w} \geq \mathbf{0}\}$, a closed, bounded convex polytope of affine dimension $N-1$. The objective is strictly convex when $\tilde{\mathbf{A}}$ has full column rank, guaranteeing a unique global minimizer $\mathbf{w}^* \in \Delta^{N-1}$ by the extreme value theorem. This problem belongs to the LSEI (least-squares with equality and inequality constraints) class in the notation of Lawson and Hanson (1995, Chapter 23), with the identifications $\mathbf{E}_1 = \tilde{\mathbf{A}}$, $\mathbf{f}_1 = \mathbf{y}$, $\mathbf{E}_2 = \mathbf{1}^\top$, $\mathbf{f}_2 = 1$, $\mathbf{G} = \mathbf{I}_N$, and $\mathbf{h} = \mathbf{0}$ [DOI:10.1137/1.9781611971217]. A dedicated algorithm for this combined equality and non-negativity least-squares structure was developed by Haskell and Hanson (1981) [DOI:10.1007/BF01584232]. In the remote sensing literature the identical formulation is designated fully constrained least squares (FCLS), where non-negative weights summing to unity represent fractional material abundances [DOI:10.1109/36.911111].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: Existence and uniqueness on a compact convex set for a continuous strictly convex objective follow from standard analysis. The LSEI classification matches Lawson and Hanson (1995) Chapter 23 exactly. Haskell and Hanson (1981) DOI:10.1007/BF01584232 directly addresses combined equality and non-negativity LS. The FCLS designation from Heinz and Chang (2001) DOI:10.1109/36.911111 is well established. All identifications are unambiguous.]

The KKT optimality conditions are derived from the Lagrangian formed by associating an unrestricted multiplier $\mu \in \mathbb{R}$ with the equality constraint and non-negative multipliers $\boldsymbol{\lambda} \in \mathbb{R}^N$ with the non-negativity constraints [DOI:10.1007/978-0-387-40065-5]:^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The Lagrangian is constructed by the standard procedure of associating an unrestricted scalar multiplier with the equality constraint and non-negative multipliers with the inequality constraints, as in Nocedal and Wright (2006, Chapter 12) DOI:10.1007/978-0-387-40065-5. The sign conventions are consistent with standard references.]

$$\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w}, \mu, \boldsymbol{\lambda}) = \|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}\|_2^2 + \mu\!\left(\mathbf{1}^\top \mathbf{w} - 1\right) - \boldsymbol{\lambda}^\top \mathbf{w}$$

Setting $\nabla_{\mathbf{w}} \mathcal{L} = \mathbf{0}$ at the optimal solution $(\mathbf{w}^*, \mu^*, \boldsymbol{\lambda}^*)$ yields the stationarity condition, obtained by differentiating the squared norm and the linear terms [DOI:10.1007/978-0-387-40065-5]:^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The stationarity condition is obtained by differentiating the Lagrangian with respect to $\mathbf{w}$ and setting equal to zero. This is a direct calculation using the gradient of a squared Euclidean norm. Nocedal and Wright (2006) provides the framework.]

$$2\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\!\left(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w}^* - \mathbf{y}\right) + \mu^*\mathbf{1} - \boldsymbol{\lambda}^* = \mathbf{0}$$

The full KKT system further requires primal feasibility ($\mathbf{1}^\top \mathbf{w}^* = 1$, $\mathbf{w}^* \geq \mathbf{0}$), dual feasibility ($\boldsymbol{\lambda}^* \geq \mathbf{0}$), and complementary slackness ($\lambda_k^* w_k^* = 0$ for all $k$). The scalar $\mu^*$ has no counterpart in the pure NNLS stationarity conditions, where the absence of an equality constraint corresponds to $\mu = 0$ identically [KB:spec.compile.research.md]. The term $\mu^* \mathbf{1}$ acts as a uniform shift applied to every component of the gradient residual vector, and its value is determined jointly by the equality constraint and the partition of the index set $\{1,\ldots,N\}$ into positive-weight and zero-weight branches [DOI:10.1007/978-0-387-40065-5].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The full KKT system for QPs with mixed equality and inequality constraints is a standard result covered by Nocedal and Wright (2006). The observation that $\mu = 0$ for NNLS follows from the KB SLOT 5, which formulates no equality constraint. These are well-established results with no contested elements.]

Defining the passive set $\mathcal{P} = \{k : w_k^* > 0\}$ and the zero set $\mathcal{Z} = \{k : w_k^* = 0\}$, complementary slackness forces $\lambda_k^* = 0$ for all $k \in \mathcal{P}$. The stationarity condition for passive-set indices then reduces to $2(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w}^* - \mathbf{y}))_k = -\mu^*$, expressing that the partial derivative of the residual sum of squares with respect to $w_k$ equals the same constant $-\mu^*$ for every GMPE carrying positive weight. For indices $k \in \mathcal{Z}$ the corresponding condition is the inequality $2(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w}^* - \mathbf{y}))_k \geq -\mu^*$. The value of $\mu^*$ represents the marginal cost of enforcing unit-sum normalization and equals zero if and only if the NNLS solution $\hat{\mathbf{w}}$ satisfies $\mathbf{1}^\top\hat{\mathbf{w}} = 1$ [DOI:10.1137/1.9781611971217][DOI:10.1007/978-0-387-40065-5].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The passive/zero partition and the simplified stationarity conditions follow by applying complementary slackness to the general KKT system stated above. The characterization of $\mu^*$ as zero iff the NNLS sum equals one follows from comparing the two KKT systems. Lawson and Hanson (1995) and Nocedal and Wright (2006) both support these observations. The derivation is rigorous.]

Three algorithmic approaches are available for computing $\mathbf{w}^*$. The LSEI algorithm of Lawson and Hanson (1995, Chapter 23) eliminates the equality constraint by dimensional reduction: substituting $w_N = 1 - \sum_{k=1}^{N-1} w_k$ into the objective converts the unit-sum equality into the linear inequality $\sum_{k=1}^{N-1} w_k \leq 1$ and produces an $(N-1)$-variable NNLS sub-problem to which the standard Lawson-Hanson active-set solver applies directly [DOI:10.1137/1.9781611971217]. The second approach augments the QP formulation of the knowledge base by appending the constraint $\mathbf{1}^\top \mathbf{w} = 1$ alongside the $N$ non-negativity constraints, tractable through the CVXR or `quadprog` interfaces described therein [KB:spec.compile.research.md]. The third approach employs iterative gradient projection onto $\Delta^{N-1}$: at each step the gradient update is projected back onto the simplex using the $O(N \log N)$ sorting-based simplex projection algorithm, which is efficient for large $N$ and standard in the simplex-constrained least-squares literature [ARXIV:1309.1541].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The LSEI dimension-reduction approach is described in Lawson and Hanson (1995) Chapter 23 DOI:10.1137/1.9781611971217 and applies directly to the current problem. The QP augmentation extends the KB SLOT 7 framework, cited via KB token. The gradient projection approach via sorting-based simplex projection is established in Wang (2013) arXiv:1309.1541. All three algorithmic citations are well supported.]

The equal-weight configuration $w_k = 1/N$ for all $k$ corresponds to the centroid of $\Delta^{N-1}$, representing the state of maximum model uncertainty with no prior preference assigned to any candidate GMPE. The constrained minimizer $\mathbf{w}^*$ departs from this centroid to the extent that the GMPE log-spectral predictions are heterogeneous relative to the target: when the columns of $\tilde{\mathbf{A}}$ are nearly parallel, the gradient of the objective is nearly uniform across branches and the optimizer remains close to the centroid; when one or more GMPEs substantially outperform the others in approximating $\mathbf{y}$, the solution concentrates weight on those branches and approaches a low-dimensional face of $\Delta^{N-1}$ [WEB:https://web.stanford.edu/~boyd/cvxbook/bv_cvxbook.pdf].^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The equal-weight centroid characterization and its interpretation as maximum model uncertainty are stated directly in the task CONTEXT. The qualitative geometric argument about proximity to the centroid is consistent with the structure of strictly convex quadratics on the simplex and supported by Boyd and Vandenberghe (2004). Precise quantitative bounds on the distance between $\mathbf{w}^*$ and $\mathbf{1}/N$ are not derived analytically here, warranting MEDIUM confidence.]

## SLOT 2: Validity of Post-Hoc Normalization Versus Direct Constrained Optimization

The NNLS formulation of the knowledge base produces a non-negative weight vector $\hat{\mathbf{w}}$ whose sum $s = \mathbf{1}^\top\hat{\mathbf{w}}$ is determined entirely by the data and the non-negativity constraint, with no guarantee that $s = 1$ [KB:spec.compile.research.md]. Post-hoc normalization defines $\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_k = \hat{w}_k / s$ for each $k$, yielding a vector in $\Delta^{N-1}$ since $\tilde{w}_k \geq 0$ and $\mathbf{1}^\top\tilde{\mathbf{w}} = 1$. The question is whether $\tilde{\mathbf{w}}$ coincides with the constrained minimizer $\mathbf{w}^* = \arg\min_{\mathbf{w} \in \Delta^{N-1}} \|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}\|_2^2$ established in SLOT 1.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The definition of post-hoc normalization and the feasibility of $\tilde{\mathbf{w}}$ in $\Delta^{N-1}$ follow immediately from the non-negativity of $\hat{\mathbf{w}}$ and the scalar division. The question is precisely stated and well-posed. No external citation is required for these elementary observations.]

The fundamental obstruction to equivalence is that the objective $f(\mathbf{w}) = \|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}\|_2^2$ is not positively homogeneous. For any scalar $c > 0$, direct expansion of the squared norm gives [DOI:10.1007/978-0-387-40065-5]:^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The expansion that follows is a direct algebraic identity for the squared Euclidean norm. The conclusion of scale non-homogeneity follows from the presence of a linear cross-term alongside the quadratic term. Nocedal and Wright (2006) provides the optimization framework context.]

$$f(c\mathbf{w}) = c^2 \|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w}\|_2^2 - 2c\, \mathbf{w}^\top\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\mathbf{y} + \|\mathbf{y}\|_2^2$$

The cross-term $-2c\, \mathbf{w}^\top\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\mathbf{y}$ is linear in $c$, whereas the quadratic term grows as $c^2$; the function $f(c\mathbf{w})$ cannot be written as $c^\alpha f(\mathbf{w})$ for any exponent $\alpha$ unless $\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{0}$. It follows that the minimizer of $f(c\mathbf{w})$ over $c > 0$ along the ray through $\hat{\mathbf{w}}$ is generally not located at $c = 1/s$, the value that projects $\hat{\mathbf{w}}$ onto the hyperplane $\mathbf{1}^\top\mathbf{w} = 1$; consequently $\tilde{\mathbf{w}}$ need not minimize $f$ over $\Delta^{N-1}$ [DOI:10.1007/978-0-387-40065-5].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The non-homogeneity is an immediate algebraic consequence of the linear cross-term established by the preceding expansion. The conclusion that the ray minimizer does not in general coincide with the simplex projection follows from the asymmetric polynomial dependence on $c$. The argument is rigorous and complete.]

A necessary and sufficient condition for $\tilde{\mathbf{w}} = \mathbf{w}^*$ is obtained by verifying the KKT stationarity condition of the simplex-constrained problem at $\tilde{\mathbf{w}}$. For $k \in \mathcal{P}$ (where $\hat{w}_k > 0$), the NNLS passive-set stationarity gives $(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\hat{\mathbf{w}})_k = (\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\mathbf{y})_k$ [KB:spec.compile.research.md]. Substituting $\tilde{\mathbf{w}} = \hat{\mathbf{w}}/s$ into the simplex KKT stationarity and applying this identity, the requirement that $\tilde{\mathbf{w}}$ satisfies stationarity with $\tilde{\lambda}_k^* = 0$ for $k \in \mathcal{P}$ reduces to [DOI:10.1007/978-0-387-40065-5][DOI:10.1007/BF01584232]:^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The derivation substitutes $\tilde{\mathbf{w}} = \hat{\mathbf{w}}/s$ into the simplex KKT stationarity established in SLOT 1 and applies the NNLS passive-set condition from the KB. Both inputs are cited and established. The algebraic simplification is rigorous.]

$$2(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top \mathbf{y})_k \!\left(\frac{1}{s} - 1\right) + \mu^* = 0 \quad \text{for all } k \in \mathcal{P}$$

For this to hold simultaneously across all passive-set indices when $s \neq 1$, the inner products $(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\mathbf{y})_k = \sum_{i=1}^n \tilde{A}_{ik}\, y_i$ must all equal the constant $\mu^* s / (2(s-1))$. The quantity $(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\mathbf{y})_k$ measures the correlation of the $k$-th GMPE log-spectral vector with the log-target; requiring these correlations to be identical for all positive-weight branches is a non-generic algebraic condition that has no structural basis in the construction of the design matrix or the PSHA target [DOI:10.1109/36.911111][DOI:10.1007/BF01584232].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The interpretation of $(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\mathbf{y})_k$ as a GMPE-target inner product is exact. The statement that requiring equal inner products is non-generic follows from the fact that distinct GMPEs have distinct log-spectral shapes. Heinz and Chang (2001) DOI:10.1109/36.911111 confirm in the FCLS context that NNLS-with-normalization and direct constrained optimization differ for practical data. Haskell and Hanson (1981) DOI:10.1007/BF01584232 provides the constraint satisfaction context.]

The geometric content of this result is as follows. The NNLS solution $\hat{\mathbf{w}}$ minimizes $f$ over the non-negative orthant; post-hoc normalization maps it to the intersection of the ray $\{c\hat{\mathbf{w}} : c > 0\}$ with the hyperplane $\mathbf{1}^\top\mathbf{w} = 1$. The constrained minimizer $\mathbf{w}^*$ is the point on $\Delta^{N-1}$ at which the gradient of $f$ projected onto the tangent space of the simplex vanishes subject to the active-set conditions; this point is determined by the full simplex KKT system with its Lagrange multiplier $\mu^*$. In general the ray through $\hat{\mathbf{w}}$ does not pass through $\mathbf{w}^*$, so the ray-intersection point $\tilde{\mathbf{w}}$ and the KKT minimizer $\mathbf{w}^*$ are distinct [WEB:https://web.stanford.edu/~boyd/cvxbook/bv_cvxbook.pdf][DOI:10.1109/36.911111].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The geometric description of post-hoc normalization as a ray intersection is exact. The characterization of $\mathbf{w}^*$ as the gradient-null point on the simplex follows directly from the KKT conditions in SLOT 1. Boyd and Vandenberghe (2004) provides the geometric optimization framework. Heinz and Chang (2001) confirm the non-coincidence in a practical application. No speculative claims are introduced.]

Three conditions under which $\tilde{\mathbf{w}} = \mathbf{w}^*$ can be stated precisely. The first is the trivial case $s = 1$: $\hat{\mathbf{w}}$ already lies in $\Delta^{N-1}$, is the minimizer of $f$ over the non-negative orthant, and is therefore also the constrained minimizer, making normalization an identity operation. The second is the non-generic algebraic condition that $(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\mathbf{y})_k$ is equal for all $k \in \mathcal{P}$, meaning all positive-weight GMPEs correlate identically with the log-target. The third is that the unconstrained OLS solution $(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\tilde{\mathbf{A}})^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\mathbf{y}$ itself lies in $\Delta^{N-1}$, in which case the unconstrained, NNLS, and simplex-constrained problems share the same global minimizer and no constraint is active [DOI:10.1007/BF01584232].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The three conditions are derived from the preceding analysis. The trivial case follows from NNLS feasibility in the simplex. The second is the necessary condition from the KKT derivation. The third case (OLS minimizer in the simplex) is standard: an unconstrained minimizer that is feasible is also the constrained minimizer. Haskell and Hanson (1981) DOI:10.1007/BF01584232 provides the formal constraint satisfaction context.]

When none of these conditions holds, strict convexity of $f$ on $\Delta^{N-1}$ and uniqueness of $\mathbf{w}^*$ imply $f(\tilde{\mathbf{w}}) > f(\mathbf{w}^*)$: the post-hoc-normalized solution is strictly sub-optimal relative to the direct constrained solution. The sub-optimality gap $f(\tilde{\mathbf{w}}) - f(\mathbf{w}^*)$ grows with the departure of $s$ from unity and with the heterogeneity of the columns of $\tilde{\mathbf{A}}$; both factors are data-dependent. Heinz and Chang (2001) demonstrated empirically that FCLS consistently achieves lower residuals than NNLS followed by normalization for realistic hyperspectral design matrices, confirming that the sub-optimality gap is non-trivial in practice [DOI:10.1109/36.911111].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The strict inequality $f(\tilde{\mathbf{w}}) > f(\mathbf{w}^*)$ for $\tilde{\mathbf{w}} \neq \mathbf{w}^*$ follows from strict convexity of $f$ on $\Delta^{N-1}$ and uniqueness of $\mathbf{w}^*$. The qualitative characterization of the gap is consistent with convex quadratic geometry but stated without a derived bound. Heinz and Chang (2001) DOI:10.1109/36.911111 provide empirical confirmation for practical data.]

Post-hoc normalization does not constitute a mathematically valid substitute for direct constrained optimization in the GMPE weight-calibration context. The unit-sum equality constraint introduces the Lagrange multiplier $\mu^*$, which uniformly shifts the gradient optimality conditions across all active branches, an effect that cannot be reproduced by rescaling the NNLS solution. The minimum log-spectral matching error subject to both non-negativity and unit-sum is achieved only by solving the simplex-constrained problem directly, through the LSEI algorithm, a QP-based solver, or a gradient projection method as described in SLOT 1. Post-hoc normalization may serve as an approximation only when $s$ is sufficiently close to unity, a condition that is data-dependent and cannot be guaranteed in advance [DOI:10.1007/BF01584232][DOI:10.1109/36.911111].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The conclusion summarizes the rigorous findings of this section. The role of $\mu^*$ as a uniform gradient shift is established exactly by the KKT system in SLOT 1. The recommendation to use direct constrained optimization is supported by the mathematical analysis and by Heinz and Chang (2001). Haskell and Hanson (1981) provides the algorithmic foundation. No claims are introduced beyond what the analysis supports.]
