## SLOT 2: Tectonic regimes controlling seismicity

The seismicity within a 1000 km radius of the Louvicourt site is controlled by stable continental region tectonic regime characteristic of the North American Craton. The site is located within the Canadian Shield physiographic province, which is an intraplate continental region characterized by Archean and Proterozoic basement rocks more than 2.7 billion years old. The tectonic setting reflects the interior of the North American Plate, where seismic activity occurs in response to the current stress field rather than active plate boundary processes. Eastern Canada, as a stable continental region, is defined by continental crust that has not experienced major tectonism, magmatism, basement metamorphism, or anorogenic intrusion since the early Cretaceous, and no rifting or major extension since the Paleogene. The regional stress field is well characterized with a maximum horizontal compressive stress axis oriented northeast-southwest, attributed to spreading along the Mid-Atlantic Ridge. Seismic activity in this stable continental interior is related to regional stress fields with earthquakes concentrated in regions of crustal weakness, including reactivation of inherited structures such as late Precambrian to early Paleozoic Iapetan rifts and aulacogens, as well as Precambrian suture zones and plate boundaries. The present seismicity in most of the Canadian Shield and bordering areas occurs near craton boundaries, with the Archean cratonic crust being essentially aseismic. Within the study radius, major seismic zones include the Charlevoix seismic zone where earthquakes occur in the Canadian Shield between the surface and 30 km depth, and the Western Quebec Seismic Zone characterized by spatial clustering of weak to moderate recent seismicity. ## SLOT 8: Executive summary of seismic record selection

Causal parameters for record selection were derived from disaggregation analysis showing the dominant hazard contributions come from earthquakes with moment magnitude 7.3 at rupture distances of 170 km, supplemented by assumed parameters including hypocentral depth of 20 km, depth to top of rupture of 10 km, and rupture plane dip of 90 degrees representative of stable continental region earthquakes. The fundamental limitation is that all selected earthquake acceleration time histories were sourced from the PEER NGA-West2 database, which contains ground motion records from active crustal regions in plate boundary settings such as California, Japan, Taiwan, and Italy, rather than from stable continental regions representative of southeastern Canada. ## SLOT 14: State of the art for amplitude scaling

The literature and state of the art regarding amplitude scaling of real seismic records reveals ongoing debate and evolving guidance, with consensus that moderate scaling is generally acceptable but large scale factors introduce concerns about bias and realism. Recent comprehensive reviews indicate that code provisions such as ASCE 7-16 have incorporated procedures for selecting and scaling recorded ground motions for nonlinear time-history analysis, with basic methodologies developed according to ASCE 7-05 and Eurocode 8. The primary argument against unrestricted amplitude scaling centers on the potentially unrealistic nature of scaled records and possible biases in engineering demand parameters of structures, with research demonstrating computed bias ranging from 15 to 60 percent for single-degree-of-freedom systems and 25 to 80 percent for multi-degree-of-freedom systems when scale factors range from 2 to 10. However, numerous investigations have found no detectable bias under the inherent uncertainty of calculations when spectral and scenario compatibility are ensured and sustained amplitude consistency is verified. The acceptable limits for legitimate scaling vary widely across the engineering seismology community, ranging from one (no scaling allowed) to 10 or more, with scale factors as high as 10 being frequently used in practice. Current state-of-the-art guidance suggests that scaled records can be safely used in nonlinear response history analysis to assess seismic structural behavior provided that spectral compatibility is achieved, scenario compatibility with disaggregation results is maintained, and secondary intensity measures remain consistent with the hazard level. The emphasis has shifted from strict limits on scale factor magnitude toward verification that the scaled motions appropriately represent the target hazard through multiple metrics including spectral shape, duration, and energy content. Modern practice increasingly recognizes that amplitude scaling is most appropriate when combined with careful selection of records that already possess characteristics similar to the target scenario, minimizing the degree of scaling required. ## SLOT 15: Reasonableness of large scaling factors

The reasonableness of accepting amplification factors much greater than 2 or much less than 0.5 remains contentious in the seismic engineering community, with modern literature suggesting that such large factors should be avoided or carefully justified through additional verification. Scale factors exceeding 2.0 or below 0.5 represent doubling or halving the amplitude of recorded ground motions, which fundamentally alters the relationship between the original earthquake source characteristics and the modified time history. Research on amplitude scaling has documented systematic bias in structural response when large scale factors are applied, with one comprehensive study showing bias ranging from 15 to 60 percent for single-degree-of-freedom systems with scale factors from 2 to 10, and bias of 25 to 80 percent for multi-degree-of-freedom systems over the same scaling range, indicating that bias increases substantially as scale factors deviate further from unity. Recent state-of-the-art reviews explicitly identify the debate surrounding scale factor limits as a central issue in ground motion selection methodology, noting that criticism of unrestricted amplitude scaling has led to proposals for various constraints on acceptable scale factor ranges. While some practitioners and jurisdictions permit scale factors up to 4 or even 10, particularly when secondary intensity measures are verified to remain consistent with target hazard levels, conservative practice increasingly recommends limiting scale factors to the range of 0.5 to 2.0 or at most 0.25 to 4.0 to minimize potential bias. The acceptance of factors much greater than 2 or much less than 0.5 requires careful justification demonstrating that the scaled records maintain appropriate duration, frequency content, energy measures, and other intensity measures beyond spectral amplitude. For geotechnical applications where duration-dependent phenomena such as liquefaction and cumulative deformation are critical, large scale factors introduce particular concern because amplitude scaling may not preserve the appropriate relationship between peak amplitude and duration of strong shaking. Modern literature emphasizes that scale factors should be minimized by selecting candidate records that already closely match the target scenario in terms of magnitude, distance, and site conditions, thereby reducing the need for large amplitude adjustments.
