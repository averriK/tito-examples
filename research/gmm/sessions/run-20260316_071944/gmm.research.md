# Ground Motion Models: Framework, Logic Trees, and Tectonic Region-Type Assignments

## SLOT 1: Definitions, Notation, and Equations for the GMM Framework

A ground motion prediction equation (GMPE), also referred to as a ground motion model (GMM) or attenuation relationship, provides a mathematical description of the expected distribution of a ground motion intensity measure (IM) conditional on a specified earthquake scenario. The conventional ergodic formulation expresses the natural logarithm of a scalar ground motion IM as the sum of a deterministic median prediction and a zero-mean random residual. For intensity measures such as the spectral acceleration $\mathrm{SA}(T)$ at oscillator period $T$, peak ground acceleration $\mathrm{PGA}$, or peak ground velocity $\mathrm{PGV}$, the standard log-normal form adopted throughout modern GMPEs is as follows.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The standard GMPE log-normal formulation is universally adopted in the NGA-West2, NGA-Sub, and European ESHM20 programs and is consistent across the KB documents and web sources accessed during research.]

$$\ln Y = \mu_{\ln Y}(M_w, \mathbf{R}, \boldsymbol{\theta}) + \epsilon$$

In this expression, $Y$ is the ground motion intensity measure, $M_w$ is moment magnitude, $\mathbf{R}$ denotes the vector of relevant source-to-site distance metrics, $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ collects additional predictor variables such as rupture geometry descriptors and site parameters, $\mu_{\ln Y}$ is the model median (the expected value of $\ln Y$ under the GMPE functional form), and $\epsilon$ is the total residual, which is assumed to follow a zero-mean normal distribution with standard deviation $\sigma_{\ln Y}$. [WEB:https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10518-022-01515-8]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The interpretation of all terms in the standard GMPE equation is consistent with published GMPE documentation in the NGA-West2 and ESHM20 literature and is supported by the cited web source.]

Under the mixed-effects decomposition adopted by most modern empirical GMPEs, the total residual $\epsilon$ is partitioned into a between-event component and a within-event component. For record $j$ belonging to event $i$, the decomposition is as follows.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The mixed-effects residual partitioning is standard in GMPE practice and is consistent with descriptions in multiple web sources accessed during research, including sources from the ESHM20 and NGA-West2 programs.]

$$\epsilon_{ij} = \eta_i + \delta_{ij}$$

Here $\eta_i$ is the between-event (inter-event) residual for event $i$, representing systematic source-to-source variation not captured by the median functional form. Its standard deviation is denoted $\tau$, referred to as the between-event standard deviation. The term $\delta_{ij}$ is the within-event (intra-event) residual for record $j$ of event $i$, representing path and site variability not resolved by the median prediction. Its standard deviation is denoted $\phi$, the within-event standard deviation. Under the assumption that $\eta_i$ and $\delta_{ij}$ are mutually independent, the total aleatory standard deviation satisfies the quadrature relation as follows.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The between-event and within-event residual definitions and their independence assumption are standard in GMPE literature and confirmed by multiple web sources.]

$$\sigma = \sqrt{\tau^2 + \phi^2}$$

All three quantities $\sigma$, $\tau$, and $\phi$ constitute the aleatory variability of the model. They are period-dependent in most modern GMPEs, and their values are tabulated or parameterized as part of the published GMPE. The quantities $\tau$ and $\phi$ may be further partitioned in non-ergodic formulations into site-specific and path-specific components, though the models documented in this chapter are predominantly ergodic. [WEB:https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10518-017-0098-6]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The characterization of sigma components as period-dependent and the distinction between ergodic and non-ergodic formulations are well supported by the cited web source and by the broader GMPE literature.]

The predictor variables entering a GMPE vary by model and tectonic region type, but the following notation applies consistently across the models documented in this chapter. Moment magnitude $M_w$ is the primary source size parameter. For source-to-site geometry, the principal distance metrics are the Joyner-Boore distance $R_{jb}$, defined as the shortest horizontal distance from the site to the surface projection of the fault rupture plane; the closest distance to the rupture plane $R_{rup}$; the horizontal distance perpendicular to the fault strike $R_x$; and the hypocentral distance $R_{hypo}$. Site amplification is parameterized primarily by $V_{S30}$, the time-averaged shear-wave velocity in the uppermost 30 m. Basin depth is captured by $z_{1.0}$, the depth to the horizon where shear-wave velocity first reaches 1.0 km/s, and by $z_{2.5}$, the depth to the 2.5 km/s horizon. Rupture geometry descriptors include the depth to the top of the rupture $z_{tor}$, the fault dip angle, and the rake angle. For subduction models, hypocentral depth $h$ is an additional predictor capturing dependence on slab depth, and a binary backarc flag distinguishes forearc and backarc site positions. ## SLOT 2: Definition of GMM/GMPE in the Context of PSHA

A ground motion model (GMM), also called a ground motion prediction equation (GMPE) or attenuation relationship, is an empirical or semi-empirical function that predicts the probability distribution of a ground motion intensity measure conditional on an earthquake scenario specified by source magnitude, source-to-site distance, and ancillary parameters. In probabilistic seismic hazard analysis (PSHA), the annual rate of exceeding a ground motion level $y$ at a site is computed by integrating over all earthquake scenarios the product of the scenario occurrence rate and the probability that the IM exceeds $y$ given that scenario. The standard PSHA hazard integral is as follows.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The role of the GMPE in PSHA and the structure of the hazard integral are well established in PSHA methodology literature and are consistent with authoritative sources accessed during research.]

$$\lambda(Y > y) = \sum_{s} \nu_s \int_{M} \int_{R} f_{M_s}(m)\, f_{R_s}(r)\, P(Y > y \mid m, r)\, dr\, dm$$

In this expression, $\lambda(Y > y)$ is the mean annual rate of exceeding $y$; the summation runs over seismic source zones $s$; $\nu_s$ is the annual rate of earthquakes above a minimum magnitude in source $s$; $f_{M_s}$ and $f_{R_s}$ are the probability density functions of magnitude and distance for source $s$; and $P(Y > y \mid m, r)$ is the probability that the ground motion exceeds $y$ given magnitude $m$ and distance $r$. This conditional exceedance probability is evaluated using the GMPE: under the log-normal assumption, $P(Y > y \mid m, r) = 1 - \Phi\bigl([\ln y - \mu_{\ln Y}(m, r)] / \sigma_{\ln Y}\bigr)$, where $\Phi$ is the standard normal cumulative distribution function. The GMPE thus enters the hazard integral through both its median prediction $\mu_{\ln Y}$ and its total standard deviation $\sigma_{\ln Y}$. [WEB:https://nhess.copernicus.org/articles/24/3049/2024/]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The PSHA hazard integral and its connection to the GMPE log-normal distribution are standard in the seismic hazard literature; the cited ESHM20 overview paper is a recent authoritative source confirming this formulation.]

GMMs constitute one of the principal sources of epistemic uncertainty in PSHA because different models are constructed from distinct datasets, adopt distinct functional forms, and encode distinct assumptions about source scaling, geometric spreading, anelastic attenuation, and site amplification. Epistemic uncertainty, as distinct from aleatory variability, represents incomplete scientific knowledge of the true ground motion process; it is in principle reducible with additional data and is represented in PSHA by a discrete set of alternative models rather than by integration over a continuous distribution. The spread among the median predictions of competing GMPEs across the magnitude-distance-period space of interest is frequently the dominant contributor to the epistemic uncertainty band on the hazard curve, particularly at low annual exceedance rates where the high-sigma tail of the ground motion distribution governs the hazard. [WEB:https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10518-022-01515-8][WEB:https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00024-022-03143-4]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The characterization of GMMs as a principal source of epistemic uncertainty in PSHA and the distinction between aleatory variability and epistemic uncertainty are well established and confirmed by the cited web sources.]

## SLOT 3: Epistemic Uncertainty and Ground-Motion Logic Trees

Epistemic uncertainty in ground motion prediction is represented in PSHA by a ground-motion logic tree, which organizes a discrete set of alternative GMPEs for each tectonic region type into a branching structure. A logic tree consists of one or more branching levels; at each level the branches represent mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive hypotheses about the ground motion process relevant to a given region and tectonic setting. Each branch carries a weight $w_k > 0$ such that $\sum_k w_k = 1$. The logic tree thus defines a discrete probability distribution over the space of plausible GMPEs, encoding the analyst's state of knowledge about which model or family of models best describes ground motion in the target region. [WEB:https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00024-022-03143-4]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The definition and structure of ground-motion logic trees are standard in PSHA methodology and are directly consistent with published descriptions in the cited web sources.]

In operational PSHA, a separate hazard curve $\lambda_k(Y > y)$ is computed for each GMPE branch $k$. The epistemic uncertainty on the hazard is propagated by weighting the branch-level hazard curves to produce a weighted ensemble. Summary statistics of this ensemble, including the mean hazard curve and fractile hazard curves at prescribed probability levels, quantify the epistemic uncertainty in the final hazard estimate. The mean annual rate of exceedance, integrating over the epistemic uncertainty, is the probability-weighted sum $\bar{\lambda}(Y > y) = \sum_k w_k \lambda_k(Y > y)$. This mean hazard curve is the quantity typically used for design and risk applications. [WEB:https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00024-022-03148-z]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The computation of mean and fractile hazard curves via logic tree branch weighting is standard PSHA practice and is confirmed by the cited methodology literature.]

The ground-motion logic trees documented in this chapter each contain a single branching level applied uniformly to all seismic sources of the corresponding tectonic region type. The four tectonic region types covered are Active Shallow Crust (ASC), Stable Continental Crust (SCC), Subduction Interface (SIF), and Subduction Intraslab (SIS). Each logic tree is identified by a unique logic tree ID and a branch set ID, as documented in the OpenQuake NRML input files. The per-TRT logic tree contents, GMPE identifiers, weights, and hazard library parameter requirements are detailed in the sections that follow. [KB:gmmLT_ASC.md][KB:gmmLT_SCC.md][KB:gmmLT_SIF.md][KB:gmmLT_SIS.md]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The single-level structure, TRT assignments, and logic tree identifiers are directly stated in all four KB documents.]

## SLOT 8: Basis for Equally Weighted Logic Tree Branches

All four ground-motion logic trees in this hazard model employ uniform or quasi-uniform branch weights. For the ASC, SIF, and SIS trees each of the ten branches carries $w_k = 0.10000$; for the SCC tree five branches carry $w_k = 0.16667$ and the collapsed NGA-East branch carries $w_k = 0.16665$. In all cases, no individual branch is assigned greater weight than any other on the basis of expert judgment or performance metrics; instead the full spread of credible ground motion predictions is represented by populating the logic tree with a large number of models drawn from diverse modeling traditions. [KB:gmmLT_ASC.md][KB:gmmLT_SCC.md][KB:gmmLT_SIF.md][KB:gmmLT_SIS.md]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The branch weights and tree sizes for all four TRTs are directly confirmed by the respective KB documents.]

In regions where the catalog of recorded strong ground motions is insufficient to discriminate statistically among alternative GMPEs -- a condition characteristic of data-scarce seismic environments -- no empirical basis exists for assigning systematically higher weights to one family of models over another. Assigning differential weights based solely on expert subjective judgment introduces apparent precision that is not warranted by the available evidence and may lead to underrepresentation of the true range of epistemic uncertainty. By contrast, adopting a large set of equally weighted branches drawn from diverse modeling traditions, including the NGA-West2 and NGA-Sub programs, European and Australasian national hazard models, and regional models for Taiwan, Chile, and Mexico, preserves the full spread of scientifically credible predictions without implying that any subset of models is more likely to be correct than another. [WEB:https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10518-022-01515-8][WEB:https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00024-022-03143-4]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The rationale for equal weighting in data-scarce regions, and the argument that differential weighting without data support risks misrepresenting epistemic uncertainty, are well established in the PSHA methodology literature and supported by the cited web sources.]

The choice to include a large number of branches -- ten for ASC, SIF, and SIS, and six for SCC -- rather than a smaller set of differentially weighted alternatives reflects the goal of spanning as wide a range of ground motion predictions as possible across the magnitude, distance, and spectral period dimensions relevant to the hazard problem. Each GMPE in the logic tree encodes distinct assumptions about source scaling, geometric spreading, anelastic attenuation, and site amplification, collectively capturing a wide range of epistemic uncertainty. The equal weighting treats each branch as an equiprobable hypothesis, which corresponds to the maximum-entropy assignment of probability under a state of equal knowledge: when no evidence distinguishes among alternatives, assigning equal probability to each is the most defensible representation of the epistemic state. This principle is particularly well motivated in regions where local strong motion data cannot be used to calibrate, validate, or rank the candidate models.
