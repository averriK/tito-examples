# Seismic Record Selection Methodology for Site Compatibility

## SLOT 1: Executive summary of seismic record selection for site compatibility

The seismic record selection methodology ensures compatibility between earthquake acceleration time histories and project-specific site conditions through a systematic process that integrates probabilistic seismic hazard analysis results with empirical ground motion databases. The approach begins with disaggregation of the site-specific seismic hazard to identify controlling earthquake scenarios characterized by magnitude, distance, and spectral shape parameters. For the reference case, hazard disaggregation at the 1/10,000 annual exceedance probability identified a modal scenario of magnitude $M_w$ 7.3 at a source-to-site distance of 170 km as the dominant contributor to seismic risk across multiple spectral periods [KB:louvicourt.md]. The selection process establishes the mean uniform hazard spectrum as the target spectrum and defines period ranges of interest based on the fundamental periods of the structure under consideration, with conditioning periods $T^*$ selected at the center of each range [KB:louvicourt.md]. Candidate records are sourced from empirical strong motion databases, specifically the PEER NGA-West2 and NGA-East databases, using search criteria based on causal parameters derived from the disaggregation analysis [KB:louvicourt.md]. Individual records are amplitude-scaled using factors generally constrained between 0.5 and 4.0 to achieve spectral compatibility with the target across the period range of interest, where spectral compatibility requires the geometric mean of the selected suite to fall within 90 percent to 130 percent of the target spectrum [KB:louvicourt.md]. Beyond spectral matching, the methodology incorporates secondary intensity measures such as Arias intensity, cumulative absolute velocity, and significant duration as additional selection criteria to ensure that the ground motions capture appropriate shaking severity and temporal characteristics [KB:louvicourt.md]. These secondary intensity measures are conditioned to the hazard level using epsilon values obtained from disaggregation, following established correlation models that relate these measures to spectral acceleration at the conditioning period [KB:louvicourt.md] [WEB:https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/conditional-mean-spectrum]. The final acceptance of a ground motion suite requires both adequate fit to the target spectrum in the geometric mean and reasonable agreement of the empirical secondary intensity measures with their conditional target values, ensuring that the selected time histories represent appropriate representations of the seismic hazard for advanced geotechnical and structural analyses [KB:louvicourt.md].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: This paragraph synthesizes information directly from the KB document Chapter 8 regarding the overall selection methodology, with support from external sources on conditional mean spectrum concepts. ## SLOT 2: Scaling methodology across period ranges

The scaling methodology accounts for the varying dynamic response characteristics of structures with different fundamental periods by establishing period-dependent ranges of interest and applying amplitude scaling factors to match the target spectrum within each range. For structures with shorter fundamental periods, exemplified by a fundamental period $T_S$ ranging from 0.25 s to 0.37 s, the conditioning period $T_S^*$ is selected as 0.31 s, representing the midpoint of the range, and the period range of interest extends from $0.2T_S^*$ to $2.0T_S^*$, corresponding to 0.062 s to 0.62 s [KB:louvicourt.md]. This range captures the spectral content relevant to the first and higher modes of response for shorter-period structures, ensuring adequate representation of high-frequency ground motion components that govern their seismic behavior. For structures with longer fundamental periods, characterized by $T_L$ of approximately 1.15 s, the conditioning period $T_L^*$ is taken as 1.15 s and the corresponding period range of interest spans from $0.2T_L^*$ to $2.0T_L^*$, or 0.23 s to 2.3 s [KB:louvicourt.md]. The use of ranges from 0.2 to 2.0 times the conditioning period provides coverage of both the fundamental mode and significant higher-mode contributions while avoiding over-emphasis on spectral periods far removed from the structural response characteristics. Within each period range, candidate ground motion records are subjected to amplitude scaling, which involves multiplying the entire acceleration time series by a constant factor such that the response spectrum of the scaled record better matches the target spectrum across the period range of interest [KB:louvicourt.md]. The scale factors applied to individual records vary based on the amplitude characteristics of each candidate motion relative to the target, with factors generally maintained within a range of 0.5 to 4.0 to preserve the physical characteristics and frequency content of the original recordings [KB:louvicourt.md]. In cases where suitable records from appropriate tectonic environments are limited, larger scale factors may be employed if the scaled records demonstrate adequate fit to both the target spectrum and the conditional secondary intensity measures [KB:louvicourt.md]. The geometric mean response spectrum of the horizontal components of each selected record is computed and compared to the target spectrum, with the suite-level geometric mean required to match the target within the specified tolerance range across the period range of interest [KB:louvicourt.md]. This scaling approach preserves the temporal characteristics, phase relationships, and frequency content of the original ground motion records while adjusting their amplitude to reflect the site-specific seismic hazard [WEB:https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10518-022-01393-0]. The methodology recognizes that different period ranges may require different candidate record characteristics, particularly when extending seismic hazard estimates from plate boundary regions to stable continental interiors, where attenuation characteristics and site amplification patterns differ systematically [KB:louvicourt.md]. For high-frequency spectral content in stable continental regions with stiff site conditions, records with shorter source-to-site distances than indicated by disaggregation may provide better spectral matches because high-frequency ground motions attenuate more rapidly in softer site conditions typical of plate boundary recording stations [KB:louvicourt.md]. The scaling methodology ultimately aims to develop suites of ground motions that simultaneously satisfy spectral matching criteria across the relevant period range while maintaining realistic ground motion characteristics represented by the secondary intensity measures and physical source-path-site combinations [KB:louvicourt.md].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: This paragraph draws extensively from Section 8.1 and 8.3 of the KB document, which explicitly describe the period ranges (0.062-0.62 s for short period, 0.23-2.3 s for long period), conditioning periods (0.31 s and 1.15 s), the 0.2T* to 2.0T* formulation, scale factor ranges (0.5-4.0), geometric mean matching requirements, and the rationale for using shorter distances for high-frequency content. ## SLOT 3: PEER API spectral matching equations and scaling procedures

The PEER Ground Motion Database provides web-based tools and application programming interfaces for ground motion record selection and scaling, though the database primarily supports amplitude scaling rather than frequency-domain spectral matching through its standard interface [WEB:https://ngawest2.berkeley.edu/]. The quantitative measure used to evaluate spectral compatibility between candidate records and target spectra is the mean squared error computed using the logarithms of spectral period and spectral acceleration, allowing users to weight different period ranges according to structural response priorities [WEB:https://ngawest2.berkeley.edu/peer_ground_motion_database_users_manual.pdf]. The database supports multiple scaling options including unscaled records with scaling factor $f = 1.0$, records scaled to match the target spectrum at a specific period $T_s$, and user-defined constant scaling factors applied uniformly across the record [WEB:https://ngawest2.berkeley.edu/peer_ground_motion_database_users_manual.pdf]. For a given target spectrum $Sa_{target}(T)$ and candidate record with response spectrum $Sa_{record}(T)$, the optimal constant amplitude scaling factor $f$ can be determined by minimizing the mean squared error in logarithmic space over the period range of interest $[T_{min}, T_{max}]$, expressed as minimizing the objective function summed over discrete period points. The database includes uniformly processed time series with response spectral ordinates computed for 111 periods ranging from 0.01 s to 20 s and 11 different damping ratios, providing comprehensive spectral information for record selection and scaling decisions [WEB:https://ngawest2.berkeley.edu/peer_ground_motion_database_users_manual.pdf]. Target spectrum types supported include user-defined spectra representing uniform hazard spectra from probabilistic seismic hazard analysis, conditional mean spectra that account for spectral correlation structure, and code-based design spectra [WEB:https://ngawest2.berkeley.edu/peer_ground_motion_database_users_manual.pdf]. When selecting records through the PEER interface, users specify search criteria including magnitude range, distance range, site condition parameters, and fault mechanism, then apply scaling to minimize misfit to the target spectrum while the database returns candidate records ranked by their spectral compatibility metric [WEB:https://ngawest2.berkeley.edu/]. The mean squared error metric in logarithmic space can be written for discrete period points $T_i$ as the summation $MSE = \sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i [\log(Sa_{record}(T_i) \cdot f) - \log(Sa_{target}(T_i))]^2$, where $w_i$ represents optional weight factors emphasizing specific period ranges and $n$ is the number of period points in the range of interest. Solving for the optimal scaling factor that minimizes this expression yields $f_{opt} = \exp\left[\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i \log(Sa_{target}(T_i)/Sa_{record}(T_i))}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i}\right]$, representing the geometric mean ratio of target to record spectral ordinates across the weighted period range. This formulation differs from more advanced spectral matching approaches that modify the frequency content of records through wavelet addition or iterative adjustment of Fourier amplitude spectra, techniques not directly implemented in the standard PEER database API but available through separate processing tools [WEB:https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10518-022-01393-0]. The PEER NGA-West2 database, which contains records from active shallow crustal regions, and the PEER NGA-East database, which includes records from stable continental regions, provide complementary ground motion sets for different tectonic environments, though the NGA-East database has limited records with magnitudes exceeding $M_w$ 5.85 [KB:louvicourt.md]. ## SLOT 4: Spectral matching method equations with amplification factors

The spectral matching methodology for determining amplification factors across a suite of $N$ seismic records aims to scale each record such that the weighted geometric mean or arithmetic mean of the scaled spectra matches a target average spectrum at $M$ discrete spectral periods. For a suite of $N$ ground motion records, each characterized by spectral acceleration values $Sa_j(T_i)$ where $j = 1, 2, ..., N$ denotes the record index and $i = 1, 2, ..., M$ denotes the period index corresponding to periods $T_1, T_2, ..., T_M$, the objective is to determine amplification factors $f_1, f_2, ..., f_N$ such that the scaled suite matches a target spectrum $Sa_{target}(T_i)$ [WEB:https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10518-022-01393-0]. In the geometric mean formulation commonly used in seismic hazard analysis, the constraint can be expressed as requiring the geometric mean of scaled spectra to equal the target at each period: $$\left[\prod_{j=1}^{N} f_j \cdot Sa_j(T_i)\right]^{1/N} = Sa_{target}(T_i)$$ for $i = 1, 2, ..., M$. Taking the logarithm of both sides yields a linear formulation: $$\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N} \log(f_j \cdot Sa_j(T_i)) = \log(Sa_{target}(T_i))$$, which simplifies to $$\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N} [\log(f_j) + \log(Sa_j(T_i))] = \log(Sa_{target}(T_i))$$. This system of $M$ equations with $N$ unknowns is generally overdetermined when $M > N$, requiring a least squares solution to minimize the error across all period points. The least squares objective function to minimize is expressed as $$\min_{f_1, ..., f_N} \sum_{i=1}^{M} w_i \left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N} \log(f_j Sa_j(T_i)) - \log(Sa_{target}(T_i))\right]^2$$, where $w_i$ are optional weights emphasizing specific period ranges of structural importance [WEB:https://www.hindawi.com/journals/sv/2020/8180612/]. For the arithmetic mean matching approach, the constraint becomes $$\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N} f_j \cdot Sa_j(T_i) = Sa_{target}(T_i)$$ for each period $T_i$, resulting in a system of linear equations that can be solved using constrained optimization with bounds $f_{min} \leq f_j \leq f_{max}$ to maintain scale factors within physically reasonable ranges such as 0.5 to 4.0 [KB:louvicourt.md]. When the number of records equals the number of period constraints ($N = M$) and the system is well-conditioned, exact solutions may exist, but typical applications involve more period points than records ($M > N$), necessitating approximate solutions that minimize the sum of squared errors. An alternative weighted formulation assigns different weights $\alpha_j$ to records based on their initial spectral characteristics, leading to the constraint $$\sum_{j=1}^{N} \alpha_j f_j Sa_j(T_i) = Sa_{target}(T_i)$$ subject to normalization $\sum_{j=1}^{N} \alpha_j = 1$, though uniform weighting ($\alpha_j = 1/N$) is most common in practice [WEB:https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2352012422007421]. The mathematical solution approach can be formulated in matrix notation as $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{f} = \mathbf{b}$, where $\mathbf{A}$ is an $M \times N$ matrix with elements $A_{ij} = Sa_j(T_i)$ representing the unscaled spectral ordinates, $\mathbf{f}$ is the $N \times 1$ vector of amplification factors $[f_1, f_2, ..., f_N]^T$, and $\mathbf{b}$ is the $M \times 1$ vector of target values scaled by the number of records $N \cdot [Sa_{target}(T_1), Sa_{target}(T_2), ..., Sa_{target}(T_M)]^T$ for arithmetic mean matching. The least squares solution is obtained through the normal equations $\mathbf{A}^T\mathbf{W}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{f} = \mathbf{A}^T\mathbf{W}\mathbf{b}$, where $\mathbf{W}$ is a diagonal matrix of period weights, yielding $\mathbf{f} = (\mathbf{A}^T\mathbf{W}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{A}^T\mathbf{W}\mathbf{b}$. In practical implementations, additional constraints may be imposed to prevent unrealistic scale factors, such as requiring all $f_j > 0$ and bounding them within an acceptable range, which transforms the problem into constrained quadratic programming solvable through iterative numerical methods [WEB:https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10518-022-01393-0]. The effectiveness of the resulting scaled suite is evaluated by computing the residual error between the mean of the scaled spectra and the target across the period range of interest, with acceptable performance typically defined as the mean falling within 10 percent to 30 percent of the target [KB:louvicourt.md]. ## SLOT 5: Baker methodology for additional intensity measures in seismic selection

The methodology developed by Baker and colleagues for incorporating additional intensity measures into seismic record selection recognizes that relying solely on spectral acceleration at a single period or matching a target response spectrum is insufficient to fully characterize the potential structural damage and geotechnical response induced by earthquake ground motions [WEB:https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/seismic-hazard-and-risk-analysis/177B4BA01FC2600AA53A22B27598A06C]. The fundamental concept underlying this approach involves conditioning secondary intensity measures on the spectral acceleration at a period of interest, accounting for the correlation structure between different ground motion parameters that arise from common dependencies on earthquake source, path, and site characteristics. For a structure with fundamental period $T^*$, the target spectral acceleration $Sa(T^*)$ is established from the seismic hazard analysis at a specified exceedance probability, and secondary intensity measures such as Arias intensity (AI), cumulative absolute velocity (CAV), and significant duration ($D_{5-75}$ and $D_{5-95}$) are conditioned on this target spectral value [KB:louvicourt.md] [WEB:https://ascelibrary.org/doi/10.1061/(ASCE)ST.1943-541X.0000215]. The conditioning process utilizes the epsilon parameter ($\epsilon$), defined as the number of standard deviations by which the target spectral acceleration exceeds the median ground motion prediction for a given earthquake scenario characterized by magnitude $M$, distance $R$, and other source parameters: $$\epsilon(T^*) = \frac{\log Sa(T^*) - \mu_{\log Sa}(T^*|M,R)}{\sigma_{\log Sa}(T^*)}}$$, where $\mu_{\log Sa}$ represents the median logarithmic spectral acceleration from a ground motion model and $\sigma_{\log Sa}$ is its standard deviation [WEB:https://www.jackwbaker.com/Publications/Baker_(2011)_CMS_Overview,_JSE.pdf]. The conditional mean value of a secondary intensity measure IM given the target spectral acceleration at period $T^*$ is computed using the correlation between the two intensity measures and the epsilon value: $$\mu_{\log IM|Sa(T^*)} = \mu_{\log IM}(M,R) + \rho_{\log IM, \log Sa(T^*)} \cdot \sigma_{\log IM} \cdot \epsilon(T^*)$$, where $\rho_{\log IM, \log Sa(T^*)}$ is the correlation coefficient between the logarithms of the secondary intensity measure and the spectral acceleration at period $T^*$, and $\mu_{\log IM}$ and $\sigma_{\log IM}$ are the median and standard deviation of the secondary intensity measure for the earthquake scenario [WEB:https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1193/090818EQS212M]. Bradley (2012) and Bradley (2015) developed conditional correlation models specifically for CAV and AI, respectively, expressing the correlation coefficients as functions of earthquake magnitude, distance, and spectral period that enable practical implementation of this conditioning approach [WEB:https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0267726115001827] [KB:louvicourt.md]. For Arias intensity, the correlation with spectral acceleration depends on the spectral period, with higher correlations at shorter periods reflecting AI's sensitivity to high-frequency ground motion content, and the conditional median AI given $Sa(T^*)$ can be estimated using the expression provided above with empirically derived correlation functions [WEB:https://www.researchgate.net/publication/334061329_Ground_Motion_Models_for_the_Horizontal_Components_of_Arias_Intensity_AI_and_Cumulative_Absolute_Velocity_CAV_Using_the_NGA-West2_Database]. Similarly, for cumulative absolute velocity, Bradley (2012) established correlation models that relate CAV to spectral acceleration across different periods, enabling computation of conditional CAV targets for ground motion selection [KB:louvicourt.md]. The significant duration intensity measures $D_{5-75}$ and $D_{5-95}$, representing the time intervals over which 5 percent to 75 percent and 5 percent to 95 percent of the Arias intensity accumulates, are typically treated as period-independent parameters and are estimated directly from ground motion models without conditioning on spectral acceleration at a specific period [KB:louvicourt.md]. The practical application of this methodology involves first performing disaggregation of the seismic hazard at the target exceedance probability and period of interest to identify the controlling earthquake scenario (magnitude, distance) and the associated epsilon value, which for rare events such as the 1/10,000 annual exceedance probability typically ranges from 1.0 to 2.0 [KB:louvicourt.md]. Using the epsilon value and established correlation models, conditional target values for each secondary intensity measure are computed, providing criteria against which candidate ground motion records can be evaluated [KB:louvicourt.md]. The ground motion selection algorithm then identifies records from empirical databases that simultaneously match the target response spectrum across the period range of interest and exhibit secondary intensity measures that are statistically consistent with their conditional target distributions [WEB:https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/eqe.2546]. The acceptance criteria typically allow the geometric mean or median of the selected suite's secondary intensity measures to fall within a factor of 1.5 to 2.0 of the conditional median target, recognizing the inherent variability in these parameters and the practical limitations of available recorded ground motions [KB:louvicourt.md].
