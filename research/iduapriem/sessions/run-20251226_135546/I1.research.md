# Seismic Record Selection Methodology for Site-Specific Applications

## SLOT 1: Executive summary of seismic record selection for site compatibility

Seismic records are selected to ensure compatibility with project site conditions through a systematic methodology that integrates site-specific probabilistic seismic hazard analysis results with amplitude scaling and secondary intensity measure validation [KB:louvicourt.md]. The overall approach begins with the development of a uniform hazard spectrum for the design annual exceedance probability, derived from probabilistic seismic hazard analysis that accounts for regional seismotectonics, earthquake recurrence parameters, and ground motion models appropriate for the tectonic setting [KB:louvicourt.md]. Magnitude-distance-epsilon disaggregation of the seismic hazard identifies the earthquake scenarios that contribute most significantly to the site hazard at the spectral periods of interest, providing causal parameters for ground motion selection [KB:louvicourt.md]. Candidate ground motion records are assembled from databases such as the PEER NGA-West2 and NGA-East databases, with selection criteria including earthquake magnitude ranges, source-to-site distances, and recording site conditions that broadly align with the disaggregation results [KB:louvicourt.md]. Individual records are amplitude-scaled so that the geometric mean response spectrum of the suite matches the target uniform hazard spectrum within a specified period range of interest, typically defined as 0.2$T^*$ to 2.0$T^*$ where $T^*$ is the conditioning period corresponding to the fundamental period of the structure [KB:louvicourt.md]. Secondary intensity measures including Arias intensity, cumulative absolute velocity, and significant duration are computed for the scaled records and compared against conditional intensity measure distributions estimated using ground motion models appropriate for the causal earthquake scenario [KB:louvicourt.md][WEB:https://www.jackwbaker.com/Publications/Baker_(2011)_CMS_Overview,_JSE.pdf]. The suite is accepted when the geometric mean spectrum falls within approximately 90 percent to 130 percent of the target spectrum across the period range of interest and the secondary intensity measures provide reasonable agreement with the conditional distributions [KB:louvicourt.md]. This comprehensive methodology ensures that the selected ground motions reflect not only the spectral amplitude characteristics but also the duration and cumulative energy content consistent with the site seismic hazard [KB:louvicourt.md].

## SLOT 2: Scaling methodology across period ranges

The seismic records are scaled differently depending on the fundamental period of the structure being analyzed, with distinct period ranges established to capture the relevant spectral response characteristics [KB:louvicourt.md]. For structures with shorter fundamental periods in the range 0.25 seconds to 0.37 seconds, a conditioning period $T_S^*$ is taken as the center of this range at 0.31 seconds, and the corresponding period range of interest for scaling extends from 0.062 seconds to 0.62 seconds, representing the interval from 0.2$T_S^*$ to 2.0$T_S^*$ [KB:louvicourt.md]. For structures with longer fundamental periods around 1.15 seconds, the conditioning period $T_L^*$ is set to 1.15 seconds and the period range of interest spans 0.23 seconds to 2.3 seconds, again corresponding to 0.2$T_L^*$ to 2.0$T_L^*$ [KB:louvicourt.md]. Within each period range, individual ground motion records are amplitude-scaled by a constant factor applied uniformly across all frequencies so that the geometric mean response spectrum of the horizontal components of the suite of records provides an optimal match to the target uniform hazard spectrum [KB:louvicourt.md]. The scale factors are typically maintained within a range of approximately 0.5 to 4.0, although this constraint may be relaxed when necessary to achieve adequate spectral matching and secondary intensity measure compatibility [KB:louvicourt.md]. The scaling process involves adjusting each candidate record by a multiplicative factor that minimizes the misfit between the scaled geometric mean spectrum and the target spectrum across the designated period range, with the objective that the suite geometric mean falls within 90 percent to 130 percent of the target spectrum at all periods within the range of interest [KB:louvicourt.md]. Different scale factors are assigned to different records in the suite, with each record scaled independently based on its spectral characteristics and contribution to achieving the target spectral match [KB:louvicourt.md]. For the short period range application, scale factors in the selected suite varied from 0.59 to 8.28, while for the long period range the factors ranged from 0.64 to 2.87, reflecting the need for larger adjustments to accommodate high-frequency content requirements and the limited availability of suitable records from stable continental regions [KB:louvicourt.md]. The period range approach ensures that the scaled ground motions accurately represent the seismic demand for the specific structural response characteristics relevant to each fundamental period, accounting for the fact that different structural periods respond to different portions of the acceleration response spectrum [KB:louvicourt.md].

## SLOT 3: PEER API spectral matching equations and scaling procedures

The PEER Ground Motion Database provides spectral matching capabilities through its application programming interface with three primary scaling methodologies: unscaled records where the scale factor equals unity, single-period matching where records are scaled to match the target spectrum at one specific period, and period range matching where records are scaled to minimize the mean squared error between the geometric mean spectra of scaled records and the target spectrum [WEB:https://ngawest2.berkeley.edu/peer_ground_motion_database_users_manual.pdf]. The mean squared error calculation incorporates a user-specified weight function that allows emphasis on particular period ranges critical to the structural application, with higher weights typically assigned to periods near the fundamental period of the structure [WEB:https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2352012422007421]. When applying period range matching through the PEER API, the optimal scale factor $\lambda$ for a given ground motion record is determined by minimizing the objective function that quantifies the difference between the scaled record spectrum and the target spectrum, integrated over the period range with appropriate weighting [WEB:https://peer.berkeley.edu/sites/default/files/baker_et_al_2011_peer_gm_report.pdf]. The database interface enables calculation of the conditional mean spectrum, which differs from the uniform hazard spectrum by conditioning the spectral shape on a specified epsilon value at the conditioning period, where epsilon represents the number of standard deviations by which the spectral acceleration at that period exceeds or falls below the median prediction from ground motion models [WEB:https://www.jackwbaker.com/Publications/Baker_(2011)_CMS_Overview,_JSE.pdf][KB:louvicourt.md]. The API implements equations for computing the conditional mean spectrum at all periods given the spectral acceleration at the conditioning period, using correlation coefficients derived from ground motion databases that describe how spectral ordinates at different periods vary together [WEB:https://www.jackwbaker.com/Publications/Baker_(2011)_CMS_Overview,_JSE.pdf]. For each structural period $T_i$ other than the conditioning period $T^*$, the conditional mean spectrum ordinate is calculated using the median spectral acceleration from the ground motion model adjusted by a factor that depends on the epsilon at $T^*$, the correlation coefficient between $T^*$ and $T_i$, and the logarithmic standard deviations at both periods [WEB:https://www.jackwbaker.com/Publications/Baker_(2011)_CMS_Overview,_JSE.pdf]. The scaling procedures through the PEER API modify the time history amplitude uniformly across all time steps by the computed scale factor, thereby preserving the frequency content and phase characteristics of the original recorded motion while adjusting the spectral amplitude to match the target [WEB:https://ngawest2.berkeley.edu/peer_ground_motion_database_users_manual.pdf]. Advanced features of the API include the ability to select records based on multiple intensity measures simultaneously, specify bounds on scale factors to avoid excessive modification of records, and filter candidate records based on metadata such as magnitude, distance, site conditions, and fault mechanism [WEB:https://ngawest2.berkeley.edu/peer_ground_motion_database_users_manual.pdf].

## SLOT 4: Spectral matching method equations with amplification factors

The spectral matching method for determining amplification factors involves solving an optimization problem where the objective is to find $N$ scale factors, one for each seismic record in the suite, such that the weighted average of the scaled spectral ordinates matches the target spectrum across $M$ discrete periods [WEB:https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2352012422007421]. Given $N$ seismic records each having spectral acceleration ordinates $Sa_j(T_i)$ at $M$ periods $T_i$ for $i = 1, 2, \ldots, M$ and $j = 1, 2, \ldots, N$, the goal is to determine amplification factors $\lambda_j$ for $j = 1, 2, \ldots, N$ such that the scaled suite matches the target spectrum $Sa_{target}(T_i)$ at all periods [WEB:https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10518-022-01393-0]. The fundamental equation for the weighted average spectrum is given by:

$$\frac{1}{N} \sum_{j=1}^{N} \lambda_j Sa_j(T_i) = Sa_{target}(T_i)$$

for each period $T_i$ where $i = 1, 2, \ldots, M$ [WEB:https://www.hindawi.com/journals/sv/2020/8180612/]. In practice, exact matching at all periods is generally not achievable, so the problem is formulated as minimizing the mean squared error between the scaled geometric mean spectrum and the target spectrum, which can be expressed as:

$$\text{MSE} = \sum_{i=1}^{M} w_i \left[ \ln\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\lambda_j Sa_j(T_i)\right) - \ln(Sa_{target}(T_i)) \right]^2$$

where $w_i$ are weight factors assigned to each period $T_i$ to emphasize periods of greatest structural importance [WEB:https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2352012422007421][WEB:https://ngawest2.berkeley.edu/peer_ground_motion_database_users_manual.pdf]. The logarithmic form is commonly used because spectral ordinates and scale factors are approximately log-normally distributed, and the error in logarithmic space provides a better measure of proportional differences [WEB:https://www.hindawi.com/journals/sv/2020/8180612/]. Alternatively, the mean squared error can be computed in linear space as:

$$\text{MSE} = \sum_{i=1}^{M} w_i \left[ \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\lambda_j Sa_j(T_i) - Sa_{target}(T_i) \right]^2$$

although this formulation is less common in earthquake engineering applications [WEB:https://www.hindawi.com/journals/sv/2020/8180612/]. To determine the optimal amplification factors $\lambda_j$, the mean squared error is minimized subject to constraints on the permissible range of scale factors, typically $0.5 \leq \lambda_j \leq 4.0$, to avoid excessive modification that might distort the physical characteristics of the recorded motions [KB:louvicourt.md][WEB:https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10518-022-01393-0]. The optimization can be performed using least-squares methods when the problem is unconstrained or quadratic programming techniques when bounds on the scale factors are imposed [WEB:https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2352012422007421]. In practice, the geometric mean of the horizontal components is computed for each record as $Sa_j(T_i) = \sqrt{Sa_{j,1}(T_i) \times Sa_{j,2}(T_i)}$ where $Sa_{j,1}$ and $Sa_{j,2}$ denote the two orthogonal horizontal components, and the suite geometric mean is calculated as the geometric mean across all $N$ records rather than the arithmetic mean to maintain consistency with ground motion model predictions that typically provide geometric mean intensities [KB:louvicourt.md]. The weight factors $w_i$ are often set to emphasize the period range of interest, with higher weights assigned near the conditioning period $T^*$ and lower weights at periods far from the fundamental structural period, for example using a triangular or trapezoidal weighting function centered at $T^*$ [WEB:https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2352012422007421].

## SLOT 5: Baker methodology for additional intensity measures in seismic selection

Baker and colleagues developed a comprehensive methodology for incorporating additional intensity measures beyond spectral acceleration into seismic record selection to improve the prediction of structural response and ensure consistency with the site seismic hazard [WEB:https://www.jackwbaker.com/Publications/Baker_(2011)_CMS_Overview,_JSE.pdf][WEB:https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/eqe.995]. The approach recognizes that structural response depends not only on spectral amplitude but also on ground motion duration and cumulative energy content, which are captured by secondary intensity measures such as Arias intensity ($AI$), cumulative absolute velocity ($CAV$), and significant duration parameters ($D_{5-75}$ and $D_{5-95}$) [KB:louvicourt.md][WEB:https://www.jackwbaker.com/Publications/Baker_(2011)_CMS_Overview,_JSE.pdf]. The generalized conditional intensity measure approach constructs the multivariate distribution of any set of ground motion intensity measures conditioned on the occurrence of a specific spectral acceleration at the conditioning period $T^*$ [WEB:https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/eqe.995]. For a secondary intensity measure $IM_2$ (such as $AI$ or $CAV$) conditioned on spectral acceleration $Sa(T^*)$, the conditional median is given by:

$$\text{median}[IM_2 | Sa(T^*)] = \text{median}[IM_2 | M, R] \times \exp(\rho_{Sa,IM_2} \times \varepsilon_{Sa(T^*)} \times \sigma_{\ln IM_2})$$

where $\text{median}[IM_2 | M, R]$ is the median value of $IM_2$ predicted by a ground motion model for the causal earthquake magnitude $M$ and distance $R$, $\rho_{Sa,IM_2}$ is the correlation coefficient between $\ln Sa(T^*)$ and $\ln IM_2$, $\varepsilon_{Sa(T^*)}$ is the epsilon value at the conditioning period, and $\sigma_{\ln IM_2}$ is the logarithmic standard deviation of $IM_2$ [WEB:https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/eqe.995][KB:louvicourt.md]. The epsilon at the conditioning period is determined from the hazard disaggregation as the number of standard deviations by which the target spectral acceleration exceeds the median ground motion model prediction for the controlling earthquake scenario:

$$\varepsilon_{Sa(T^*)} = \frac{\ln Sa_{target}(T^*) - \ln Sa_{model}(T^* | M, R, \text{site})}{\sigma_{\ln Sa(T^*)}}$$

where $Sa_{target}(T^*)$ is the target spectral acceleration from the uniform hazard spectrum, $Sa_{model}(T^* | M, R, \text{site})$ is the median spectral acceleration predicted by the ground motion model for the causal parameters, and $\sigma_{\ln Sa(T^*)}$ is the logarithmic standard deviation [KB:louvicourt.md][WEB:https://www.jackwbaker.com/Publications/Baker_(2011)_CMS_Overview,_JSE.pdf]. For the Louvicourt application, epsilon at both conditioning periods was estimated to be 1.5 from the hazard disaggregation at the 1/10,000 annual exceedance probability [KB:louvicourt.md]. Correlation coefficients $\rho_{Sa,AI}$ and $\rho_{Sa,CAV}$ between spectral acceleration and Arias intensity or cumulative absolute velocity have been empirically derived from ground motion databases, with Bradley providing correlation models showing that these secondary measures are positively correlated with spectral acceleration at periods near the conditioning period [WEB:https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/eqe.995][KB:louvicourt.md]. For Arias intensity conditioned on spectral acceleration, the conditional median equation becomes:

$$\text{median}[AI | Sa(T^*)] = \exp(\mu_{\ln AI} + \rho_{Sa,AI} \times \varepsilon \times \sigma_{\ln AI})$$

where $\mu_{\ln AI}$ is the median logarithmic Arias intensity from the ground motion model of Farhadi and Pezeshk for the causal magnitude and distance, and the correlation model is taken from Bradley [KB:louvicourt.md][WEB:https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1193/090818EQS212M]. Similarly for cumulative absolute velocity:

$$\text{median}[CAV | Sa(T^*)] = \exp(\mu_{\ln CAV} + \rho_{Sa,CAV} \times \varepsilon \times \sigma_{\ln CAV})$$

using the CAV ground motion model of Farhadi and Pezeshk and the correlation model from Bradley [KB:louvicourt.md][WEB:https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1193/090818EQS212M]. For significant duration parameters $D_{5-75}$ and $D_{5-95}$, which represent the interval over which 5 percent to 75 percent or 5 percent to 95 percent of the Arias intensity accumulates, the methodology treats these as period-independent intensity measures that are not conditioned on spectral acceleration but rather predicted directly from the earthquake magnitude and distance using models such as Bommer et al. [KB:louvicourt.md]. The conditional distributions of secondary intensity measures are characterized by their median values and logarithmic standard deviations, and selected ground motions are evaluated by comparing their empirical secondary intensity measures against the theoretical conditional distributions to verify that the suite of motions is statistically consistent with the site hazard [KB:louvicourt.md][WEB:https://www.jackwbaker.com/Publications/Baker_(2011)_CMS_Overview,_JSE.pdf]. The validation involves plotting the cumulative distribution function of the secondary intensity measures from the scaled records and comparing it to the theoretical conditional distribution, with acceptable suites showing reasonable agreement without systematic bias toward higher or lower intensity measure values [KB:louvicourt.md]. This methodology ensures that the selected ground motions capture not only the correct spectral amplitude but also the duration and energy characteristics that influence nonlinear structural response, liquefaction potential, and cumulative damage [WEB:https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/eqe.995][KB:louvicourt.md].
