# Seismic Record Selection Methodology for Site-Specific Applications

## Executive Summary of Seismic Record Selection for Site Compatibility

The seismic record selection methodology ensures compatibility between earthquake acceleration time histories and project-specific site conditions through a systematic process that integrates probabilistic seismic hazard analysis results with empirical ground motion databases. The approach begins with disaggregation of the site-specific seismic hazard to identify controlling earthquake scenarios characterized by magnitude, distance, and spectral shape parameters. For stable continental regions, hazard disaggregation at rare annual exceedance probabilities identifies modal scenarios with magnitudes approximately $M_w$ 7.3 at source-to-site distances around 170 km as dominant contributors to seismic risk across multiple spectral periods [@Louvicourt2024]. The selection process establishes the mean uniform hazard spectrum as the target spectrum and defines period ranges of interest based on the fundamental periods of the structure under consideration, with conditioning periods $T^*$ selected at the center of each range [@Louvicourt2024].

Candidate records are sourced from empirical strong motion databases, specifically the PEER NGA-West2 and NGA-East databases, using search criteria based on causal parameters derived from the disaggregation analysis [@Louvicourt2024]. Individual records are amplitude-scaled using factors generally constrained between 0.5 and 4.0 to achieve spectral compatibility with the target across the period range of interest, where spectral compatibility requires the geometric mean of the selected suite to fall within 90 percent to 130 percent of the target spectrum [@Louvicourt2024]. Scale factors applied uniformly to the entire acceleration time series preserve the original frequency content and non-stationary characteristics of recorded ground motions, which are important for capturing realistic seismic response, particularly for geotechnical applications where ground motion characteristics beyond spectral shape influence behavior [@Louvicourt2024].

Beyond spectral matching, the methodology incorporates secondary intensity measures such as Arias intensity, cumulative absolute velocity, and significant duration as additional selection criteria to ensure that the ground motions capture appropriate shaking severity and temporal characteristics [@Louvicourt2024][@Baker2021]. These secondary intensity measures are conditioned to the hazard level using epsilon values obtained from disaggregation, following established correlation models that relate these measures to spectral acceleration at the conditioning period [@Louvicourt2024][@Baker2021][@Bradley2012][@Bradley2015]. The conditioning approach recognizes that spectral ordinates across different periods are correlated, and that selecting motions to match only a uniform hazard spectrum without considering spectral shape can result in overly conservative ground motion suites [@Baker2011a]. The final acceptance of a ground motion suite requires both adequate fit to the target spectrum in the geometric mean and reasonable agreement of the empirical secondary intensity measures with their conditional target values, ensuring that the selected time histories represent appropriate representations of the seismic hazard for advanced geotechnical and structural analyses [@Louvicourt2024].

## Scaling Methodology Across Period Ranges

The scaling methodology accounts for the varying dynamic response characteristics of structures with different fundamental periods by establishing period-dependent ranges of interest and applying amplitude scaling factors to match the target spectrum within each range. For structures with shorter fundamental periods, exemplified by fundamental periods $T_S$ ranging from 0.25 s to 0.37 s, the conditioning period $T_S^*$ is selected as 0.31 s, representing the midpoint of the range, and the period range of interest extends from $0.2T_S^*$ to $2.0T_S^*$, corresponding to 0.062 s to 0.62 s [@Louvicourt2024]. This range captures the spectral content relevant to the first and higher modes of response for shorter-period structures, ensuring adequate representation of high-frequency ground motion components that govern their seismic behavior. For structures with longer fundamental periods, characterized by $T_L$ of approximately 1.15 s, the conditioning period $T_L^*$ is taken as 1.15 s and the corresponding period range of interest spans from $0.2T_L^*$ to $2.0T_L^*$, or 0.23 s to 2.3 s [@Louvicourt2024]. The use of ranges from 0.2 to 2.0 times the conditioning period provides coverage of both the fundamental mode and significant higher-mode contributions while avoiding over-emphasis on spectral periods far removed from the structural response characteristics.

Within each period range, candidate ground motion records are subjected to amplitude scaling, which involves multiplying the entire acceleration time series by a constant factor such that the response spectrum of the scaled record better matches the target spectrum across the period range of interest [@Louvicourt2024]. The scale factors applied to individual records vary based on the amplitude characteristics of each candidate motion relative to the target, with factors generally maintained within a range of 0.5 to 4.0 to preserve the physical characteristics and frequency content of the original recordings [@Louvicourt2024]. In cases where suitable records from appropriate tectonic environments are limited, larger scale factors may be employed if the scaled records demonstrate adequate fit to both the target spectrum and the conditional secondary intensity measures [@Louvicourt2024]. The geometric mean response spectrum of the horizontal components of each selected record is computed and compared to the target spectrum, with the suite-level geometric mean required to match the target within the specified tolerance range across the period range of interest [@Louvicourt2024][@Kaklamanos2022].

For high-frequency spectral content in stable continental regions with stiff site conditions, records with shorter source-to-site distances than indicated by disaggregation may provide better spectral matches because high-frequency ground motions attenuate more rapidly in softer site conditions typical of plate boundary recording stations [@Louvicourt2024]. The scaling methodology ultimately aims to develop suites of ground motions that simultaneously satisfy spectral matching criteria across the relevant period range while maintaining realistic ground motion characteristics represented by the secondary intensity measures and physical source-path-site combinations [@Louvicourt2024].

## PEER API Spectral Matching Equations and Scaling Procedures

The PEER Ground Motion Database provides web-based tools and application programming interfaces for ground motion record selection and scaling, supporting multiple scaling methodologies including unscaled records with scaling factor $f = 1.0$, single-period matching where records are scaled to match the target spectrum at one specific period, and period range matching where records are scaled to minimize the mean squared error between the geometric mean spectra of scaled records and the target spectrum [@PEERDatabase2024][@PEERReport2011]. The mean squared error calculation incorporates a user-specified weight function that allows emphasis on particular period ranges critical to the structural application, with higher weights typically assigned to periods near the fundamental period of the structure [@PEERDatabase2024].

For single-period scaling, each record is scaled by a factor $f_i$ such that the spectral acceleration of the scaled record matches the target spectrum at a specified period $T_s$. The scale factor for the $i$-th record is computed as $f_i = Sa_{target}(T_s) / Sa_i(T_s)$, where $Sa_{target}(T_s)$ is the target spectral acceleration at period $T_s$ and $Sa_i(T_s)$ is the spectral acceleration of the unscaled $i$-th record at that period. For minimum mean square error scaling, the optimal constant amplitude scaling factor $f$ can be determined by minimizing the objective function over the period range of interest $[T_{min}, T_{max}]$. The mean squared error metric in logarithmic space can be written for discrete period points $T_j$ as the summation:

$$
MSE = \sum_{j=1}^{M} w(T_j) [\log(Sa_{record}(T_j) \cdot f) - \log(Sa_{target}(T_j))]^2
$$

where $M$ is the number of spectral ordinates, $w(T_j)$ is a weight function at period $T_j$ that emphasizes periods within the range of structural interest [@PEERDatabase2024]. Solving for the optimal scaling factor that minimizes this expression yields:

$$
f_{opt} = \exp\left[\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{M} w(T_j) \log(Sa_{target}(T_j)/Sa_{record}(T_j))}{\sum_{j=1}^{M} w(T_j)}\right]
$$

representing the geometric mean ratio of target to record spectral ordinates across the weighted period range [@PEERDatabase2024].

The database interface enables calculation of the conditional mean spectrum, which differs from the uniform hazard spectrum by conditioning the spectral shape on a specified epsilon value at the conditioning period, where epsilon represents the number of standard deviations by which the spectral acceleration at that period exceeds or falls below the median prediction from ground motion models [@Baker2011a][@Louvicourt2024]. The API implements equations for computing the conditional mean spectrum at all periods given the spectral acceleration at the conditioning period, using correlation coefficients derived from ground motion databases that describe how spectral ordinates at different periods vary together [@Baker2011a]. The scaling procedures through the PEER API modify the time history amplitude uniformly across all time steps by the computed scale factor, thereby preserving the frequency content and phase characteristics of the original recorded motion while adjusting the spectral amplitude to match the target [@PEERDatabase2024].

## Spectral Matching Method Equations with Amplification Factors

The spectral matching methodology for determining amplification factors across a suite of $N$ seismic records aims to scale each record such that the weighted geometric mean or arithmetic mean of the scaled spectra matches a target average spectrum at $M$ discrete spectral periods. For a suite of $N$ ground motion records, each characterized by spectral acceleration values $Sa_j(T_i)$ where $j = 1, 2, \ldots, N$ denotes the record index and $i = 1, 2, \ldots, M$ denotes the period index corresponding to periods $T_1, T_2, \ldots, T_M$, the objective is to determine amplification factors $f_1, f_2, \ldots, f_N$ such that the scaled suite matches a target spectrum $Sa_{target}(T_i)$ [@Kaklamanos2022].

In the geometric mean formulation commonly used in seismic hazard analysis, the constraint can be expressed as requiring the geometric mean of scaled spectra to equal the target at each period:

$$
\left[\prod_{j=1}^{N} f_j \cdot Sa_j(T_i)\right]^{1/N} = Sa_{target}(T_i)
$$

for $i = 1, 2, \ldots, M$. Taking the logarithm of both sides yields a linear formulation:

$$
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N} \log(f_j \cdot Sa_j(T_i)) = \log(Sa_{target}(T_i))
$$

which simplifies to:

$$
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N} [\log(f_j) + \log(Sa_j(T_i))] = \log(Sa_{target}(T_i))
$$

This system of $M$ equations with $N$ unknowns is generally overdetermined when $M > N$, requiring a least squares solution to minimize the error across all period points. The least squares objective function to minimize is expressed as:

$$
\min_{f_1, \ldots, f_N} \sum_{i=1}^{M} w_i \left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N} \log(f_j Sa_j(T_i)) - \log(Sa_{target}(T_i))\right]^2
$$

where $w_i$ are optional weights emphasizing specific period ranges of structural importance [@Rupakhety2020][@Kaklamanos2022].

For the arithmetic mean matching approach, the constraint becomes:

$$
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N} f_j \cdot Sa_j(T_i) = Sa_{target}(T_i)
$$

for each period $T_i$, resulting in a system of linear equations that can be solved using constrained optimization with bounds $f_{min} \leq f_j \leq f_{max}$ to maintain scale factors within physically reasonable ranges such as 0.5 to 4.0 [@Louvicourt2024]. The mathematical solution approach can be formulated in matrix notation as $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{f} = \mathbf{b}$, where $\mathbf{A}$ is an $M \times N$ matrix with elements $A_{ij} = Sa_j(T_i)$ representing the unscaled spectral ordinates, $\mathbf{f}$ is the $N \times 1$ vector of amplification factors $[f_1, f_2, \ldots, f_N]^T$, and $\mathbf{b}$ is the $M \times 1$ vector of target values scaled by the number of records $N \cdot [Sa_{target}(T_1), Sa_{target}(T_2), \ldots, Sa_{target}(T_M)]^T$ for arithmetic mean matching. The least squares solution is obtained through the normal equations:

$$
\mathbf{f} = (\mathbf{A}^T\mathbf{W}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{A}^T\mathbf{W}\mathbf{b}
$$

where $\mathbf{W}$ is a diagonal matrix of period weights [@Kaklamanos2022]. In practical implementations, additional constraints may be imposed to prevent unrealistic scale factors, which transforms the problem into constrained quadratic programming solvable through iterative numerical methods [@Kaklamanos2022].

## Baker Methodology for Additional Intensity Measures in Seismic Selection

The methodology developed by Baker and colleagues for incorporating additional intensity measures into seismic record selection recognizes that relying solely on spectral acceleration at a single period or matching a target response spectrum is insufficient to fully characterize the potential structural damage and geotechnical response induced by earthquake ground motions [@Baker2021][@Baker2011a]. The fundamental concept underlying this approach involves conditioning secondary intensity measures on the spectral acceleration at a period of interest, accounting for the correlation structure between different ground motion parameters that arise from common dependencies on earthquake source, path, and site characteristics [@Bradley2010][@Baker2011a].

For a structure with fundamental period $T^*$, the target spectral acceleration $Sa(T^*)$ is established from the seismic hazard analysis at a specified exceedance probability, and secondary intensity measures such as Arias intensity ($AI$), cumulative absolute velocity ($CAV$), and significant durations ($D_{5-75}$ and $D_{5-95}$) are conditioned on this target spectral value [@Louvicourt2024][@Baker2011a]. The conditioning process utilizes the epsilon parameter ($\epsilon$), defined as the number of standard deviations by which the target spectral acceleration exceeds the median ground motion prediction for a given earthquake scenario characterized by magnitude $M$, distance $R$, and other source parameters:

$$
\epsilon(T^*) = \frac{\ln Sa(T^*) - \mu_{\ln Sa}(T^*|M,R)}{\sigma_{\ln Sa}(T^*)}
$$

where $\mu_{\ln Sa}$ represents the median logarithmic spectral acceleration from a ground motion model and $\sigma_{\ln Sa}$ is its standard deviation [@Baker2011a][@Louvicourt2024].

The conditional mean value of a secondary intensity measure $IM$ given the target spectral acceleration at period $T^*$ is computed using the correlation between the two intensity measures and the epsilon value:

$$
\mu_{\ln IM|Sa(T^*)} = \mu_{\ln IM}(M,R) + \rho_{\ln IM, \ln Sa(T^*)} \cdot \sigma_{\ln IM} \cdot \epsilon(T^*)
$$

where $\rho_{\ln IM, \ln Sa(T^*)}$ is the correlation coefficient between the logarithms of the secondary intensity measure and the spectral acceleration at period $T^*$, and $\mu_{\ln IM}$ and $\sigma_{\ln IM}$ are the median and standard deviation of the secondary intensity measure for the earthquake scenario [@Bradley2010][@Louvicourt2024]. The conditional standard deviation is:

$$
\sigma_{\ln IM|Sa(T^*)} = \sigma_{\ln IM} \sqrt{1 - \rho_{\ln IM, \ln Sa(T^*)}^2}
$$

reflecting the reduction in uncertainty about the secondary intensity measure once the spectral acceleration at the conditioning period is known [@Bradley2010].

Bradley developed conditional correlation models specifically for $CAV$ and $AI$, expressing the correlation coefficients as functions of earthquake magnitude, distance, and spectral period that enable practical implementation of this conditioning approach [@Bradley2012][@Bradley2015][@Louvicourt2024]. For Arias intensity, the correlation with spectral acceleration depends on the spectral period, with higher correlations at shorter periods reflecting $AI$'s sensitivity to high-frequency ground motion content [@Bradley2015]. Similarly, for cumulative absolute velocity, Bradley established correlation models that relate $CAV$ to spectral acceleration across different periods, enabling computation of conditional $CAV$ targets for ground motion selection [@Bradley2012][@Louvicourt2024].

The significant duration intensity measures $D_{5-75}$ and $D_{5-95}$, representing the time intervals over which 5 percent to 75 percent and 5 percent to 95 percent of the Arias intensity accumulates, are typically treated as period-independent parameters and are estimated directly from ground motion models without conditioning on spectral acceleration at a specific period [@Louvicourt2024][@Bommer2009]. The practical application of this methodology involves first performing disaggregation of the seismic hazard at the target exceedance probability and period of interest to identify the controlling earthquake scenario and the associated epsilon value [@Louvicourt2024]. Using the epsilon value and established correlation models, conditional target values for each secondary intensity measure are computed, providing criteria against which candidate ground motion records can be evaluated [@Louvicourt2024][@Bradley2010].

The ground motion selection algorithm then identifies records from empirical databases that simultaneously match the target response spectrum across the period range of interest and exhibit secondary intensity measures that are statistically consistent with their conditional target distributions [@Bradley2010]. The acceptance criteria typically allow the geometric mean or median of the selected suite's secondary intensity measures to fall within reasonable bounds of the conditional target distributions, recognizing the inherent variability in these parameters and the practical limitations of available recorded ground motions [@Louvicourt2024]. This approach provides a more physically consistent and less conservative ground motion selection framework compared to traditional uniform hazard spectrum matching, as it accounts for the realistic spectral shapes and temporal characteristics expected from earthquakes that control the seismic hazard at the site [@Baker2011a][@Baker2021].
