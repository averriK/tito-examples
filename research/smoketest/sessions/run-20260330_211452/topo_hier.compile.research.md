## SLOT 2: Highest-Density Metal and Structural Significance

Among the three metals catalogued in the knowledge base, copper possesses the greatest density at $8.96\ \text{g/cm}^3$, surpassing iron at $7.874\ \text{g/cm}^3$ and aluminum at $2.70\ \text{g/cm}^3$. [@Ref001]


High density is relevant to structural applications because it determines the mass contained within a given volume of material. A greater mass per unit volume increases the self-weight of structural components, which affects the loads transferred to foundations and supporting elements, and can constrain geometric and material selection decisions. In applications where load-bearing capacity per unit cross-section is the primary criterion, higher density may accompany greater mechanical stiffness or strength for certain materials; however, the additional weight imposes design constraints in contexts where minimizing total mass is a priority. The net suitability of a high-density material in any structural role depends on the balance between mechanical performance requirements and permissible weight. [@Ref001]


## SLOT 3: Iron versus Aluminum Comparison

The principal property differences between iron and aluminum, as documented in the knowledge base, are summarized below. [@Ref001]
- **Melting point**: Iron melts at $1538\ ^\circ\text{C}$; aluminum melts at $660\ ^\circ\text{C}$, a difference of $878\ ^\circ\text{C}$. Iron exhibits substantially greater thermal resistance under high-temperature conditions. [@Ref001]
- **Density**: Iron has a density of $7.874\ \text{g/cm}^3$; aluminum has a density of $2.70\ \text{g/cm}^3$. Aluminum is approximately 2.9 times less dense, resulting in a substantially lower mass per unit volume. [@Ref001]
- **Atomic number**: Iron has atomic number 26; aluminum has atomic number 13. This difference reflects their respective positions in the periodic table and their distinct electron shell configurations. [@Ref001]
