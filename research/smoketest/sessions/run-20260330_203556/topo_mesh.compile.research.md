## SLOT 2: Highest-Density Metal and Structural Relevance

Among the three metals in the KB, copper has the greatest density at 8.96 g/cm$^{3}$, surpassing iron at 7.874 g/cm$^{3}$ and aluminum at 2.70 g/cm$^{3}$. [@Ref001]

High density is relevant to structural applications because mass per unit volume governs the inertial and gravitational behavior of a component. A material with the density of copper, approximately 3.3 times that of aluminum, provides substantially greater resistance to displacement and deformation for an equivalent geometric cross-section. This property makes dense metals advantageous in applications that require counterweights, vibration damping, radiation shielding, or resistance to dynamic impact, where the inherent mass of the material contributes directly to functional performance. [@Ref001]

## SLOT 3: Iron versus Aluminum Comparison

Iron and aluminum differ substantially across all three physical properties documented in the KB. Iron has a melting point of 1538 C versus aluminum's 660 C, a difference of 878 C, indicating that iron maintains its solid state at temperatures well above aluminum's melting threshold. Iron's density of 7.874 g/cm$^{3}$ is approximately 2.9 times aluminum's 2.70 g/cm$^{3}$, making iron considerably heavier per unit volume. Iron also carries a higher atomic number (26) compared to aluminum (13), placing it further along the periodic table. Taken together, iron is denser, thermally more stable, and heavier than aluminum, while aluminum is substantially lighter and melts at a much lower temperature. [@Ref001]
