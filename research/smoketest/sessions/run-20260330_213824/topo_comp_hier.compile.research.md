# Physical Properties of Selected Metals

## SLOT 1: Metals ranked by melting point

The three metals documented in the knowledge base are listed below in descending order of melting point, from highest to lowest. [@MetalsKB]

1. Iron (Fe): $1538\,^\circ\text{C}$ [@MetalsKB][@RSCIron]
2. Copper (Cu): $1085\,^\circ\text{C}$ [@MetalsKB][@RSCCopper]
3. Aluminum (Al): $660\,^\circ\text{C}$ [@MetalsKB][@RSCAluminium]

## SLOT 2: Highest-density metal and structural relevance

Among the three metals in the knowledge base, copper exhibits the highest density at $8.96\,\text{g/cm}^3$, exceeding both iron at $7.874\,\text{g/cm}^3$ and aluminum at $2.70\,\text{g/cm}^3$. [@MetalsKB][@RSCCopper]

Greater mass per unit volume is the functional property that makes high-density materials applicable wherever concentrated mass within a constrained space is the primary design requirement. Dense metals are used as counterweights in aircraft control surfaces and crane assemblies, as ballast in marine vessels and offshore platforms, and as attenuation barriers in protective cladding, because their higher mass density provides stability and resistance to displacement in compact form. [@EngineerFixDense][@LKABBallast] In radiation shielding, density governs the linear attenuation coefficient directly: higher-density materials interact with incident photons more frequently per unit thickness, permitting effective attenuation at reduced bulk volume. [@NISTXray][@MarShieldGamma] Stiffness and wear resistance in metallic materials are not simple functions of density; stiffness is governed primarily by atomic bond strength (quantified by the elastic modulus), and wear resistance correlates with hardness rather than density alone. [@MatPropCopper] Because the knowledge base documents only melting point and density for the three listed metals, quantitative comparison of their mechanical performance in load-bearing or high-contact applications requires property data beyond the current KB scope.

## SLOT 3: Iron versus aluminum comparison

The principal property differences between iron and aluminum, as documented in the knowledge base, are summarized below. [@MetalsKB]

- **Melting point**: Iron melts at $1538\,^\circ\text{C}$; aluminum melts at $660\,^\circ\text{C}$, a difference of $878\,^\circ\text{C}$. Iron exhibits substantially greater thermal resistance under high-temperature conditions. [@MetalsKB][@RSCIron][@RSCAluminium]
- **Density**: Iron has a density of $7.874\,\text{g/cm}^3$; aluminum has a density of $2.70\,\text{g/cm}^3$. Aluminum is approximately 2.9 times less dense, resulting in a substantially lower mass per unit volume. [@MetalsKB][@RSCIron][@RSCAluminium]
- **Atomic number**: Iron has atomic number 26; aluminum has atomic number 13, reflecting their respective positions in the periodic table. [@MetalsKB][@RSCIron][@RSCAluminium]
