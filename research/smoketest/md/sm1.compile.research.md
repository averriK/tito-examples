## SLOT 2: Highest-Density Metal and Structural Relevance

Among the three metals in the knowledge base, copper exhibits the highest density at $8.96\,\text{g/cm}^3$, exceeding both iron at $7.874\,\text{g/cm}^3$ and aluminum at $2.70\,\text{g/cm}^3$. [@Ref001]

High density in a structural material implies greater mass per unit volume, a property relevant in applications where inertia, resistance to penetration, or radiation attenuation is required, including counterweights, ballast systems, and protective cladding. The closer atomic packing associated with high-density metals can also contribute to stiffness and wear resistance in load-bearing and high-contact components. The KB provides only density and melting-point values and does not describe mechanical or structural properties; the structural relevance discussed in this paragraph is based on general materials engineering principles not explicitly stated in the KB. [@Ref001]

## SLOT 3: Iron versus Aluminum Comparison

Iron and aluminum differ substantially in both properties reported in the knowledge base. Iron has a melting point of $1538\,^\circ\text{C}$, compared with $660\,^\circ\text{C}$ for aluminum, a difference of $878\,^\circ\text{C}$ that reflects iron's considerably greater thermal stability and resistance to softening or melting at elevated temperatures. In terms of density, iron at $7.874\,\text{g/cm}^3$ is approximately 2.9 times denser than aluminum at $2.70\,\text{g/cm}^3$, indicating that aluminum offers a substantial mass advantage for applications in which minimizing weight is a priority. [@Ref001]
