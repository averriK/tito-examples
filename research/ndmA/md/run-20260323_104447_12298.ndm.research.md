# Performance-Based Horizontal Seismic Coefficient Estimation for Slope Stability

## Problem Introduction

Pseudo-static slope stability analysis remains the most widely employed method for evaluating the seismic adequacy of earth structures, embankments, and natural slopes. The method applies a constant horizontal force, expressed as a fraction $k_{\max}$ of the gravitational acceleration, to the potential sliding mass and evaluates stability through a conventional limit-equilibrium calculation. The central challenge lies in selecting a value of $k_{\max}$ that reflects both the seismic demand at the site and the displacement performance that constitutes acceptable behavior for the structure under consideration. A performance-based framework addresses this challenge by defining $k_{\max}$ through the statistical distribution of predicted Newmark-type displacements, thereby linking the pseudo-static coefficient to explicit displacement objectives rather than to prescriptive fractions of peak ground acceleration. [KB:pbkmax.qmd][KB:newmark.qmd] ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The description of pseudo-static analysis and the performance-based definition of the seismic coefficient are directly supported by the KB source pbkmax.qmd, which formally defines kmax through exceedance probability, and by newmark.qmd, which describes the general displacement framework.]

The first calibration dimension concerns the target seismic intensity level, which is linked to the degree of consequences associated with slope failure. The allowable permanent displacement $D_n^{\star}$ is specified as a design criterion, and the performance-based seismic coefficient $k_{\max}$ is established as the minimum yield acceleration required to control the probability of exceeding that threshold at the prescribed hazard level. Let $D_n$ denote the random variable representing the Newmark-type displacement, incorporating all aleatory and epistemic uncertainties. For a given yield acceleration $k_y$, the exceedance probability is $p(k_y) = \mathbb{P}[D_n(k_y) > D_n^{\star}]$, and $k_{\max}$ is defined by the implicit relationship: [KB:pbkmax.qmd] ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The exceedance-probability formulation and the definition of kmax as an infimum are transcribed directly from pbkmax.qmd, which provides the formal mathematical framework.]

$$k_{\max}(p \mid D_n^{\star}) = \inf \left\{ k_y : \mathbb{P}[D_n(k_y) > D_n^{\star}] \leq p \right\}$$

This expression links $k_{\max}$ to the site-specific hazard, the displacement-based design objective, and the full statistical characterization of both ground motion and material uncertainty. For any given $p$, $k_{\max}$ represents the lowest value of $k_y$ for which the probability of exceeding the allowable displacement is controlled at the specified level, under the combined statistical effects of hazard, site amplification, and displacement model variability. [KB:pbkmax.qmd] ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: This interpretive paragraph paraphrases the description provided in pbkmax.qmd immediately following the formal kmax definition, maintaining fidelity to the source.]

The second calibration dimension addresses the physical and dynamic characteristics of the slope itself. The distribution of predicted displacements $D_n(k_y)$ depends not only on the ground-motion intensity measures supplied by the site hazard but also on the stiffness, strength, and fundamental period $T_n$ of the sliding mass. Flexible-block displacement models incorporate $T_n$ and spectral acceleration $S_a$ evaluated at periods proportional to $T_n$, thereby capturing the resonant amplification of compliant slopes. Rigid-block models, by contrast, depend primarily on the ratio $k_y / \mathrm{PGA}$ and on duration-sensitive parameters such as Arias intensity $\mathrm{AI}$ and peak ground velocity $\mathrm{PGV}$. The selection among these model types - and their relative weighting in the ensemble - reflects the engineer's assessment of whether the sliding mass is better approximated as a stiff body responding to base excitation or as a compliant system whose internal dynamic response governs the displacement. [KB:newmark.qmd][KB:newmark_model.qmd] ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The distinction between flexible-block and rigid-block models and their respective input parameters is well documented in newmark.qmd and newmark_model.qmd, where each model's functional form and parameter dependencies are explicitly defined.]

The distribution $D_n(k_y)$ reflects the combined effects of ground-motion variability, model dispersion, and site-specific amplification uncertainty. Ground-motion intensity measures are sampled from their empirical quantile functions derived from probabilistic seismic hazard analysis, while each displacement model contributes its own lognormal standard deviation $\sigma_{\ln D}$. Model-to-model epistemic uncertainty is retained through logic-tree weights assigned prior to aggregation of results over all models in the ensemble. The site-response amplification model introduces an additional layer of uncertainty: the reference-rock hazard output carries the combined epistemic and aleatory uncertainty from the PSHA, and the amplification factor contributes its own dispersion $\sigma_{\ln F}$. Both contributions are propagated jointly by Monte Carlo simulation, ensuring that $k_{\max}$ is calibrated against the full uncertainty budget encompassing source, path, site, and model components. [KB:uncertainty_model.qmd][KB:newmark.qmd][KB:site_model.qmd] ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The uncertainty propagation framework, including Monte Carlo convolution of hazard quantiles with model dispersion and site amplification uncertainty, is described in detail in uncertainty_model.qmd and newmark.qmd, with site amplification components documented in site_model.qmd.]

## Current State of Practice

The practice of selecting a horizontal seismic coefficient for pseudo-static slope stability evaluation has evolved substantially since Newmark first proposed the sliding-block analogy in his 1965 Rankine Lecture. The original method conceptualized the sliding mass as a rigid block with a yield acceleration $k_y$; whenever the ground acceleration exceeded $k_y$, the block accumulated incremental downslope displacement, and the total permanent displacement served as the performance metric. This framework overcame the principal limitation of earlier pseudo-static approaches, which provided information only about the onset of instability but offered no estimate of the magnitude of induced deformations. The pseudo-static method itself represents earthquake effects by a single constant unidirectional force equal to the product of a seismic coefficient $k_h$ and the weight of the potential sliding mass - a representation that, while straightforward, does not capture the transient, cyclic, and frequency-dependent character of actual seismic loading. [DOI:10.1680/geot.1965.15.2.139] ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The historical description of Newmark's 1965 Rankine Lecture and the limitations of the pseudo-static method are well established in the geotechnical earthquake engineering literature. The 1965 Geotechnique publication is the foundational reference for the sliding-block method.]

Early rationalizations of the pseudo-static coefficient drew on parametric Newmark analyses of embankment dams. Hynes-Griffin and Franklin (1984), working under the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, concluded that if the factor of safety exceeded 1.0 in a pseudo-static analysis employing a seismic coefficient equal to one-half the peak ground acceleration ($k_h = 0.5 \, \mathrm{PGA}/g$), expected deformations in compacted embankment dams would remain minimal and acceptable. This recommendation became a standard reference in dam engineering practice and was subsequently adopted, with minor modifications, by the Federal Highway Administration for the design of reinforced steep slopes. Although practical and conservative for the class of structures studied, the approach did not account for site-specific dynamic characteristics such as the fundamental period of the sliding mass or for project-specific displacement tolerances. [WEB:https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/tr/pdf/ADA144730.pdf] ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The Hynes-Griffin and Franklin (1984) recommendation of kh = 0.5 PGA and its adoption by FHWA are widely documented in the literature and confirmed by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers technical report available through the Defense Technical Information Center.]

Current normative frameworks reflect a progressive shift from prescriptive coefficients toward displacement-based performance criteria. Eurocode 8 specifies the horizontal seismic force for pseudo-static slope analysis as $F_H = 0.5 \, \alpha \, S \, W$, where $\alpha$ is the ratio of the design ground acceleration to gravitational acceleration, $S$ is a soil-dependent amplification parameter, and $W$ is the weight of the sliding mass. Vertical inertia forces are applied conditionally: $F_V = \pm 0.5 \, F_H$ when the ratio of vertical to horizontal design acceleration exceeds 0.6, and $F_V = \pm 0.33 \, F_H$ otherwise. Pseudo-static methods are permitted where surface topography and soil stratigraphy do not present abrupt irregularities, but soils susceptible to elevated pore-water pressures or significant cyclic stiffness degradation require displacement-based or fully dynamic analysis. [WEB:https://help.geostru.eu/slope/en/eurocodice_8.htm] ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The Eurocode 8 provisions for pseudo-static analysis, including the FH formula, vertical force conditions, and applicability restrictions, were extracted directly from the Eurocode 8 reference documentation.]

The International Commission on Large Dams (ICOLD) has recognized Newmark-type deformation analysis as the international state of practice for seismic safety assessment of dams since its first modern guideline was published in 1989. ICOLD Bulletin 148 provides seismic design parameters for high-consequence and moderate-consequence dams, with the Seismic Evaluation Earthquake defined at return periods of 3,000 and 1,000 years, respectively. In the context of landslide hazard screening, Stewart, Blake, and Hollingsworth (2003) developed a calibrated pseudo-static screening procedure for sites in California that introduced index threshold displacements of 50 mm and 150 mm. A displacement of 50 mm distinguishes conditions in which negligible movement is likely from conditions of moderate displacement; the 150 mm threshold separates moderate from large displacement categories. [WEB:https://www.icoldchile.cl/boletines/148.pdf][WEB:https://escholarship.org/content/qt1qm5j970/qt1qm5j970_noSplash_ed01377a63352fbbf12ee273d2ba5f81.pdf] ^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: ICOLD Bulletin 148 return periods and the Stewart-Blake-Hollingsworth threshold displacements are sourced from publicly available documents. The specific statement that ICOLD recognized Newmark analysis as state of practice since 1989 is supported by secondary references in the web search results, though the original 1989 ICOLD bulletin was not directly consulted.]

The transition to fully performance-based selection of the seismic coefficient is exemplified by the work of Bray and Travasarou (2009) and Macedo and Candia (2020). Bray and Travasarou proposed a rational basis for selecting the pseudo-static coefficient by inverting their simplified displacement model: the engineer specifies a project-specific allowable displacement $D_n^{\star}$ and solves for the yield acceleration $k_y$ that controls the probability of exceeding that threshold, thereby eliminating the subjectivity inherent in prescriptive coefficient selection. Macedo and Candia extended this approach through a probabilistic framework grounded in seismically induced displacement hazard curves, yielding pseudo-static coefficient estimates that are consistent with the allowable displacement level, the properties of the sliding mass, the seismic demand at the site, and the hazard design level. Both procedures represent a significant advance over earlier prescriptive approaches in that they integrate site-specific hazard, slope dynamic properties, and explicit displacement performance objectives into a unified probabilistic framework. [DOI:10.1061/(ASCE)GT.1943-5606.0000012][DOI:10.1016/j.soildyn.2020.106109] ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The Bray-Travasarou (2009) pseudo-static coefficient procedure and the Macedo-Candia (2020) displacement hazard curve approach are published in peer-reviewed ASCE and Elsevier journals with verifiable DOIs. The characterization of their methodologies is consistent with the abstracts and descriptions retrieved from web search.]

In a retrospective review of seismic slope stability methods, Jibson (2011) observed that sliding-block analysis provides the greatest practical utility among available approaches: it is substantially simpler to apply than stress-deformation analysis while yielding significantly more informative results than pseudo-static analysis alone. This assessment reinforces the contemporary practice of calibrating the pseudo-static coefficient against displacement-based performance objectives rather than relying on prescriptive fractions of peak ground acceleration. The methodology adopted in this work follows this contemporary paradigm, employing an ensemble of Newmark displacement models to derive $k_{\max}$ as the yield acceleration that satisfies explicit exceedance-probability criteria at each hazard level. [DOI:10.1016/j.enggeo.2011.09.002][KB:pbkmax.qmd] ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: Jibson's 2011 retrospective review is a peer-reviewed publication in Engineering Geology with a verifiable DOI. The connection to the adopted methodology is supported by the formal kmax definition in pbkmax.qmd.]

## Newmark Displacement Analysis Methods

The Newmark sliding-block approach employed in this work conceptualizes the potential sliding mass as either a rigid block or a flexible (compliant) block with a yield acceleration $k_y = a_y / g$. When the ground acceleration exceeds $k_y$, the mass experiences incremental downslope movement, and the total permanent displacement $D$ accumulates over the course of shaking. The response is characterized probabilistically using empirical or semi-empirical relationships that express the predicted displacement in lognormal space. All models used in this framework follow the general form: [KB:newmark.qmd] ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The general Newmark framework description and the lognormal formulation are directly stated in newmark.qmd, which provides the overarching displacement model specification.]

$$\ln D = \mu_{\ln D}(\mathrm{IM}, k_y, T_n, M_w) + \epsilon \, \sigma_{\ln D}$$

In this expression, $\mathrm{IM}$ denotes the set of ground-motion intensity measures specific to each model (e.g., $\mathrm{PGA}$, $S_a$ at a reference period, Arias intensity $\mathrm{AI}$, peak ground velocity $\mathrm{PGV}$), $T_n$ is the fundamental period of the sliding mass, and $M_w$ is the earthquake moment magnitude where included by the model. The deterministic term $\mu_{\ln D}$ is the model-predicted mean displacement in natural-log space, and $\sigma_{\ln D}$ is the associated standard deviation. The stochastic term $\epsilon$ is a standard normal variate accounting for record-to-record variability not captured by the regression. Ground-motion intensity measures are obtained from the site hazard results; where the hazard is derived from a general ground-motion prediction equation or site-specific response analysis, spectral accelerations are evaluated at the periods required by each model, typically at $S_a(\alpha T_n)$ with $\alpha$ in the range 1.3 to 1.5. [KB:newmark.qmd] ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The parameter definitions, the role of the stochastic term, and the spectral-acceleration evaluation convention are explicitly documented in newmark.qmd.]

The rigid-block idealization treats the sliding mass as an infinitely stiff body for which dynamic amplification within the mass is negligible and the response is governed entirely by the base excitation. Four rigid-block models are included in the ensemble. The Jibson (2007) [@Jibson2007] model uses Arias intensity $\mathrm{AI}$ and the ratio $k_y / \mathrm{PGA}$ as predictors, capturing duration effects through the cumulative energy content of the ground motion. The Saygili and Rathje (2008) [@SaygiliRathje2008] multi-parameter model incorporates both $\mathrm{AI}$ and $\mathrm{PGA}$ simultaneously. Two earlier formulations - Ambraseys and Menu (1988) [@AmbraseysMenu1988] and Yegian et al. (1991) [@Yegian1991] - express displacement solely as a function of the acceleration ratio $r = k_y / \mathrm{PGA}$, reflecting the foundational Newmark assumption that slide strength relative to peak ground acceleration is the primary controlling variable. [KB:newmark_model.qmd] ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The classification of rigid-block models and their respective input parameters are transcribed from newmark_model.qmd, which provides the complete functional forms for each model. The cited citekeys are preserved from the KB source.]

The flexible-block (compliant) idealization accounts for the dynamic response of the sliding mass by incorporating the fundamental period $T_n$ and spectral acceleration $S_a$ evaluated at a period proportional to $T_n$. Three flexible-block models are included. The Bray and Travasarou (2007) [@BrayTravasarou2007] model evaluates $S_a$ at $1.5 \, T_n$ and depends on $k_y$, $T_n$, and earthquake magnitude $M_w$. Bray and Macedo (2017) [@BrayMacedo2017] developed a flexible-block formulation calibrated specifically for subduction-zone earthquakes, using $S_a$ at $T_n$ with piecewise coefficients that distinguish short-period ($T_n < 0.1$ s) from longer-period sliding masses. Bray and Macedo (2019) [@BrayMacedo2019] extended the framework to shallow-crustal earthquakes, retaining the same functional structure but with coefficients recalibrated against a larger and more recent strong-motion dataset; spectral acceleration is evaluated at $1.3 \, T_n$, and an additional correction applies for high peak ground velocities ($\mathrm{PGV} > 115$ cm/s). All three flexible-block models include an explicit dependence on $M_w$ to capture the influence of earthquake duration on accumulated displacement. [KB:newmark_model.qmd] ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: All flexible-block model descriptions, including the specific spectral-acceleration evaluation periods, piecewise coefficient structure, and PGV correction, are directly documented in newmark_model.qmd. The cited citekeys are preserved from the KB source.]

## Appendix - Newmark Displacement Equations

This appendix presents the complete predictive equations for each Newmark displacement model included in the ensemble. All models express the mean log-displacement $\mu_{\ln D}$ as a function of ground-motion intensity measures and slope parameters, with associated dispersion $\sigma_{\ln D}$ in natural-log space. The following notation applies throughout: $D$ denotes permanent displacement in centimeters; $k_y = a_y / g$ is the dimensionless yield-acceleration ratio; $\mathrm{PGA}$ is the peak ground acceleration in units of $g$; $\mathrm{PGV}$ is the peak ground velocity in cm/s; $\mathrm{AI}$ is Arias intensity in m/s; $T_n$ is the fundamental period of the sliding mass in seconds; $S_a$ is the spectral acceleration in units of $g$ evaluated at a model-specific period; and $M_w$ is the moment magnitude. All dispersions $\sigma$ are one-standard-deviation values in natural-log space unless stated otherwise. [KB:newmark_model.qmd] ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The notation summary and units are directly transcribed from the notation block at the head of the appendix in newmark_model.qmd.]

### Bray and Macedo (2017) - Flexible-Block, Subduction Earthquakes

The Bray and Macedo (2017) [@BrayMacedo2017] model was calibrated for subduction-zone interface and intraslab earthquakes under a flexible-block idealization. The mean log-displacement is $\mu_{\ln D} = a_0 + a_1 \ln S_a + a_2 (\ln S_a)^2$, where $S_a$ is the spectral acceleration at the fundamental period $T_n$ and the regression coefficients are: [KB:newmark_model.qmd] ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The functional form and all numerical coefficients are transcribed directly from newmark_model.qmd.]

$$\begin{aligned} a_0 &= \begin{cases} -5.864 + 0.550\,M_w - 9.421\,T_n - 3.353\ln k_y - 0.390(\ln k_y)^2, & T_n < 0.1 \\ -6.896 + 0.550\,M_w + 3.081\,T_n - 0.803\,T_n^{2} - 3.353\ln k_y - 0.390(\ln k_y)^2, & T_n \geq 0.1 \end{cases} \\ a_1 &= 3.060 + 0.538\ln k_y, \qquad a_2 = -0.225. \end{aligned}$$

The associated dispersion is $\sigma_{\ln D} = 0.73$. The model is applicable to compliant slope systems with both planar and non-planar failure surfaces subjected to subduction-zone ground motions. [KB:newmark_model.qmd] ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The dispersion value and applicability description are taken directly from newmark_model.qmd. The model type (subduction earthquakes) is stated explicitly in the KB heading.]

### Bray and Macedo (2019) - Flexible-Block, Shallow-Crustal Earthquakes

The Bray and Macedo (2019) [@BrayMacedo2019] model employs the same functional structure as the 2017 formulation but with coefficients recalibrated for shallow-crustal earthquakes along active plate margins. The spectral acceleration is evaluated at $1.3\,T_n$, and an additional correction applies when peak ground velocity exceeds 115 cm/s. The regression coefficients are: [KB:newmark_model.qmd] ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The functional form, spectral-acceleration period, PGV threshold, and all numerical coefficients are transcribed directly from newmark_model.qmd.]

$$\begin{aligned} a_0 &= \begin{cases} -4.551 + 0.607\,M_w - 9.690\,T_n - 2.491\ln k_y - 0.245(\ln k_y)^2, & T_n < 0.1 \\ -5.894 + 0.607\,M_w + 3.152\,T_n - 0.910\,T_n^{2} - 2.491\ln k_y - 0.245(\ln k_y)^2, & T_n \geq 0.1 \end{cases} \\ &\quad + \begin{cases} 0, & \mathrm{PGV} \leq 115\;\text{cm/s} \\ \ln \mathrm{PGV} - 4.75, & \mathrm{PGV} > 115\;\text{cm/s} \end{cases} \\ a_1 &= 2.703 + 0.344\ln k_y, \qquad a_2 = -0.089. \end{aligned}$$

The associated dispersion is $\sigma_{\ln D} = 0.74$. The model was calibrated against a large dataset of shallow-crustal earthquake recordings and is applicable to flexible slope systems with both planar and non-planar failure surfaces. If $\mathrm{PGV}$ is not directly available, it may be estimated from $\mathrm{PGA}$. [KB:newmark_model.qmd] ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: All coefficients and the PGV estimation note are taken from newmark_model.qmd. The dispersion value matches the KB source.]

### Bray and Travasarou (2007) - Flexible-Block

The Bray and Travasarou (2007) [@BrayTravasarou2007] model is a flexible-block formulation that evaluates spectral acceleration at $1.5\,T_n$. The mean log-displacement is: [KB:newmark_model.qmd] ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The functional form is transcribed directly from newmark_model.qmd.]

$$\mu_{\ln D} = -1.10 - 2.83\ln k_y - 0.333(\ln k_y)^2 + 0.566\ln k_y\ln S_a + 3.04\ln S_a - 0.244(\ln S_a)^2 + 0.278(M_w - 7).$$

The associated dispersion is $\sigma_{\ln D} = 0.66$. This model was developed from coupled stick-slip analyses of flexible sliding masses subjected to a broad suite of recorded ground motions and is applicable to deep-seated and shallow failure surfaces in compliant slopes. [KB:newmark_model.qmd] ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The dispersion value and the characterization of the calibration basis (coupled stick-slip analyses) are consistent with newmark_model.qmd and the published reference.]

### Jibson (2007) - Rigid-Block

The Jibson (2007) [@Jibson2007] model is a rigid-block formulation that incorporates Arias intensity as a duration-sensitive predictor. The mean log-displacement is: [KB:newmark_model.qmd] ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The equation is transcribed directly from newmark_model.qmd, preserving the conversion factor between log-base-10 and natural-log space.]

$$\mu_{\ln D} = \left[0.561\log_{10}\mathrm{AI} - 3.833\log_{10}(k_y / \mathrm{PGA}) - 1.474\right] \ln 10.$$

The dispersion in $\log_{10}$ space is $\sigma_{\log_{10}D} = 0.616$, equivalent to $\sigma_{\ln D} = 0.616 \ln 10$. Arias intensity $\mathrm{AI}$ is expressed in m/s and may be estimated from $\mathrm{PGA}$ when direct measurements are unavailable. The model was calibrated against rigid-block analyses of recorded accelerograms and is applicable to shallow, translational failure surfaces where the sliding mass may be reasonably approximated as infinitely stiff. [KB:newmark_model.qmd] ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The dispersion value, unit convention, and applicability statement are directly supported by newmark_model.qmd.]

### Saygili and Rathje (2008) - Rigid-Block, Multi-Parameter

The Saygili and Rathje (2008) [@SaygiliRathje2008] multi-parameter rigid-block model introduces both Arias intensity and peak ground acceleration as simultaneous predictors. The mean log-displacement is: [KB:newmark_model.qmd] ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The equation and variable definitions are transcribed directly from newmark_model.qmd.]

$$\mu_{\ln D} = 2.39 - 5.24\,r - 18.78\,r^{2} + 42.01\,r^{3} - 29.15\,r^{4} - 1.56\ln \mathrm{PGA} + 1.38\ln \mathrm{AI}, \qquad r = k_y / \mathrm{PGA}.$$

The dispersion is amplitude-dependent: $\sigma_{\ln D} = 0.46 + 0.56\,r$, increasing as the ratio of yield acceleration to peak ground acceleration grows. This behavior reflects larger prediction uncertainty for slopes operating closer to their yield threshold. The model was calibrated against a large suite of rigid-block Newmark analyses and is applicable to stiff, shallow failure surfaces. [KB:newmark_model.qmd] ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The variable dispersion formula and its physical interpretation are directly from newmark_model.qmd. The polynomial coefficients match the KB source exactly.]

### Ambraseys and Menu (1988) - Rigid-Block

The Ambraseys and Menu (1988) [@AmbraseysMenu1988] model is a classical rigid-block formulation that depends solely on the acceleration ratio $r = k_y / \mathrm{PGA}$. ## Ensemble Numerical Model

The numerical model adopted in this work computes Newmark-type permanent displacements through a weighted ensemble of the seven individual displacement models documented in the preceding appendix. For each seismic scenario and realization, permanent displacements are computed across all models - three flexible-block formulations (Bray and Macedo 2017 [@BrayMacedo2017], Bray and Macedo 2019 [@BrayMacedo2019], Bray and Travasarou 2007 [@BrayTravasarou2007]) and four rigid-block formulations (Jibson 2007 [@Jibson2007], Saygili and Rathje 2008 [@SaygiliRathje2008], Ambraseys and Menu 1988 [@AmbraseysMenu1988], Yegian et al. 1991 [@Yegian1991]) - and the ensemble prediction is formed as a weighted sum of the individual model contributions. This approach preserves model-to-model epistemic uncertainty by explicitly retaining the full suite of available predictive relationships rather than selecting a single preferred model. No predictive models were excluded from the ensemble. [KB:newmark.qmd][KB:uncertainty_model.qmd] ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The ensemble structure and the statement that no models were excluded are directly stated in newmark.qmd. The list of seven models matches the complete set documented in newmark_model.qmd.]

The weighting strategy assigns greater weight to more recent models on the basis that these formulations were calibrated against larger, more comprehensive, and higher-quality ground-motion datasets. The flexible-block models by Bray and Macedo (2017, 2019) and Bray and Travasarou (2007), which incorporate thousands of strong-motion recordings and account for site-specific dynamic parameters including fundamental period and spectral acceleration, receive higher weights than earlier rigid-block formulations. The Jibson (2007) and Saygili and Rathje (2008) models, which introduced duration-sensitive intensity measures to the rigid-block framework, receive intermediate weights. The Ambraseys and Menu (1988) and Yegian et al. (1991) models, calibrated against smaller and older datasets, receive the lowest weights in the ensemble. Logic-tree weights are assigned before aggregation, thereby maintaining explicit separation between epistemic model uncertainty and aleatory ground-motion variability throughout the simulation. [KB:newmark.qmd][KB:uncertainty_model.qmd] ^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The general principle that logic-tree weights are assigned before aggregation and that more recent models are favored is supported by newmark.qmd and uncertainty_model.qmd. However, the specific statement about relative weight magnitudes (higher, intermediate, lowest) is an inference from the recency-based weighting rationale described in the KB; explicit numerical weights are not documented in the KB sources.]

The total distribution of predicted displacements is obtained by convolving the empirical variability in input ground-motion parameters with the conditional lognormal scatter from each displacement model. For each realization, the simulation proceeds as follows: a set of correlated reference-rock spectral ordinates is drawn from the empirical hazard quantile functions, preserving inter-period correlation via a Gaussian copula with the Baker and Jayaram (2009) [@BakerJayaram2009] exponential decay model; for each required oscillator period, an independent site-amplification factor is drawn from its lognormal distribution with parameters determined by the site-response model [@Stewart2020][@Hashash2020]; the site-amplified intensity measures are computed; the deterministic mean displacement $\mu_{\ln D}$ is calculated from each model's regression; and the model's dispersion $\sigma_{\ln D}$ is applied with an independent standard normal deviate $\epsilon$. Quantiles of interest, including the median, 84th, and 95th percentile displacements, are computed directly from the assembled ensemble distribution across all models and realizations. The resulting displacement estimates feed into the performance-based seismic coefficient computation, where $k_{\max}$ is determined as the yield acceleration that controls the exceedance probability for the prescribed displacement threshold at each hazard level. [KB:uncertainty_model.qmd][KB:newmark.qmd][KB:pbkmax.qmd][KB:site_model.qmd] ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The Monte Carlo simulation procedure, including the Gaussian copula for inter-period correlation, the Baker-Jayaram decay model, the lognormal site-amplification sampling, and the final kmax computation, are described in detail across uncertainty_model.qmd, newmark.qmd, site_model.qmd, and pbkmax.qmd. All procedural steps are directly traceable to these KB sources.]
