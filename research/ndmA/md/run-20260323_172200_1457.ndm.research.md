# Horizontal Seismic Coefficient Estimation for Slopes: Performance-Based Framework

---

## SLOT 1: Problem Introduction - Performance-Based Seismic Coefficient Selection

The selection of a horizontal seismic coefficient $k_h$ for pseudo-static limit-equilibrium slope stability analysis constitutes a fundamental design decision in performance-based earthquake engineering of earth structures. The coefficient represents, in a time-independent sense, the equivalent horizontal acceleration imposed on the sliding mass during seismic loading. Its value directly governs the computed factor of safety, yet prescriptive approaches that assign fixed fractions of the free-field peak ground acceleration (PGA) without reference to consequence severity or slope mechanics lack a defensible technical basis for contemporary engineering practice. [KB:newmark.qmd]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The general concept of pseudo-static seismic coefficient and its role in limit-equilibrium analysis is well established in geotechnical earthquake engineering; the KB confirms the Newmark framework and the connection between seismic coefficient and displacement prediction.]

The performance-based framework addresses this gap by calibrating $k_h$ to two interrelated groups of parameters. The first group establishes the target ground-motion intensity level, expressed as an annual exceedance probability (AEP) that is coupled to the consequence category of the structure. Earth structures with higher consequence ratings - such as tailings storage facilities (TSFs) and waste rock dumps (WRDs) operating under regulatory requirements for extreme seismic events - are assigned lower AEPs. In the present framework, service levels span AEPs from $1/100$ per annum for routine operational loading to $1/10{,}000$ per annum for the most severe design scenario. [KB:pbkmax.qmd]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The KB (pbkmax.qmd) explicitly states that performance-based seismic coefficients are reported across service levels with AEPs from 1/100 to 1/10,000 for TSF and WRD scenarios, supporting both the AEP range and the consequence-based framing.]

The second group characterizes the physical and dynamic properties of the slope itself. The yield acceleration ratio $k_y = a_y/g$ - the minimum horizontal acceleration normalized by gravity required to initiate sliding along a given failure surface - is established from geotechnical analysis of the failure mechanism and material shear strength. The fundamental period of the sliding mass $T_n$ governs which portion of the spectral demand is relevant to displacement generation; its value depends on the shear stiffness of the fill or soil and on the geometric extent of the potential sliding body. Together, $k_y$ and $T_n$ define the mechanical response of the slope to a given seismic excitation. [KB:newmark.qmd][KB:newmark_model.qmd]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The KB defines $k_y$, $T_n$, and their roles as inputs to the displacement models explicitly in both newmark.qmd and newmark_model.qmd, supporting the description of these parameters and their physical interpretation.]

The methodology connects these two parameter groups through Newmark sliding-block displacement analysis. Empirical and semi-empirical regression models translate site-specific ground-motion intensity measures - including PGA, spectral acceleration $S_a(T)$, peak ground velocity (PGV), and Arias intensity (AI) - into probability distributions of permanent residual displacement. The performance-based seismic coefficient $k_{max}$ is then defined as the minimum value of $k_y$ for which the probability that predicted displacement exceeds the allowable threshold $D_n^*$ is controlled at the target exceedance probability $p$. This formulation produces a design coefficient that is internally consistent with both the site seismic hazard and the prescribed structural performance objective. [KB:pbkmax.qmd][KB:newmark.qmd]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The KB (pbkmax.qmd) defines the performance-based coefficient through the infimum relation $k_{max}(p|D_n^*) = \inf\{k_y : P[D_n(k_y) > D_n^*] \leq p\}$, directly supporting this description. The linkage to intensity measures is confirmed in newmark.qmd.]

Seismic slope stability assessment in contemporary engineering practice has evolved from purely prescriptive approaches toward performance-based methodologies that explicitly account for site-specific hazard and project-specific deformation tolerance. The following survey covers the principal normative documents, published guidelines, and professional recommendations governing seismic stability assessment of slopes and embankments through limit-equilibrium and displacement-based methods.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: This introductory framing reflects a well-documented evolution in the geotechnical engineering literature, corroborated by the web search results identifying Bray & Travasarou (2009) and Macedo et al. ### Regulatory and Code-Based Frameworks

The U.S. Army Corps of Engineers (USACE) Engineer Manual EM 1110-2-1902 and Engineer Regulation ER 1110-2-1806 establish the seismic design and evaluation requirements for civil works including earth embankments [WEB:https://www.publications.usace.army.mil/Portals/76/Publications/EngineerManuals/EM_1110-2-1902.pdf][WEB:https://www.publications.usace.army.mil/Portals/76/Publications/EngineerRegulations/ER_1110-2-1806.pdf]. ER 1110-2-1806 defines the Operating Basis Earthquake (OBE) as the event with a 50% probability of exceedance in 100 years (mean return period of approximately 144 years) and the Maximum Design Earthquake (MDE) at lower AEPs. Limit-equilibrium stability analysis under these ground-motion levels, with minimum factors of safety of 1.1 to 1.5 depending on the load condition, constitutes the primary design check for earth embankments under USACE practice.^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The OBE definition (50% in 100 years) and the MDE concept are confirmed from the web search result referencing ER 1110-2-1806. Eurocode 8 (EN 1998-5) prescribes a pseudo-static horizontal seismic coefficient tied to the design peak ground acceleration at the site and the slope consequence class. For slopes where the critical acceleration significantly exceeds the design PGA, Eurocode 8 permits Newmark displacement calculations as an alternative to pseudo-static analysis, recognizing that deformation-based performance verification is more rational for ductile earth structures [WEB:https://help.geostru.eu/slope/en/eurocodice_8.htm]. Most code-based recommendations restrict the pseudo-static method to conditions where the pseudo-static coefficient does not exceed approximately 0.3, above which dynamic amplification effects become non-negligible.^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The Eurocode 8 description is consistent with web search results referencing EN 1998-5 and the Geostru documentation. The International Commission on Large Dams (ICOLD) classifies seismic stability assessment methods into three tiers: simplified pseudo-static methods, simplified dynamic methods (Newmark sliding-block), and full dynamic analysis using finite element or finite difference time-domain formulations. For structures of moderate-to-high consequence, ICOLD guidelines endorse the Newmark displacement approach as the preferred simplified dynamic method [WEB:https://www.resolutionmineeis.us/sites/default/files/references/ancold-2012.pdf]. This recommendation is consistent with Eurocode 8 and is adopted in practice for dam safety evaluations where deformation is the governing performance parameter.^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The three-tier ICOLD classification and endorsement of Newmark methods is confirmed from the web search result referencing ICOLD and Eurocode 8 in the context of tailings dam stability. ### Performance-Based Professional Guidance

The foundational performance-based framework for pseudo-static seismic coefficient selection was advanced by Bray and Travasarou (2007, 2009), who demonstrated that the seismic coefficient should be derived by inverting the displacement-based design criterion rather than by applying an arbitrary fraction of the free-field PGA [DOI:10.1061/(ASCE)GT.1943-5606.0000012]. Their procedure links $k_h$ to the project-specific allowable displacement, the fundamental period $T_n$ of the sliding mass, and the site spectral demand, yielding coefficients that in many practical cases amount to 25% to 50% of the free-field PGA for slopes that can accommodate 1-2 inches (25-50 mm) of permanent seismic displacement.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The Bray & Travasarou (2009) procedure and the 25-50% PGA finding are confirmed from multiple web search results including the ASCE library DOI and the ResearchGate abstract. The DOI:10.1061/(ASCE)GT.1943-5606.0000012 corresponds to the JGGE 2009 publication directly.]

Macedo et al. (2018) extended the Bray-Travasarou framework into a fully probabilistic performance-based earthquake engineering (PBEE) procedure, integrating PSHA-derived seismic hazard with Newmark displacement models to produce hazard-consistent estimates of seismic slope performance across a range of return periods [DOI:10.1193/122516EQS251M]. A subsequent probabilistic assessment by Macedo et al. (2020) further examined the sensitivity of the pseudo-static coefficient to hazard level, slope period, and allowable displacement threshold, confirming that the performance-based approach yields coefficients systematically lower than those from prescriptive 50%-PGA rules for ductile slopes [WEB:https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0267726119309844].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The Macedo et al. (2018) paper DOI:10.1193/122516EQS251M is confirmed from the journals.sagepub.com URL returned by the web search. The 2020 ScienceDirect URL was also returned directly in the search results. Both citations are traceable.]

### Mining Sector Practice

For tailings storage facilities and waste rock dumps, regulatory guidance in Canada - including the Canadian Dam Safety Association (CDA) Dam Safety Guidelines and the Mining Association of Canada (MAC) guidelines for tailings facilities management - requires that seismic stability be assessed under consequence-category-specific design earthquake levels. Extreme-consequence structures are typically assigned design AEPs corresponding to return periods of 10,000 years or longer. Under this framework, pseudo-static analysis is considered a screening-level tool only; deformation-based methods, including Newmark displacement analysis, are required for final design verification of facilities in moderate-to-high seismicity regions [WEB:https://open.library.ubc.ca/soa/cIRcle/collections/59368/items/1.0314230].^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The CDA and MAC framing of consequence categories and the role of Newmark analysis as a final design tool for TSFs is consistent with the UBC Library search result referencing a thesis on seismic permanent displacements for TSFs and WRDs. Moderate confidence is appropriate.]

## SLOT 3: Newmark Displacement Analysis Methodologies - Rigid and Flexible Block Models

The Newmark sliding-block framework models a potentially unstable slope as a discrete mass that accumulates incremental downslope displacement whenever the ground acceleration exceeds the yield acceleration $k_y \cdot g$ of the sliding system. Permanent displacement $D$ is obtained by double integration of the ground acceleration time history over those intervals during which the driving inertial force exceeds the resisting frictional capacity. Two distinct physical idealizations of the sliding mass govern the selection of applicable displacement prediction models: the rigid-block model and the flexible-block (compliant) model. [KB:newmark.qmd]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The Newmark framework description, the definition of yield acceleration $k_y$, and the double-integration procedure are stated consistently in newmark.qmd, which is the primary KB source for this section.]

The rigid-block idealization treats the sliding mass as mechanically stiff, such that every point within the mass experiences the same acceleration as the base excitation at the failure surface. Under this assumption, the dynamic response is governed solely by the yield acceleration $k_y$ and the amplitude and frequency content of the base motion; internal deformation and resonance amplification are neglected. This idealization is physically appropriate for relatively thin, stiff, or shallow translational failure surfaces where the fundamental period of the sliding mass is short - generally $T_n < 0.1$ s - relative to the dominant periods of the ground motion. Ground-motion intensity measures suitable for rigid-block models include PGA, Arias intensity (AI), and PGV, which capture the amplitude, energy content, and duration of shaking without requiring spectral characterization of the sliding body. [KB:newmark_model.qmd]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The rigid-block idealization, its applicability condition ($T_n < 0.1$ s), and the relevant intensity measures (PGA, AI, PGV) are directly consistent with the model descriptions in newmark_model.qmd for the Ambraseys & Menu, Yegian, Jibson, and Saygili & Rathje models, which are categorized as rigid-block models.]

The following displacement prediction models are applicable to the rigid-block idealization: Ambraseys and Menu (1988), Yegian et al. (1991), Jibson (2007), and Saygili and Rathje (2008). The Jibson (2007) model was calibrated on 2270 strong-motion records from 30 worldwide earthquakes and incorporates Arias intensity as the primary predictor alongside the critical acceleration ratio $r = k_y/\mathrm{PGA}$, improving the capture of duration and energy content effects not fully represented by PGA alone [KB:newmark_model.qmd][WEB:https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0013795207000300]. The Saygili and Rathje (2008) model is a multi-parameter rigid-block formulation combining AI and PGA with a heteroscedastic dispersion structure that accounts for increasing prediction uncertainty as the slope approaches its critical condition.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The classification of these four models as rigid-block, their calibration bases, and their input requirements are confirmed in newmark_model.qmd. The Jibson 2007 calibration dataset size (2270 records from 30 earthquakes) was corroborated by the web search result referencing the Jibson 2007 regression study in Engineering Geology.]

The flexible-block (compliant) idealization recognizes that real embankments and fill structures possess finite stiffness and exhibit dynamic amplification of the base motion before that amplified signal drives sliding. The peak acceleration experienced at the centroid of the sliding mass exceeds the free-field PGA, and the relevant seismic demand is characterized by the spectral acceleration $S_a$ evaluated at a period near the fundamental period of the sliding mass - typically at $\alpha T_n$ with $\alpha$ in the range 1.3 to 1.5, reflecting the period elongation associated with shear strain softening during strong shaking. This coupling between the dynamic site response of the embankment and the Newmark sliding computation is the defining feature of the flexible-block model class. [KB:newmark.qmd]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The flexible-block description, the $\alpha T_n$ spectral evaluation period with $\alpha \in [1.3, 1.5]$, and the concept of period elongation under strain softening are explicitly stated in newmark.qmd, supporting this paragraph directly.]

The flexible-block displacement prediction models applicable within this framework are Bray and Travasarou (2007) and Bray and Macedo (2017, 2019). All three incorporate $T_n$, spectral acceleration $S_a(\alpha T_n)$, and moment magnitude $M_w$ as explicit predictors, capturing both site amplification and ground-motion duration within a single regression. The Bray and Macedo (2017) and Bray and Macedo (2019) models are additionally differentiated by tectonic regime: the 2017 model is calibrated for subduction zone earthquakes, while the 2019 model targets shallow-crustal seismicity. The 2019 model incorporates a PGV correction term for near-fault velocity pulse conditions when PGV exceeds 115 cm/s. The distinction between tectonic regimes is of practical importance for sites exposed to both crustal and subduction seismic sources, as the spectral shape, duration, and amplitude scaling of subduction records differ substantially from those of shallow-crustal events. [KB:newmark_model.qmd]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The three flexible-block models (Bray & Travasarou 2007; Bray & Macedo 2017, 2019), their input parameters ($T_n$, $S_a$, $M_w$), the subduction/shallow-crustal distinction, and the PGV correction term are all explicitly documented in newmark_model.qmd. The information is internally consistent and unambiguous.]

The fundamental period $T_n$ is estimated from the geometry and shear stiffness of the embankment or fill. Shear modulus as a function of void ratio and confining stress is obtained from the empirical Ishihara (1997) formulation, and $T_n$ is updated to account for shear strain softening under the design seismic demand. The demarcation at $T_n = 0.1$ s separates the regime in which rigid-block models are applicable from that in which flexible-block models are required, and is respected explicitly in the coefficient expressions of both the Bray and Macedo (2017) and Bray and Macedo (2019) models, which employ piecewise coefficient definitions at this threshold. [KB:newmark_model.qmd]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The Ishihara (1997) shear modulus formulation and the $T_n = 0.1$ s demarcation are confirmed in newmark_model.qmd, including the piecewise coefficient expressions that change at $T_n = 0.1$ s in both the 2017 and 2019 Bray & Macedo models.]

## SLOT 5: Ensemble Numerical Model - Weighted Sum of Displacement Predictions

The seven displacement prediction models documented in the preceding appendix are integrated into a single ensemble through a weighted-sum approach. For each combination of input parameters and ground-motion realization, each model independently computes a log-space mean displacement $\mu_{\ln D}^{(i)}$ and applies its model-specific dispersion $\sigma_{\ln D}^{(i)}$ to generate an individual displacement realization $\ln D^{(i)}$. The ensemble prediction is assembled by aggregating individual model predictions according to a set of logic-tree weights $w_i$, with $\sum_i w_i = 1$. No model is excluded from the ensemble. [KB:newmark.qmd][KB:uncertainty_model.qmd]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The ensemble (weighted-sum) approach, the use of logic-tree weights, and the statement that no models are excluded are all confirmed in newmark.qmd. The uncertainty_model.qmd confirms that model-to-model epistemic uncertainty is retained via logic-tree weights aggregated over all models.]

The weighting scheme assigns proportionally greater influence to models calibrated on larger and more comprehensive ground-motion databases, reflecting the superior statistical robustness and broader applicability domain of more recent regression analyses. The Bray and Macedo (2017) and Bray and Macedo (2019) flexible-block models, each calibrated on extensive datasets of subduction and shallow-crustal records respectively incorporating a wider range of magnitude-distance-site combinations than earlier studies, receive higher weights in the ensemble relative to the Ambraseys and Menu (1988) and Yegian et al. (1991) models, which were developed from comparatively limited record sets. This weighting philosophy reflects the principle that models with broader empirical support and more explicit treatment of ground-motion characteristics should dominate the ensemble prediction. [KB:newmark_model.qmd][KB:newmark.qmd]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The principle that more recent models calibrated on larger datasets receive greater weight is stated directly in the task specification and is consistent with standard logic-tree weighting philosophy in seismic hazard and displacement analysis. The KB confirms the model suite and the relative comprehensiveness of the Bray & Macedo 2017/2019 calibration bases compared to the older Ambraseys & Menu and Yegian models.]

The ensemble framework serves two complementary objectives. First, it reduces sensitivity to the idiosyncratic features of any individual regression dataset or functional form, mitigating the risk that a single model's extrapolation behavior outside its calibration domain dominates the result. Second, it preserves the epistemic uncertainty associated with displacement model selection, representing it explicitly through the spread of model predictions rather than collapsing all candidates into a single best-estimate formula. This model-to-model epistemic component supplements the aleatory variability captured within each model by its standard deviation $\sigma_{\ln D}$. [KB:uncertainty_model.qmd]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The two roles of the ensemble (reducing sensitivity to individual model features and preserving epistemic uncertainty) are consistent with the uncertainty framework described in uncertainty_model.qmd, which distinguishes between aleatory variability (within-model $\sigma_{\ln D}$) and epistemic uncertainty (across-model logic-tree weights). This is standard practice in PBEE displacement analysis.]

Quantiles of the ensemble displacement distribution - including the median, 84th percentile, and 95th percentile - are computed directly from the assembled realizations across all models and ground-motion samples. The resulting distribution reflects the joint effect of ground-motion variability (sampled from PSHA hazard quantile functions), site amplification uncertainty (propagated through the lognormal amplification model), and displacement model uncertainty (represented by the weighted logic-tree ensemble). This combined distribution forms the statistical basis for the performance-based seismic coefficient calculation presented in the following section. [KB:newmark.qmd][KB:uncertainty_model.qmd]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The specific quantiles reported (median, 84th, 95th percentile) and the three sources of variability (ground-motion, site amplification, model uncertainty) are all confirmed in newmark.qmd and uncertainty_model.qmd. The uncertainty decomposition is consistent across both KB files.]

## SLOT 6: Seismic Coefficient Derivation from Target Residual Displacement

Once the ensemble model has established the statistical distribution of permanent displacement $D_n(k_y)$ as a function of the yield acceleration ratio, the performance-based seismic coefficient $k_{max}$ is determined by inverting a displacement performance criterion. Let $D_n^*$ denote the allowable residual displacement, specified as a project design criterion for permanent ground deformations under seismic loading. For a given yield acceleration ratio $k_y$, the exceedance probability is defined as [KB:pbkmax.qmd]:^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The framing of $k_{max}$ as the inversion of a displacement criterion and the definition of the exceedance probability $p(k_y) = P[D_n(k_y) > D_n^*]$ are taken directly from pbkmax.qmd, which presents this methodology as its primary content.]

$$p(k_y) = \mathbb{P}[D_n(k_y) > D_n^*]$$

This exceedance probability is a monotonically decreasing function of $k_y$: increasing the yield strength of the slope reduces the probability that predicted displacement exceeds any fixed threshold. The performance-based seismic coefficient $k_{max}$ is accordingly defined as the infimum of $k_y$ that controls the exceedance probability at the target level $p$:^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The monotonicity argument and the infimum definition of $k_{max}$ are directly from pbkmax.qmd, which states this expression as the core design equation. The physical interpretation (higher $k_y$ reduces exceedance probability) is internally consistent with the Newmark framework.]

$$k_{max}(p\,|\,D_n^*) = \inf\!\left\{k_y : \mathbb{P}[D_n(k_y) > D_n^*] \leq p\right\}$$

This expression directly links the design coefficient to three independently specified quantities: the target exceedance probability $p$ (reflecting the consequence category and return period assigned to the structure), the allowable displacement $D_n^*$ (reflecting the tolerable permanent deformation of the slope), and the full statistical characterization of displacement through the ensemble model. The random variable $D_n(k_y)$ incorporates the combined variability in ground-motion intensity measures, site amplification, and displacement model prediction, ensuring that the resulting $k_{max}$ is hazard-consistent. The inversion is implemented numerically by evaluating the displacement exceedance probability over a sweep of candidate $k_y$ values. For each candidate, the ensemble model generates displacement realizations incorporating variability from ground-motion intensity measures (drawn from PSHA hazard quantile functions), site amplification (drawn from the lognormal amplification factor distribution), and model dispersion (drawn from each model's $\sigma_{\ln D}$ through a standard normal variate $\epsilon$). The fraction of realizations exceeding $D_n^*$ yields an empirical estimate of $p(k_y)$. The performance coefficient $k_{max}$ is identified as the smallest $k_y$ for which this empirical fraction falls at or below $p$. To maintain numerical stability in logarithmic operations, displacement realizations below a minimum threshold are floored at $10^{-16}$ m prior to processing.
