# Technical Memorandum: Performance-Based Horizontal Seismic Coefficient Estimation for Slope Stability Analysis

---

## 1. Problem Introduction - Performance-Based Seismic Coefficient Selection

Pseudo-static slope stability analysis remains the standard method in geotechnical engineering practice for evaluating the seismic performance of natural slopes, earth embankments, tailings storage facilities, and waste rock dumps. In this method, the dynamic effect of an earthquake is represented as an equivalent static horizontal force equal to the product of a dimensionless seismic coefficient $k_h$ and the weight $W$ of the potentially sliding mass. A limit-equilibrium analysis is then conducted to compute the factor of safety under the combined action of gravity and the pseudo-static inertial force. The validity of this approach depends critically on the selection of an appropriate value for $k_h$, which must faithfully represent the seismic demand relevant to the structure's performance objective rather than being assigned by precedent or convention. [KB:newmark.qmd]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The pseudo-static limit-equilibrium method and the role of k_h are fundamental, well-established concepts in geotechnical earthquake engineering. The characterization is fully consistent with the KB and corroborated by multiple authoritative external references including USACE EM 1110-2-1902 and NCHRP Report 611.]

The performance-based framework described in this memorandum calibrates $k_h$ to two interrelated groups of parameters. The first group defines the target level of seismic hazard, expressed as an annual exceedance probability or an equivalent return period, which is itself determined by the consequence class associated with slope failure. Facilities for which failure would produce severe or irreversible consequences - such as tailings impoundments situated above populated areas or sensitive receiving environments - are assigned more stringent hazard levels corresponding to lower annual exceedance probabilities (longer return periods), resulting in more intense ground-motion demands and correspondingly higher design coefficients. The second group encompasses the physical and dynamic characteristics of the slope: the yield (critical) acceleration ratio $k_y = a_y / g$, which is the minimum normalized ground acceleration required to initiate sliding along the critical failure surface; the shear strength parameters governing that surface; and the fundamental period $T_n$ of the potentially sliding mass, which governs the dynamic response of the deformable block to the input motion. Together, these two groups of parameters determine both the seismic demand imposed on the slope and the resistance available to oppose it, and they jointly govern the permanent co-seismic displacement that would accumulate whenever the inertial demand exceeds the yield threshold. [KB:newmark.qmd][KB:pbkmax.qmd]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The two-parameter calibration framework, linking consequence-driven hazard levels and slope dynamic characteristics to the design seismic coefficient, is directly described in the KB sources and is consistent with established performance-based design principles. The connection between consequence severity, exceedance probability, and design intensity is corroborated by ICOLD Bulletin 148 and NCHRP Report 611.]

The link between these parameters and the design coefficient $k_h$ is established through the Newmark sliding-block displacement model, which translates a prescribed combination of ground-motion intensity measures and slope properties into a probabilistic estimate of permanent co-seismic displacement. By evaluating the ensemble-predicted displacement distribution as a function of the trial yield acceleration and comparing it against a project-specific allowable displacement threshold $D_n^{\star}$, the framework identifies the minimum $k_y$ - and hence the design seismic coefficient $k_{\max}$ - for which the probability of exceeding the allowable displacement is held below a specified target. This inversion procedure yields a seismic coefficient that is anchored to site-specific hazard, slope dynamics, and an explicitly stated displacement performance objective, rather than being assigned as a uniform fraction of peak ground acceleration. [KB:newmark.qmd][KB:pbkmax.qmd]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The inversion procedure relating the ensemble Newmark displacement distribution to k_max is directly and unambiguously described in pbkmax.qmd. The characterization of k_max as a performance-based inversion rather than a fixed fraction of PGA is consistent with both KB sources and with the external literature on Bray and Travasarou (2009).]

## 2. Current State of Practice - Standards, Guidelines, and Recommendations

The seismic design of slopes, embankments, and earth dams has historically relied on the pseudo-static limit-equilibrium method, with the horizontal seismic coefficient assigned as a fixed fraction of the regional design peak ground acceleration - commonly 0.10g to 0.15g for many decades - or determined by engineering judgment. Over the past three decades, the profession has progressively transitioned toward performance-based procedures in which $k_h$ is linked to an explicit, project-specific displacement tolerance and a probabilistic hazard level reflecting the consequence class of the structure. This transition has been driven by advances in ground-motion databases, empirical displacement prediction models, and probabilistic hazard analysis, which together make it feasible to define seismic design criteria in terms of quantified performance outcomes rather than nominal force levels. The U.S. Army Corps of Engineers provides foundational guidance through Engineer Manual EM 1110-2-1902 (Slope Stability, 2003), which endorses pseudo-static limit-equilibrium analysis as one of several acceptable methods and discusses the Newmark sliding-block approach for estimating permanent seismic deformation. The companion manual EM 1110-2-2100 (Stability of Concrete Structures, 2005) establishes stability criteria applicable to other engineered structures on or adjacent to slopes. Neither manual prescribes a universal pseudo-static coefficient value; instead, both direct the engineer to consider site-specific seismic hazard and acceptable performance criteria when selecting $k_h$, establishing the principle that the coefficient selection must reflect project-specific conditions. Residual displacement thresholds are typically in the range of 100 mm to 300 mm for operational performance objectives and may be substantially lower for critical or consequence-category facilities. ## 3. Newmark Displacement Analysis Methodologies - Rigid and Flexible Block Models

The Newmark (1965) sliding-block method provides the physical and analytical foundation for all displacement prediction models employed in the present framework. The method conceptualizes the potentially sliding mass as a block resting on a frictional inclined surface. When the earthquake-induced ground acceleration exceeds the critical yield acceleration $k_y g$ - the minimum horizontal acceleration at which the driving forces along the failure surface equal the resisting forces - the block begins to slide downslope. Displacement accumulates incrementally as the double integral of the excess acceleration (the difference between the ground acceleration and $k_y g$) over each interval during which the ground acceleration exceeds the yield threshold, and the total permanent displacement $D$ represents the cumulative downslope movement over the full duration of the earthquake record. [KB:newmark.qmd]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The physical basis of the Newmark sliding-block method, including the yield acceleration concept and the double-integration procedure, is accurately described and fully consistent with the KB sources and with the foundational geotechnical literature.]

**Rigid-Block Idealization.** The rigid-block formulation treats the sliding mass as internally rigid: all points within the mass accelerate coherently with the base until the yield threshold is exceeded, whereupon the mass displaces as a unit. This idealization is physically appropriate when the sliding mass is stiff relative to the frequency content of the ground motion - specifically when the fundamental period $T_n$ of the mass is substantially shorter than the dominant period of the input - or when the failure surface is shallow and translational in character. The rigid-block model requires only the yield acceleration $k_y$ and one or more scalar ground-motion intensity measures, typically the peak ground acceleration ($\mathrm{PGA}$), the Arias intensity ($\mathrm{AI}$, proportional to the integral of the squared acceleration over time), or the ratio $r = k_y / \mathrm{PGA}$, making it computationally efficient and broadly applicable for preliminary assessments and screening-level analyses. The rigid-block models employed in the present ensemble are those of Ambraseys and Menu [@AmbraseysMenu1988], Yegian, Marciano, and Ghahraman [@Yegian1991], Jibson [@Jibson2007], and Saygili and Rathje [@SaygiliRathje2008]. [KB:newmark_model.qmd]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The rigid-block idealization and the conditions under which it is appropriate are well-established in the geotechnical earthquake engineering literature and are consistent with the KB. The enumeration of rigid-block models is drawn directly from newmark_model.qmd, which classifies each model explicitly as a "Rigid-Block Model."]

**Flexible-Block Idealization.** The flexible-block (or compliant sliding-mass) formulation extends the Newmark framework to account for the dynamic response of the deformable sliding mass. In this idealization, the mass has finite stiffness characterized by its fundamental period $T_n$, and the seismic demand experienced at the failure surface reflects both the input ground motion and the dynamic amplification or de-amplification within the mass. Spectral acceleration evaluated at a period equal to a constant multiple of $T_n$ - typically $1.3 T_n$ to $1.5 T_n$, reflecting the period elongation associated with inelastic yielding - serves as the primary ground-motion intensity measure. Moment magnitude $M_w$ is also included as a predictor in flexible-block formulations because it governs the duration of strong shaking, which exerts a controlling influence on cumulative displacement for masses with non-negligible periods. The flexible-block idealization is physically appropriate for deep-seated rotational slides, engineered embankments, and thick earth fills where the sliding mass is sufficiently thick and flexible to exhibit measurable dynamic response. The flexible-block models employed in the ensemble are those of Bray and Travasarou [@BrayTravasarou2007], applicable to general tectonic settings, and the tectonic-regime-specific models of Bray and Macedo for subduction-zone earthquakes [@BrayMacedo2017] and shallow-crustal earthquakes [@BrayMacedo2019]. [KB:newmark_model.qmd]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The flexible-block idealization, the use of spectral acceleration at a multiple of T_n, and the role of magnitude in duration-sensitive formulations are well-described in the KB. The models enumerated are explicitly classified as "Flexible-Block (compliant) Model" in newmark_model.qmd. The evaluation period descriptions (1.3 T_n for BM2019, 1.5 T_n for BT2007) are stated explicitly in the KB.]

All predictive displacement models within this framework, regardless of block idealization, are cast in the following general lognormal regression form: [KB:newmark.qmd]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The general lognormal framework for all displacement models is stated directly and unambiguously in newmark.qmd. The lead-in statement is accurate and consistent with all seven model equations documented in the KB.]

$$
\ln D = \mu_{\ln D}(\mathrm{IM},\, k_y,\, T_n,\, M_w) + \epsilon\,\sigma_{\ln D}
$$

where $D$ is the predicted permanent displacement in centimeters, $\mathrm{IM}$ denotes the set of ground-motion intensity measures specific to each model (e.g., $\mathrm{PGA}$, spectral acceleration $S_a$ at a model-specific period, Arias intensity $\mathrm{AI}$, or peak ground velocity $\mathrm{PGV}$), $\mu_{\ln D}$ is the model-predicted mean in natural-log space, $\sigma_{\ln D}$ is the associated log-space standard deviation representing within-model aleatory variability, and $\epsilon$ is a standard normal random variate capturing the residual scatter not explained by the regression predictors. This common probabilistic structure allows all seven models to be integrated consistently within the ensemble simulation framework described in Section 5. [KB:newmark.qmd]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The parameter definitions and the probabilistic structure of the general displacement model are taken directly from newmark.qmd and are accurately rendered here. The enumeration of IM types is consistent with the specific model formulations in newmark_model.qmd.]

Ground-motion intensity measures required by the displacement models are obtained from the probabilistic seismic hazard analysis (PSHA) or site-specific response calculations. Spectral accelerations are evaluated at the model-specific reference periods, typically $S_a(\alpha T_n)$ with $\alpha$ in the range 1.3 to 1.5. When the required spectral ordinates are not available at the exact periods from the hazard output, logarithmic interpolation in period is applied. Where the site hazard is referenced to rock conditions, period-dependent site amplification factors are applied to translate reference-rock ground motions to site-specific values, with amplification model uncertainty propagated as a lognormal variable within the Monte Carlo simulation. [KB:newmark.qmd]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The procedure for obtaining ground-motion intensity measures from PSHA and applying logarithmic interpolation and site amplification factors is directly described in newmark.qmd. The description is accurate and internally consistent.]

## 5. Ensemble Numerical Model - Weighted Sum of Displacement Predictions

The displacement estimation framework employs a weighted ensemble that combines predictions from all seven Newmark displacement models documented in the appendix above. Rather than selecting a single displacement predictor, the ensemble approach retains epistemic uncertainty across the full suite of models by assigning each model a logic-tree weight and aggregating the resulting displacement realizations into a single, combined distribution. This structure is analogous to the multi-model logic-tree used in probabilistic seismic hazard analysis to represent uncertainty in ground-motion prediction equations: the weighted combination acknowledges that no single model constitutes the definitive predictor of sliding displacement and that the dispersion of predictions across the ensemble constitutes a meaningful component of the total uncertainty budget. The ensemble thereby captures both the within-model aleatory variability (represented by each model's $\sigma_{\ln D}$) and the between-model epistemic uncertainty (represented by the scatter of weighted predictions across the suite). [KB:newmark.qmd][KB:uncertainty_model.qmd]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The weighted ensemble approach and its analogy to the logic-tree treatment of epistemic uncertainty in PSHA are directly described in the KB sources, particularly uncertainty_model.qmd. The distinction between aleatory and epistemic components is consistent with the uncertainty framework in the KB.]

The assignment of logic-tree weights follows the principle that models calibrated against larger and more comprehensive ground-motion databases, and that incorporate a broader set of physically motivated predictors, should receive proportionally greater weight in the ensemble. The three flexible-block models of Bray and collaborators [@BrayTravasarou2007; @BrayMacedo2017; @BrayMacedo2019] and the multi-parameter rigid-block model of Saygili and Rathje [@SaygiliRathje2008] were all calibrated using datasets of 688 or more records from multiple tectonic settings and incorporate duration and spectral shape effects; these models accordingly receive higher weights. The earlier rigid-block formulations of Ambraseys and Menu [@AmbraseysMenu1988] (50 records, near-field only) and Yegian et al. [@Yegian1991] (probabilistic time-history integrations, polynomial in $r$ only) receive lower weights, reflecting their more restricted calibration bases. The Jibson (2007) [@Jibson2007] model, calibrated on 2,270 records but employing a simpler parameterization without period dependence, occupies an intermediate position. The specific numerical weight values are defined at the project level and encoded in the logic-tree specification, ensuring full transparency and traceability of the weighting scheme. ## 6. Seismic Coefficient Derivation from Target Residual Displacement

Once the ensemble displacement distribution $D_n(k_y)$ has been established as a function of the trial yield acceleration $k_y$ for a given seismic service level, the performance-based seismic coefficient $k_{\max}$ is obtained by inversion. The central question is: for a specified allowable displacement $D_n^{\star}$ and a target exceedance probability $p$, what is the minimum yield acceleration at which the ensemble-predicted displacement will exceed $D_n^{\star}$ with probability no greater than $p$? The quantity $k_{\max}$ is the answer to this question and constitutes the design seismic coefficient for input into the pseudo-static limit-equilibrium analysis. [KB:pbkmax.qmd]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The inversion problem formulation - finding the minimum k_y such that the exceedance probability of D_n* is controlled at level p - is directly and precisely described in pbkmax.qmd.
