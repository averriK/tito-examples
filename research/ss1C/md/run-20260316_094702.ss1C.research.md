# Seismotectonic Setting: Rampura Agucha Mine Tailings Storage Facility

### SLOT 1: Regional Seismic Generation Mechanisms

The seismic hazard of the region surrounding the Rampura Agucha Mine (RAM) tailings storage facility is governed by four distinct seismogenic mechanism types, all present within the India and surroundings domain as characterized by the GEM probabilistic seismic hazard assessment (PSHA) input model for India (IND model, based on Nath and Thingbaijam, 2012) [KB:ind-report.pdf.md]. These mechanism types are Active Shallow Crust (ASC), Stable Continental Crust (SCC), Subduction Interface (SIF), and Subduction Intra-slab (SIS).^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The KB (ind-report.pdf.md) explicitly identifies tectonic region types including active shallow crust (normal and strike-slip/reverse), stable shallow crust, subduction interface (megathrust and standard), and subduction intraslab (standard and Himalayan), covering all four requested classification types. The GEM IND model documentation is the primary source and is internally consistent.]

Stable Continental Crust (SCC) is the dominant seismogenic mechanism at the regional scale. The Indian plate interior is classified as a Stable Continental Region (SCR), where seismicity arises from reactivation of ancient Precambrian basement structures under the contemporary compressive stress regime imposed by the ongoing India-Asia collision. The Indian subcontinent is notably rigid relative to the Asian crust with which it collides, enabling compressive stresses to propagate over great distances and generate large intraplate earthquakes far from active plate boundaries [KB:ind-report.pdf.md]. The 2001 Mw 7.6 Bhuj earthquake in Gujarat exemplifies the capacity of the stable Indian crust to produce destructive events within the shield interior, killing over 20,000 people [KB:ind-report.pdf.md][WEB:https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9487858/].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The KB explicitly describes the Indian subcontinent as strong and capable of transmitting compressive stresses over great distances, and references the 2001 Bhuj earthquake directly. The PMC paper on Aravalli-Delhi Fold Belt (ADFB) seismicity corroborates the SCR classification and the intraplate character of hazard-controlling sources. No contradictions detected across sources.]

Active Shallow Crust (ASC) seismicity occurs in several tectonically deformed zones within the region. The GEM IND model assigns the ASC tectonic region type, in both normal-faulting and strike-slip/reverse sub-categories, to the Himalayan foreland belt, the Aravalli-Delhi Fold Belt (ADFB), and the Gujarat-Kutch rift zone [KB:ind-report.pdf.md]. The ADFB extends approximately 800 km in a NE-SW orientation from central India through Rajasthan toward Delhi and constitutes the principal zone of active intraplate crustal deformation nearest to the RAM site, with moderate-magnitude earthquakes distributed along its entire length [WEB:https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9487858/].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The KB lists "active shallow crust normal" and "active shallow crust strike-slip reverse" as distinct tectonic region types in the IND model. The PMC paper on ADFB seismicity confirms the fold belt as a major zone of active intraplate crustal seismicity. Both sources are authoritative and mutually consistent.]

The Subduction Interface (SIF) mechanism is represented by the Himalayan convergent system to the north and by the Makran subduction zone to the northwest. The India-Asia collision drives the Indian plate northward at 35-45 mm/yr, producing thrust-faulting events on the Himalayan arc large enough to cause damaging ground motion across northern India [KB:ind-report.pdf.md]. The GEM IND model assigns both "subduction interface" and "subduction interface megathrust" TRTs to the Himalayan and Makran arc systems, with dedicated sets of ground-motion prediction equations for each [KB:ind-report.pdf.md].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The KB directly states the 35-45 mm/yr India-Asia convergence rate and explicitly lists subduction interface and subduction interface megathrust as distinct TRTs with their respective GMPE sets. The information is fully supported by the KB and is consistent with the broader seismological literature.]

Subduction Intra-slab (SIS) seismicity is associated principally with the Hindu Kush-Pamir seismic zone in northwestern Pakistan and Afghanistan, where the Indian plate descends to depths of 200-250 km generating prolific deep-focus earthquake activity. The GEM IND model defines separate SIS TRTs for the general intraslab category and for the Himalayan intraslab sub-region [KB:ind-report.pdf.md]. Although major SIS sources are located at distances exceeding 1,200 km from the RAM site, large-magnitude deep-focus events in the Hindu Kush have historically produced felt shaking across the Indian subcontinent, and their long-period energy content can affect structures at great distances.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The KB identifies "subduction intraslab" and "subduction intraslab Himalayas" as distinct TRTs with assigned GMPE sets, directly supporting the SIS mechanism classification. The distance claim (>1,200 km) is consistent with the epicenters.csv catalog, which shows Hindu Kush deep-focus events at Repi values exceeding 1,200 km from the site. No contradictions detected.]

### SLOT 2: Site-specific Seismogenic Setting

The RAM site, located at approximately 25.84 degrees N, 74.74 degrees E in Bhilwara district, Rajasthan, approximately 220 km from Jaipur, lies within the Precambrian Indian Shield [KB:ind-report.pdf.md]. The site falls specifically within the Stable Continental Crust (SCC) seismogenic domain. Within the GEM IND hazard model, the source zones covering this portion of Rajasthan are assigned the "stable shallow crust" tectonic region type, for which ground-motion characterization employs GMPEs developed for stable continental settings: Raghukanth and Iyengar (2007), Campbell (2003 NSHMP2008), Toro et al. (2002 SHARE), and Atkinson and Boore (2006) [KB:ind-report.pdf.md].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The site's location within the Indian Shield Precambrian craton is confirmed by the task description (Bhilwara district, Rajasthan) and is consistent with the KB's description of the Indian subcontinent as a stable, strong crust. The KB explicitly lists the four GMPEs assigned to the "stable shallow crust" tectonic region type in the IND model. This assignment is unambiguous and directly from the KB.]

Although multiple seismogenic mechanisms coexist at the continental scale (SCC, ASC, SIF, and SIS), the seismogenic region in which the RAM site itself is located corresponds to the SCC domain. The Bureau of Indian Standards classifies Bhilwara district and most of Rajasthan as seismic Zone II, representing the least seismically hazardous category in the national framework [WEB:https://asc-india.org/seismi/seis-rajasthan.htm]. Seismic hazard to the site is controlled principally by SCC background seismicity on intraplate faults within and around the ADFB, with supplementary attenuated contributions from more distant Himalayan (SIF, ASC), Hindu Kush (SIS), and Makran (SIF) sources [KB:ind-report.pdf.md][WEB:https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9487858/]. The northwestern Indian block is among the relatively stable Precambrian regions of the subcontinent; however, the strong crustal rigidity that affords this stability also enables stress transmission over long distances, meaning that very large remote events can contribute non-trivially to long-period hazard at the site [KB:ind-report.pdf.md].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The SCC classification is consistent with the KB's model structure (stable shallow crust GMPEs assigned to central Rajasthan) and the ASC India source for Zone II classification. The description of attenuated contributions from remote sources follows directly from the KB's tectonic overview. The claim about stress transmission is stated verbatim in the KB. All claims are well-supported and mutually consistent.]

### SLOT 4: Active Faults with Neotectonic Activity

Under ANCOLD guidelines, a fault is classified as active if it shows evidence of movement during the Holocene (last approximately 11,000 years) or if it is a large fault with evidence of displacement during the latest Pleistocene (approximately 11,000 to 35,000 years before present). A neotectonic fault is one that has moved within the current crustal stress regime, potentially extending to the last 5-10 million years, and which may therefore move again in the future [WEB:https://www.ancold.org.au/?product_tag=active-fault]. Several structures in the region surrounding the RAM site satisfy one or both criteria on the basis of paleo-seismological, geomorphic, or seismological evidence.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The ANCOLD active fault definition is sourced directly from the ANCOLD website, which is the authoritative reference for ANCOLD guidelines. The definition is consistent with the task instruction describing the 10,000-35,000 year window as the ANCOLD neotectonic criterion. The source is primary and unambiguous.]

The **Great Boundary Fault (GBF)** is the most prominent mapped tectonic structure in southeastern Rajasthan. This NNE-SSW trending lineament extends approximately 400 km from the Chittorgarh-Bundi area to Sawai Madhopur, separating the Vindhyan sedimentary sequence on the east from the Aravalli metamorphic rocks on the west [WEB:https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF03023972]. The fault is described structurally as a dextral strike-slip dominated reverse basement fault, extending through the full crustal thickness. Soft-sediment deformation structures identified in close proximity to the GBF have been interpreted as the product of the Usawan paleo-earthquake, estimated at Mw 6.5-7.2, providing evidence of Quaternary neotectonic reactivation that supports ANCOLD active-fault classification [WEB:https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0169555X23003574].^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The GBF structural description and the Usawan paleo-earthquake evidence are supported by two web sources (Springer journal and ScienceDirect article on GBF geomorphology). The fault's existence and structural character are well-supported. The precise age of the soft-sediment deformation structures is not quantified in the available sources, making the link to the specific ANCOLD 10,000-35,000 year window inferred rather than directly demonstrated. Confidence is medium because the neotectonic age assignment rests on Quaternary inference rather than absolute dating.]

The **Jahazpur Thrust** is a west-dipping compressional structure within Bhilwara district, confirmed by seismic reflection profiling across the ADFB. It lies on the western flank of the fold belt, where published seismological analyses indicate that currently active faults generate modern microseismicity consistent with the regional N-S to NE-SW compressive stress orientation [WEB:https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9487858/]. Western-flank faults of the ADFB, including the Jahazpur Thrust, contrast with the eastern-flank structures such as the GBF in exhibiting clear seismological signatures but subdued geomorphic expression, which is characteristic of blind or partially buried active faults in a stable continental setting. Specific radiometric neotectonic age constraints have not been published for the Jahazpur Thrust, but its seismogenic activity is inferred from modern microseismicity and from alignment with the present-day intraplate stress regime.^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The Jahazpur Thrust is referenced in the PMC paper on ADFB seismicity, which confirms the west-dipping geometry from seismic reflection profiling and identifies western-flank faults as more active than eastern-flank structures. However, specific neotectonic age data for the Jahazpur Thrust are absent from the available sources, so ANCOLD criterion compliance is inferred from seismicity rather than directly demonstrated by paleoseismological evidence. Confidence is medium accordingly.]

The **Narmada-Son Lineament (NSL)**, an approximately EW-trending paleo-rift system in central India located approximately 450-500 km southeast of the RAM site, is among the most seismically active zones in Peninsular India. The lineament is bounded by the Narmada North Fault (NNF) and Narmada South Fault (NSF) and has experienced multiple episodes of seismic reactivation, including the 1997 Jabalpur earthquake (Mw 5.8, depth 35 km) [WEB:https://www.researchgate.net/figure/Seismotectonic-map-of-the-Narmada-Son-Lineament-NSL-zone-comprising-Jabalpur-region-in_fig1_251679601]. Paleoseismological investigations on the Tapti Fault, a parallel central Indian rift structure, have documented Holocene-age surface ruptures of estimated magnitude Mw 7.6-8.4, confirming that crustal faults in this broader rift system are capable of satisfying the ANCOLD active-fault criterion [WEB:https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/2014JB011294].^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The NSL seismic activity and structural description are supported by web sources. The 1997 Jabalpur earthquake is well-documented. The Tapti Fault Holocene surface rupture is cited from the Copley et al. (2014) AGU paper, which is a peer-reviewed source. The inference that the NSL satisfies ANCOLD active criteria is well-grounded. However, the NSL is approximately 450-500 km from the site, so its relevance to site-proximate hazard is limited relative to the ADFB structures.]

The **Konoi Fault** and the associated **Sardar Shahr Fault** are north-south to NNW-SSE trending structures in western Rajasthan. The Konoi Fault was the inferred source of the 1991 Jaisalmer earthquake (Mw 5.4, maximum intensity VIII), the largest instrumentally recorded earthquake in Rajasthan, which caused significant structural damage in the Jaisalmer area. The occurrence of an instrumentally recorded Holocene earthquake on this fault places it within the ANCOLD active-fault category without ambiguity [WEB:https://asc-india.org/seismi/seis-rajasthan.htm].^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The association of the Konoi Fault with the 1991 Jaisalmer earthquake is from the ASC India website, an authoritative source for Indian seismicity data. The magnitude, intensity, and structural damage from the 1991 event are consistently reported. The ANCOLD active-fault classification follows logically from the Holocene occurrence. The Sardar Shahr Fault co-location with the Konoi Fault is from the same source. Confidence is medium because the ASC India website, while authoritative, is not a peer-reviewed publication.]

### SLOT 5: Distance to Nearest Active Fault

The RAM site (approximately 25.84 degrees N, 74.74 degrees E) is situated in Bhilwara district in the immediate vicinity of the Aravalli-Delhi Fold Belt. Based on published geological mapping and seismic reflection surveys, the nearest identified active fault structure is the **Jahazpur Thrust**, which occupies the western flank of the ADFB within Bhilwara district, located approximately 50-60 km east-northeast of the RAM site near Jahazpur town (approximately 25.63 degrees N, 75.26 degrees E) [WEB:https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9487858/].^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The Jahazpur Thrust is identified in the PMC paper on ADFB seismicity as a confirmed west-dipping structure within Bhilwara district. The distance estimate of 50-60 km is computed from the approximate location of Jahazpur town relative to the mine coordinates (~25.84 N, 74.74 E), and yields approximately 57 km. However, the precise fault trace is not published with coordinates in the available sources, so the distance carries spatial uncertainty. The estimate is an approximation based on the structural geology literature.]

The **Great Boundary Fault** constitutes the next-nearest well-documented fault, with its closest mapped segment in the Bundi-Sawai Madhopur sector at an approximate distance of 100 km to the east of the RAM site. The seismotectonic literature notes that the eastern flank structures of the ADFB, including the GBF, display less current seismogenic activity than the western-flank faults, implying that the Jahazpur Thrust and adjacent poorly mapped structures represent the more hazard-relevant proximate sources [WEB:https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9487858/]. Significant uncertainty attaches to all distance estimates in this portion of the Indian Shield, given the subdued geomorphic expression of stable continental intraplate structures and the limited coverage of detailed neotectonic investigations across Rajasthan.^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The GBF distance estimate (~100 km) is computed from published fault-trace descriptions and the mine location, and is a reasonable order-of-magnitude estimate. The assessment that the GBF eastern flank is less active than western-flank structures is stated in the PMC paper. The acknowledgment of uncertainty is appropriate given limited precision of SCC fault-trace mapping. No contradictions detected, but the low density of detailed neotectonic data introduces genuine epistemic uncertainty in the distance values.]
