# Seismotectonic Setting: Rampura Agucha Mine, Rajasthan, India

The Rampura Agucha Mine (RAM) tailings storage facility, owned and operated by Hindustan Zinc Limited (HZL), is situated in Bhilwara District, Rajasthan, India (approximately 25.1 degrees N, 74.7 degrees E), approximately 220 km southwest of Jaipur. As the second-largest zinc mine in the world, the facility requires rigorous characterization of the seismotectonic environment to support probabilistic seismic hazard assessment of the site and surrounding region.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: Site location and ownership are stated in the project context and consistent with publicly available information on the Rampura Agucha deposit. No contradictions found.]

### SLOT 1: Regional Seismic Generation Mechanisms

The seismic hazard of the region surrounding the RAM site is controlled by multiple tectonic generation mechanisms that reflect the complex geodynamic setting of the Indian subcontinent and its bordering plates. The Indian plate moves northward relative to the Eurasian plate at a convergence rate of approximately 35-45 mm/yr; this collision has driven the uplift of the Himalaya, the Tien Shan, the Pamir ranges, and the Tibetan Plateau. The Indian subcontinent is substantially stronger and more rigid than the Asian crustal block against which it collides, so that the principal locus of active deformation and seismicity lies in and north of the Himalayan arc, outside the Indian subcontinent proper. [KB:ind-report.pdf.md] Within the framework employed by the GEM India probabilistic seismic hazard model (v2012.2.0, based on Nath and Thingbaijam, 2012), four seismic generation mechanism types are recognized for the broader region surrounding the RAM site: Active Shallow Crust (ASC), Stable Continental Crust (SCC), Subduction Interface (SIF), and Subduction Intra-slab (SIS). [KB:ind-report.pdf.md]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The tectonic overview, convergence rate (35-45 mm/yr), and the four-category tectonic region classification are directly sourced from the GEM India hazard model documentation in the KB, which is based on the peer-reviewed reference Nath and Thingbaijam (2012). No contradictions detected between KB content and external research.]

**Active Shallow Crust (ASC):** Active shallow crustal seismicity is concentrated in the Himalayan fold-and-thrust belt and associated ranges, located approximately 800-1,000 km north-northeast of the RAM site. The Himalayan thrusts accommodate at least half of the total Indo-Eurasian plate convergence and represent the dominant source of the largest earthquakes affecting northern India, with potential maximum magnitudes exceeding $M_w$ 8.0. [KB:ind-report.pdf.md] The GEM India hazard model treats ASC sources under three sub-categories: active shallow crust normal, active shallow crust strike-slip/reverse, and a dedicated Himalayan active shallow crust source; each is assigned a distinct GMPE logic tree in the model implementation. [KB:ind-report.pdf.md] Exceptions to the predominantly extra-Indian distribution of ASC seismicity include northwest and northeast India, where the Himalayan belt crosses the national border, and the Shillong region of eastern India, where intraplate faults accommodate a component of plate convergence. [KB:ind-report.pdf.md]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The role of Himalayan thrusts in accommodating convergence and generating the largest regional earthquakes is explicitly stated in the KB. The three ASC sub-categories and their GMPE assignments are listed in the KB GMPE table. The geographic distance of 800-1,000 km from RAM to the Himalayan front is consistent with the known geography and corroborated by the epicenters catalog [KB:epicenters.csv], which shows the closest named Himalayan-belt events (1905 Kangra, 2005 Kashmir) at approximately 780-973 km epicentral distance.]

**Stable Continental Crust (SCC):** The Indian Shield, encompassing the Precambrian cratonic terrain of peninsular and northwestern India including the entirety of Rajasthan, is classified within the SCC seismic generation mechanism. Despite the overall tectonic stability of this terrain, the rigid Indian crust is capable of transmitting compressive stresses over great distances, and damaging intraplate earthquakes have occurred well within the interior of the subcontinent. [KB:ind-report.pdf.md] The 2001 $M_w$ 7.6 Bhuj earthquake in Gujarat -- which caused approximately 20,000 fatalities -- constitutes the most significant recent example of large intraplate seismicity within the Indian SCC domain. [KB:ind-report.pdf.md][DOI:10.1007/s12040-022-01957-3] The GEM India model assigns four GMPEs to the stable shallow crust tectonic region type (RaghukanthIyengar2007, Campbell2003MwNSHMP2008, ToroEtAl2002SHARE, AtkinsonBoore2006) and separate GMPE sets for intraplate margin upper and intraplate margin lower zones, which represent transitional environments at the margins of the shield. [KB:ind-report.pdf.md]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The SCC classification of the Indian Shield and the reference to the 2001 Bhuj earthquake are explicitly stated in the KB. The GMPE assignments for stable shallow crust and intraplate margin zones are listed in the KB's GMPE table. The fatality figure of approximately 20,000 is from external peer-reviewed literature (Banerjee et al., 2022); the KB states "over 2,000 people," which is technically accurate but understates the actual toll; the external figure is used here as it is better supported by post-event studies. No structural contradictions found.]

**Subduction Interface (SIF):** Two active subduction systems contribute SIF seismicity to the broader regional hazard. The Makran subduction zone, along the northern Arabian Sea coast of Pakistan and Iran (approximately 1,100-1,500 km west of the RAM site), accommodates the northward subduction of the Arabian Plate beneath the Eurasian Plate and has produced large megathrust earthquakes, including the 1945 $M_w$ 8.1 event and associated tsunami. [KB:ind-report.pdf.md][KB:epicenters.csv] The Indo-Burma subduction zone in northeastern India and Myanmar (approximately 2,000 km east of RAM) constitutes a second active subduction interface system; it is treated in the GEM India model as both a subduction interface megathrust source and a standard subduction interface source, each with separate GMPE logic trees. [KB:ind-report.pdf.md]^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The SIF classification for the Makran and Indo-Burma systems is consistent with the KB's GMPE table, which lists "subduction interface megathrust" and "subduction interface" as distinct tectonic region types. The 1945 Makran earthquake is present in the epicenters catalog. The approximate distances from RAM (1,100-2,000 km) are derived from geographic inference rather than an explicit KB statement, which introduces moderate uncertainty for those specific figures.]

**Subduction Intra-slab (SIS):** Deep intraslab seismicity occurs within the subducting plates of the Makran, Indo-Burma, and Himalayan systems. The Hindu Kush seismic zone of Afghanistan and northern Pakistan is among the world's most active zones for intermediate- and deep-focus earthquakes, with focal depths commonly exceeding 200 km; it generates numerous $M_w > 7.0$ events felt across South Asia. [KB:ind-report.pdf.md][KB:epicenters.csv] The GEM India model assigns four GMPEs for the subduction intraslab tectonic region type (Gupta2010SSlab, AbrahamsonEtAl2015SSlab, AtkinsonBoore2003SSlabCascadia, ZhaoEtAl2006SSlab) and a separate set for the subduction intraslab Himalayan context. [KB:ind-report.pdf.md]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The SIS classification and the specific GMPE assignments are directly stated in the KB. The Hindu Kush deep seismicity is confirmed by multiple entries in the epicenters catalog showing depths of 200-240 km for events in the Jurm, Afghanistan area (e.g., event 16958043 at 200 km depth and multiple Jurm events at 204-240 km). Strong corroborating evidence from both KB sources.]

### SLOT 2: Site-Specific Seismogenic Setting

The RAM site lies within the Aravalli-Delhi Fold Belt (ADFB), a Proterozoic orogenic belt composed of Archean basement rocks (Bhilwara Gneissic Complex, approximately 3.2-2.5 Ga) overlain by Proterozoic metasedimentary sequences, which forms the structural backbone of the Aravalli mountain range in western India. The site falls entirely within the **Stable Continental Crust (SCC)** seismogenic domain. No active subduction system or Himalayan-type active thrust belt is present within the immediate tectonic province of Rajasthan; the dominant seismogenic regime for the sources closest to the RAM site is intraplate in character, driven by far-field compressive stress transmitted through the rigid Indian Shield as a consequence of ongoing Indo-Eurasian plate collision. [KB:ind-report.pdf.md][DOI:10.1007/s12040-022-01957-3]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The SCC classification of the Indian Shield and the intraplate seismicity character are explicitly stated in the KB. The ADFB and BGC geological context is supported by external research (Banerjee et al., 2022, DOI:10.1007/s12040-022-01957-3). The characterization of Rajasthan as an intraplate domain is further corroborated by national seismic hazard assessments and the ASC India seismicity database [WEB:https://asc-india.org/seismi/seis-rajasthan.htm]. No contradictions detected.]

Within the SCC classification, the RAM site occupies an intraplate shield sub-environment. While the GEM India hazard model distinguishes between stable shallow crust and intraplate margin (upper and lower) zones within the broader SCC category, the Bhilwara area of the ADFB is characterized by the stable shallow crust sub-type, reflecting its position well within the Precambrian cratonic interior rather than near a plate boundary. [KB:ind-report.pdf.md] Current seismicity in the ADFB exhibits low to moderate activity dominated by scattered, generally shallow events (focal depths to approximately 40 km across the full crustal section) occurring preferentially on poorly-mapped structures oblique or transverse to the dominant NNE-SSW structural fabric of the fold belt; reverse and strike-slip focal mechanisms are predominant. [DOI:10.1007/s12040-022-01957-3] Under the Indian national seismic zonation standard (BIS IS:1893), Bhilwara District is classified within Seismic Zone II, the lowest assigned hazard category, corresponding to maximum Modified Mercalli Intensity (MMI) of VI or below and a zone factor $Z = 0.10$. [WEB:https://asc-india.org/seismi/seis-rajasthan.htm]^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The GEM model sub-classification of stable shallow crust and intraplate margin zones is supported by the KB GMPE table. The characterization of ADFB seismicity as scattered, shallow, and preferentially on oblique structures is supported by Banerjee et al. (2022). The BIS IS:1893 Zone II classification for Bhilwara is supported by the ASC India web source and multiple secondary references in external research; however, the web source is not a peer-reviewed publication, reducing confidence for the Zone II claim to MEDIUM.]

Multiple seismogenic mechanisms may coexist at the continental scale: the ASC-dominated Himalayan belt, the SIF Makran system, and the SIS Hindu Kush seismic zone all contribute to the regional hazard budget. Nevertheless, the RAM site itself is situated within a single, well-defined seismogenic domain -- the stable continental crust of the Indian Shield -- and the sources with the most direct influence on the site's seismic hazard are intraplate SCC sources, particularly the active fault structures of the ADFB. [KB:ind-report.pdf.md][DOI:10.1007/s12040-022-01957-3]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The coexistence of multiple tectonic mechanisms at the continental scale while the site falls within a single SCC domain is consistent with both the KB and external research. The identification of ADFB fault structures as the primary nearby seismic source is well supported by geographic and geological arguments. No contradictions detected.]

### SLOT 4: Active Faults with Neotectonic Activity

For the purposes of seismic hazard assessment at the RAM site, neotectonic activity is defined in accordance with ANCOLD criteria as evidence of fault displacement within the last 10,000 to 35,000 years, encompassing the Holocene and extending into the late Pleistocene. This temporal window captures fault activity relevant to the recurrence of potentially large, hazard-controlling earthquakes within the operational and post-operational lifetime of a tailings storage facility. [DOI:10.1007/s12040-022-01957-3]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The ANCOLD neotectonic recency window (10,000-35,000 years) is stated in the SLOT 4 task instruction and is consistent with established ANCOLD dam safety guidelines. The application of this criterion to the ADFB region is standard practice in seismic hazard assessments for critical infrastructure in stable continental settings.]

The **Great Boundary Fault (GBF)** is the most significant neotectonic fault structure identified in the broader RAM region. The GBF is a northeast-trending reverse-dextral fault system with an approximate strike length of 400 km, separating Precambrian Aravalli metasedimentary terrain (to the west) from the Proterozoic Vindhyan Supergroup (to the east). Multiple independent lines of geomorphic and stratigraphic evidence confirm neotectonic activity on the GBF within the ANCOLD recency window: (i) documented systematic deflection of river courses consistent with ongoing eastward block tilting along the fault trace; (ii) soft-sediment deformation structures (SSDS) in proximal Quaternary deposits indicative of seismic shaking during and after the deposition of those sediments; and (iii) liquefaction-induced features attributable to a historical paleo-earthquake estimated at $M_w$ 6.5-7.2, interpreted as the product of GBF seismogenesis. [DOI:10.1007/s12040-022-01957-3][WEB:https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9487858/] The GBF is described in the recent literature as "active at present," affecting Quaternary fluvial geomorphology and near-surface sediment architecture. [DOI:10.1007/s12040-022-01957-3]^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The GBF's activity is well-supported by Banerjee et al. (2022, DOI and PMC sources), which is a peer-reviewed publication in the Journal of Earth System Science. The geomorphic evidence (river deflection, SSDS, liquefaction) constitutes standard neotectonic indicators. The KB does not explicitly name the GBF, so this section relies primarily on external research. The paleo-earthquake magnitude estimate (Mw 6.5-7.2) is inferred from the scale of liquefaction features and is inherently uncertain; hence the confidence level is MEDIUM rather than HIGH.]

Fault structures within the **northern Aravalli belt** also exhibit characteristics of neotectonic activity. Geomorphic studies of the Aravalli mountain range document Neogene and Quaternary rejuvenation of basement fault structures within the Precambrian crust of northeastern Rajasthan, expressed in the landscape as segmented uplifted crustal blocks with linear truncations of hill fronts, faceted triangular spurs, hanging valleys, faulted gossans, and Quaternary sediment accumulation in fault-controlled troughs and grabens. [DOI:10.1007/s12040-014-0405-4] Active tectonics index analyses (stream-length gradient index $S_L$, mountain front sinuosity $S_{mf}$, valley floor width-to-height ratio $V_f$) applied to sub-watersheds in the southern and central Aravalli range -- including areas approximately 100 km south-southwest of the RAM site -- confirm relative tectonic activity consistent with ongoing or recent fault displacement. [DOI:10.1007/s12517-019-5028-2]^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The neotectonic evidence for northern Aravalli belt faults is supported by two external DOI-identified peer-reviewed sources (Springer, 2014 and 2019/2020) applying recognized geomorphic indicators. A limitation is that the specific faults responsible for the documented features are not individually named or mapped at sufficient resolution in the available literature; activity is attributed to the general basement fault network of the ADFB. This structural ambiguity introduces moderate uncertainty.]

The **Jahazpur Thrust**, a crustal-scale seismic reflector within the eastern flank of the ADFB, is a thrust structure extending from approximately 5 km depth near Jahazpur town to near-Moho depths, representing a major crustal discontinuity approximately 40-50 km northeast of the RAM site. Available seismicity data indicate that current earthquake activity in the ADFB preferentially occurs on the western flank of the fold belt and on structures transverse or oblique to the main NNE-SSW structural trend, rather than on the Jahazpur Thrust itself; this suggests the Jahazpur Thrust may be comparatively less active at present. [DOI:10.1007/s12040-022-01957-3] In the absence of direct paleoseismic trench data or dated fault exposures, the ANCOLD neotectonic classification of the Jahazpur Thrust cannot be definitively established from available published literature.^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The Jahazpur Thrust's crustal-scale nature and the inference of relative inactivity based on seismicity distribution are supported by Banerjee et al. (2022). The absence of specific paleoseismic data for this structure means its ANCOLD classification remains uncertain, reducing confidence to MEDIUM. Its proximity (~40-50 km NE) and crustal scale still make it a structure warranting attention in hazard assessments.]

### SLOT 5: Distance to Nearest Active Fault

Among the fault structures with documented or probable neotectonic activity in the region, the **Great Boundary Fault** represents the nearest structure with confirmed Quaternary and probable Holocene displacement. The GBF trace passes through the Chittorgarh area approximately 70-80 km southeast of the RAM site (closest approach), and through the Bundi area approximately 100 km to the east. Based on the geographic relationship between the RAM site coordinates (approximately 25.1 degrees N, 74.7 degrees E) and the known GBF trace position, the minimum distance from the RAM site to this neotectonic fault is estimated at approximately **70-80 km** (southeast direction). [DOI:10.1007/s12040-022-01957-3][WEB:https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9487858/]^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The GBF's geographic position relative to Chittorgarh and Bundi is described in external peer-reviewed literature. The distance estimate of 70-80 km is derived from the geographic relationship between RAM coordinates and the GBF trace position as inferred from the literature. The precise fault trace geometry has not been digitized at site-specific resolution for this analysis, introducing moderate spatial uncertainty; the estimate may range from approximately 60 to 90 km depending on the specific fault segment considered.]

The Jahazpur Thrust, located approximately 40-50 km northeast of the RAM site, may represent a nearer crustal-scale fault structure. However, given the uncertainty regarding its current neotectonic activity status -- specifically the absence of confirmed Holocene surface rupture data -- its classification as an active fault per ANCOLD criteria is not conclusively supported by available published evidence. If the Jahazpur Thrust is conservatively treated as a potentially active structure, the effective distance to the nearest fault of seismogenic significance in the vicinity of the RAM site could be reduced to approximately **40-50 km** (northeast direction). [DOI:10.1007/s12040-022-01957-3]^[Confidence: LOW, Rationale: The distance of 40-50 km to the Jahazpur Thrust is supported by geographic inference from external research (Banerjee et al., 2022), but the neotectonic activity status of this structure is ambiguous. The confidence is LOW because the key parameter -- whether this fault qualifies as neotectonic -- is uncertain, and the distance figure itself is approximate.]
