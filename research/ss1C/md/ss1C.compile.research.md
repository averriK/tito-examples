# Seismic Hazard Context -- Rampura Agucha Mine

## 1. Regional seismic generation mechanisms

The seismic hazard of the region surrounding the Rampura Agucha Mine (RAM) is governed by four seismogenic mechanism types, all recognized within the GEM probabilistic seismic hazard assessment (PSHA) input model for India (hereafter GEM IND model), based on Nath and Thingbaijam (2012) [@NathThingbaijam2012]. These mechanism types are Active Shallow Crust (ASC), Stable Continental Crust (SCC), Subduction Interface (SIF), and Subduction Intra-slab (SIS). The Indian Plate moves northward relative to Eurasia at approximately 35-45 mm/yr, driving deformation and seismicity across several tectonically distinct domains within this four-category framework [@NathThingbaijam2012].

**Stable Continental Crust (SCC)** is the dominant seismogenic mechanism in the region immediately surrounding the RAM site. Peninsular India, including the entirety of Rajasthan, constitutes a stable continental region (SCR) in which the Aravalli-Bundelkhand craton has remained largely intact since the Proterozoic. Seismicity in the SCR is intraplate in origin, generated by reactivation of ancient Precambrian basement fault structures under far-field compressive stresses transmitted from the India-Eurasia collision zone. The rigid Indian crust is capable of transmitting these compressive stresses over great distances, and damaging intraplate earthquakes have occurred well within the stable interior of the plate; the 2001 $M_W$ 7.6 Bhuj earthquake in Gujarat, which caused approximately 20,000 fatalities, constitutes the most significant modern example of large intraplate seismicity within the Indian SCC domain [@NathThingbaijam2012][@BanerjeeEtAl2022a]. The GEM IND model assigns the tectonic region type label "stable shallow crust" to central and western Rajasthan, with ground-motion characterization based on GMPEs calibrated for stable continental settings: Raghukanth and Iyengar (2007), Campbell (2003 NSHMP2008), Toro et al. (2002 SHARE), and Atkinson and Boore (2006) [@NathThingbaijam2012].

**Active Shallow Crust (ASC)** seismicity characterizes the orogenic belts flanking peninsular India, particularly the Himalayan collision zone and associated fold-thrust systems. The Himalayan arc, including the Main Boundary Thrust and Main Central Thrust, is located approximately 800-1,000 km north-northeast of the RAM site; Himalayan thrust faults accommodate at least half of the total Indo-Eurasian plate convergence and represent the dominant source of the largest earthquakes affecting northern India, with potential maximum magnitudes exceeding $M_W$ 8.0 [@NathThingbaijam2012]. The Aravalli-Delhi Fold Belt (ADFB), extending approximately 800 km in a NE-SW orientation from central India through Rajasthan toward Delhi, constitutes the principal zone of active intraplate crustal deformation nearest to the RAM site, with moderate-magnitude earthquakes distributed along its length [@BanerjeeEtAl2022a]. The GEM IND model assigns ASC tectonic region types under three sub-categories: "active shallow crust normal," "active shallow crust strike-slip reverse," and a dedicated Himalayan sub-category, each with a distinct ground-motion logic tree [@NathThingbaijam2012].

**Subduction Interface (SIF)** seismicity is generated by two active subduction systems within the broader regional domain. The Makran subduction zone, located along the northern Arabian Sea coast of Pakistan and Iran approximately 1,100-1,200 km west-northwest of the RAM site, is the principal active subduction interface in the northwestern Indian Ocean; the 1945 $M_W$ 8.1 Makran earthquake, which generated a destructive tsunami in the Arabian Sea, is the largest historical event on this structure [@NathThingbaijam2012][@GEMIndiaEpicenters2012]. The Indo-Burma subduction zone in northeastern India and Myanmar, approximately 2,000 km east of RAM, constitutes a second active subduction interface system. The GEM IND model assigns "subduction interface" and "subduction interface megathrust" tectonic region types to these source systems, each with dedicated GMPE logic trees [@NathThingbaijam2012].

**Subduction Intra-slab (SIS)** seismicity is associated principally with the Hindu Kush-Pamir seismic zone of Afghanistan and northern Pakistan, where the Indian plate descends to depths of 200-250 km, generating prolific intermediate- and deep-focus earthquake activity. Large-magnitude deep-focus events in the Hindu Kush have historically produced felt shaking across the Indian subcontinent. The GEM IND model defines separate SIS tectonic region types for the general intraslab category and for the Himalayan intraslab sub-region, with four GMPEs assigned to the standard intraslab type: Gupta (2010), Abrahamson et al. (2015), Atkinson and Boore (2003 Cascadia), and Zhao et al. (2006) [@NathThingbaijam2012][@GEMIndiaEpicenters2012].

## 2. Site-specific seismogenic setting

The RAM site, located at approximately 25.84 degrees N, 74.74 degrees E in Bhilwara district, Rajasthan, falls within the **Stable Continental Crust (SCC)** seismogenic domain. The site occupies the Aravalli-Delhi Fold Belt (ADFB), a Proterozoic orogenic belt composed of Archean basement rocks (Bhilwara Gneissic Complex, approximately 3.2-2.5 Ga) overlain by Proterozoic metasedimentary sequences, forming the structural backbone of the Aravalli mountain range in western India. No active subduction system or Himalayan-type active thrust belt is present within the immediate tectonic province of Rajasthan; the dominant seismogenic regime for sources closest to the RAM site is intraplate in character, driven by far-field compressive stress transmitted through the rigid Indian Shield as a consequence of ongoing Indo-Eurasian plate collision [@NathThingbaijam2012][@BanerjeeEtAl2022b].

Within the GEM IND hazard model, the source zones covering the Bhilwara area are assigned the "stable shallow crust" tectonic region type, for which ground-motion characterization employs GMPEs developed for stable continental settings: Raghukanth and Iyengar (2007), Campbell (2003 NSHMP2008), Toro et al. (2002 SHARE), and Atkinson and Boore (2006) [@NathThingbaijam2012]. Under the Bureau of Indian Standards seismic zonation classification (IS 1893:2016), Bhilwara district falls within Seismic Zone II, the lowest hazard category in the national framework, corresponding to a zone factor $Z = 0.10$ and expected maximum Modified Mercalli Intensity (MMI) of VI or below [@ASCIndiaRajasthan].

Although multiple seismogenic mechanisms coexist at the continental scale -- ASC-dominated Himalayan belt, SIF Makran system, and SIS Hindu Kush seismic zone -- the RAM site itself lies within a single, well-defined seismogenic domain: the stable continental crust of the Indian Shield. The seismic sources with the most direct influence on the site's hazard are intraplate SCC sources, particularly the active fault structures of the ADFB, supplemented by attenuated contributions from more distant Himalayan (ASC, SIF), Hindu Kush (SIS), and Makran (SIF) sources [@NathThingbaijam2012][@BanerjeeEtAl2022a]. The strong crustal rigidity that characterizes the stable interior of the Indian Shield also permits stress transmission over long distances, meaning that very large remote events can contribute non-trivially to long-period hazard at the site [@NathThingbaijam2012].

The regional earthquake catalog for the area surrounding the RAM site is drawn primarily from the reference epicentral compilation assembled for the GEM IND model [@GEMIndiaEpicenters2012], which documents events with $M > 7.0$ ordered by increasing epicentral distance from the RAM site. Events are listed below in order of proximity.

**Events with $M > 7.0$ from the reference catalog, ordered by increasing epicentral distance from the RAM site:**

- **Historical (date not recorded in catalog)**, northern Rajasthan/southern Haryana region (approx. 28.78 degrees N, 75.16 degrees E): $M_B$ 7.1; depth 20 km; epicentral distance approximately 329 km NNE. This is the closest $M > 7.0$ event to the RAM site in the regional catalog [@GEMIndiaEpicenters2012].
- **26 January 2001**, Bhuj, Gujarat, India (approx. 23.4 degrees N, 70.3 degrees E): $M_W$ 7.6; depth 16 km; epicentral distance approximately 523 km WSW. Catastrophic intraplate earthquake on the Kachchh Mainland Fault system; approximately 20,000 fatalities; the canonical modern example of large-magnitude SCC seismicity within the Indian Shield [@GEMIndiaEpicenters2012][@NathThingbaijam2012].
- **Historical (date not recorded in catalog)**, Kumaon Himalaya/western Nepal border region (approx. 29.73 degrees N, 80.75 degrees E): $M_W$ 7.2; depth 20 km; epicentral distance approximately 731 km NNE [@GEMIndiaEpicenters2012].
- **4 April 1905**, Kangra, Himachal Pradesh, India (approx. 32.6 degrees N, 76.9 degrees E): $M_W$ 7.9; depth 20 km; epicentral distance approximately 780 km NNE. One of the most destructive historical earthquakes in India, causing approximately 19,000 fatalities in the Kangra valley [@GEMIndiaEpicenters2012].
- **27 February 1997**, near Harnai, Balochistan, Pakistan (approx. 29.98 degrees N, 68.21 degrees E): $M_W$ 7.1; epicentral distance approximately 789 km WNW [@GEMIndiaEpicenters2012].
- **20 October 1909**, near Bhag, Balochistan, Pakistan: $M_W$ 7.16; epicentral distance approximately 790 km WNW [@GEMIndiaEpicenters2012].
- **27 August 1931**, near Mach, Balochistan, Pakistan: $M_W$ 7.2; epicentral distance approximately 843 km WNW [@GEMIndiaEpicenters2012].
- **30 May 1935**, near Quetta, Balochistan, Pakistan (approx. 28.96 degrees N, 66.44 degrees E): $M_W$ 7.6; depth 25 km; epicentral distance approximately 890 km WNW. Approximately 30,000-60,000 fatalities; one of the deadliest seismic events in South Asian recorded history [@GEMIndiaEpicenters2012].
- **8 October 2005**, Kashmir, Azad Kashmir, Pakistan (approx. 34.54 degrees N, 73.59 degrees E): $M_W$ 7.6; depth 26 km; epicentral distance approximately 973 km NNW. Approximately 86,000 fatalities; extensive infrastructure damage across northern Pakistan and Azad Kashmir [@GEMIndiaEpicenters2012].
- **25 April 2015**, Gorkha, Nepal (approx. 28.23 degrees N, 84.73 degrees E): $M_W$ 7.8; depth 8.2 km; epicentral distance approximately 1,024 km NE. Approximately 9,000 fatalities; triggered a major aftershock sequence including a $M_W$ 7.3 event on 12 May 2015 [@GEMIndiaEpicenters2012].
- **27 November 1945**, Makran subduction zone, Pakistan-Iran (approx. 24.98 degrees N, 63.68 degrees E): $M_W$ 8.1; depth 15 km; epicentral distance approximately 1,115 km WNW. Subduction interface event; generated a destructive tsunami affecting the Arabian Sea coastline [@GEMIndiaEpicenters2012].
- **15 January 1934**, Bihar-Nepal border (approx. 27.28 degrees N, 86.94 degrees E): $M_W$ 8.0; depth 15 km; epicentral distance approximately 1,223 km NE. Approximately 10,000-11,000 fatalities across Bihar and Nepal; among the largest instrumentally recorded Himalayan-belt earthquakes [@GEMIndiaEpicenters2012].

The following supplementary events are not present in the $M > 7.0$ regional catalog but are seismologically relevant to the regional context:

- **16 June 1819**, Rann of Kutch (Allah Bund), Gujarat, India: $M$ approximately 7.7-8.0; epicentral distance approximately 600-700 km WSW. This pre-instrumental intraplate event produced an extensive surface rupture scarp estimated at 80-200 km length and remains one of the largest known intraplate earthquakes within the Indian Shield, attributed to reverse faulting in the Kutch rift zone [@ASCIndiaRajasthan][@IJESRajasthan].
- **15 August 1906**, Thar Desert, northwestern Rajasthan (approx. 27.0 degrees N, 73.0 degrees E): $M_W$ approximately 6.2; the largest instrumentally recorded earthquake within Rajasthan; epicentral distance approximately 300 km WNW of RAM [@ASCIndiaRajasthan].
- **18 November 1991**, Jaisalmer district, Rajasthan (approx. 26.9 degrees N, 70.5 degrees E): $M_W$ 5.4; associated with the Konoi Fault; maximum intensity VIII; structural damage at Konoi village; epicentral distance approximately 340 km west of RAM [@ASCIndiaRajasthan].

## 4. Active faults with neotectonic activity

> Confidence: MIXED -- This section includes HIGH-confidence content plus selected MEDIUM-confidence structured details preserved to avoid dropping essential structure during compilation.

For the purposes of seismic hazard assessment at the RAM site, neotectonic activity is defined in accordance with ANCOLD (Australian National Committee on Large Dams) criteria as evidence of fault displacement during the Holocene (last approximately 11,000 years) or, for large faults, during the latest Pleistocene (11,000-35,000 years before present). Faults satisfying either condition are considered capable of future movement and are directly relevant to seismic hazard assessment for dam and tailings storage facilities [@ANCOLD2019][@MDPIActiveFault2024].

The following fault structures in the region surrounding the RAM site exhibit evidence of neotectonic activity meeting one or more ANCOLD criteria.

**Great Boundary Fault (GBF)**

The GBF is the most significant neotectonic structure identified in the broader RAM region. It is a northeast-trending reverse-dextral fault system with an approximate strike length of 400 km, separating Precambrian Aravalli metasedimentary terrain to the west from the Proterozoic Vindhyan Supergroup to the east, and passing through Chittorgarh and Bundi districts in Rajasthan [@SpringerGBF1994]. Multiple independent lines of geomorphic and stratigraphic evidence confirm neotectonic activity on the GBF within the ANCOLD recency window: systematic deflection of river courses consistent with ongoing block tilting along the fault trace; soft-sediment deformation structures (SSDS) in proximal Quaternary deposits indicative of seismic shaking; and liquefaction-induced features attributable to a paleoearthquake (the Usawan event) estimated at $M_W$ 6.5-7.2 based on the scale of observed deformation features [@GBFGeomorph2023][@BanerjeeEtAl2022b]. The GBF is described in the recent literature as active at present, affecting Quaternary fluvial geomorphology and near-surface sediment architecture [@BanerjeeEtAl2022b].

**Jahazpur Thrust**

The Jahazpur Thrust is a west-dipping crustal-scale compressional structure within Bhilwara district, confirmed by seismic reflection profiling across the ADFB. It extends from approximately 5 km depth near Jahazpur town to near-Moho depths, representing a major crustal discontinuity approximately 40-50 km northeast of the RAM site. Current seismicity in the ADFB is distributed preferentially on the western flank of the fold belt and on structures transverse or oblique to the main NNE-SSW structural trend, rather than on the Jahazpur Thrust itself, suggesting the Jahazpur Thrust may be comparatively less active at present [@BanerjeeEtAl2022a][@BanerjeeEtAl2022b]. In the absence of primary paleoseismic trench data or dated fault exposures, its ANCOLD neotectonic classification cannot be definitively established from the published literature.

**Narmada-Son Lineament (NSL)**

The NSL is a major approximately E-W Precambrian rift structure approximately 1,200 km in length, extending across central India at approximately 22-23 degrees N latitude, approximately 200-500 km south of the RAM site. The NSL is bounded by the Narmada North Fault and Narmada South Fault and exhibits Holocene neotectonic activity on the basis of morphotectonic evidence including differential uplift, basin inversion, and river incision patterns [@NSLGeomorph2013]. The 1997 Jabalpur earthquake ($M_W$ 5.8) on the eastern NSL segment confirms present-day seismogenic activity, and GPS-derived convergence rates of approximately 2 $\pm$ 1 mm/yr across the structure indicate ongoing strain loading [@NSLGnss2021][@NSLSeismotectMap]. The parallel Tapti Fault has yielded paleoseismological evidence of Holocene-age surface ruptures with estimated magnitudes of $M_W$ 7.6-8.4, confirming that the broader central Indian rift system satisfies the ANCOLD active-fault criterion [@CopleyEtAl2014].

**Kachchh Mainland Fault (KMF) system**

The KMF system in Gujarat is one of the best-documented neotectonic fault systems in India, located approximately 600-650 km WSW of the RAM site. Paleoseismic investigations of the Jhura segment of the KMF have identified multiple Holocene surface-rupturing earthquakes, with events dated between approximately 10,000 and 890 years before present and estimated magnitudes ranging from $M_W$ 5.8 to 6.5 per event. Fault-normal slip rates of 0.04-0.36 mm/yr have been determined from geomorphic and geodetic methods. The 1819 Allah Bund earthquake and the 2001 Bhuj earthquake ($M_W$ 7.6) confirm that the broader Kachchh seismogenic zone is capable of generating large to great intraplate earthquakes [@KMFPaleoseis2024][@KMFScRep2024].

**Additional structures**

Geomorphic studies of the Aravalli mountain range document Neogene and Quaternary rejuvenation of basement fault structures in northeastern Rajasthan, expressed as segmented uplifted crustal blocks with linear hill-front truncations, triangular faceted spurs, hanging valleys, and fault-controlled Quaternary sediment accumulations [@AravagelliGeomorph2014]. Active tectonics index analyses (stream-length gradient index $S_L$, mountain front sinuosity $S_{mf}$, valley floor width-to-height ratio $V_f$) applied to sub-watersheds in the southern and central Aravalli range confirm relative tectonic activity consistent with ongoing or recent fault displacement [@AravagelliActiveTect2019]. The Konoi Fault in western Rajasthan was the inferred source of the 1991 Jaisalmer earthquake ($M_W$ 5.4, maximum intensity VIII), the largest instrumentally recorded earthquake in Rajasthan, unambiguously placing it within the ANCOLD active-fault category [@ASCIndiaRajasthan].

## 5. Distance to nearest active fault

> Confidence: MEDIUM -- No HIGH-confidence evidence was available for this section; content below is included with limited confidence.

Among the fault structures with documented neotectonic activity in the region, the **Great Boundary Fault (GBF)** represents the nearest to the RAM site with confirmed Quaternary and probable Holocene displacement. Published geological mapping places one principal segment of the GBF in the Chittorgarh area, approximately 65-80 km southeast of Bhilwara district, and another segment in the Bundi-Sawai Madhopur sector to the east-southeast. Based on the geographic relationship between the RAM site coordinates (approximately 25.1-25.84 degrees N, 74.74 degrees E) and the published GBF trace positions, the minimum distance from the site to the nearest GBF segment is estimated at approximately **50-80 km** in the southeasterly direction [@GBFGeomorph2023][@BanerjeeEtAl2022a][@BanerjeeEtAl2022b].

The **Jahazpur Thrust**, located approximately 50-60 km east-northeast of the RAM site within Bhilwara district, may represent a nearer crustal-scale fault structure [@BanerjeeEtAl2022a]. However, its ANCOLD neotectonic classification remains uncertain in the absence of published paleoseismic trench data or dated fault exposures, and it is therefore not conclusively established as an active fault per ANCOLD criteria. If treated conservatively as a potentially active structure, the effective distance to the nearest fault of seismogenic significance could be reduced to approximately 40-60 km in the northeast direction.

All distance estimates carry spatial uncertainty of approximately $\pm 20$ km, reflecting the limited resolution of published fault-trace mapping and the subdued geomorphic expression characteristic of stable continental intraplate structures in this part of Rajasthan.

## 6. Slip rates, maximum magnitude, and distances of major active faults

> Confidence: MIXED -- This section includes HIGH-confidence content plus selected MEDIUM-confidence structured details preserved to avoid dropping essential structure during compilation.

The GEM IND hazard model for India employs a single set of areal seismogenic source zones and two smoothed-gridded point source models rather than individual fault sources for the Rajasthan and Peninsular India region [@NathThingbaijam2012]. Formally assigned fault-level slip rates and $M_{max}$ values from the GEM model are therefore not available for individual structures in this area. The parameters reported below are derived from the published geoscientific literature and represent best estimates or ranges, reflecting the limited neotectonic characterization of stable continental faults in the Indian Shield.

The following table summarizes available parameter estimates for the principal active faults in the RAM region:

| Fault | Approx. Distance from RAM | Slip Rate | $M_{max}$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Great Boundary Fault (GBF) | ~50-80 km (SE) | Not quantified; inferred $< 1$ mm/yr | $7.60 \pm 0.67$ (seismogenic zone); $M_W$ 6.5-7.2 (Usawan paleoseismic) |
| Jahazpur Thrust | ~50-60 km (ENE) | Not quantified; inferred $< 0.1$ mm/yr | Not individually assigned |
| Narmada-Son Lineament | ~200-250 km (S) | $\approx 2 \pm 1$ mm/yr (GPS convergence) | $M_W$ 6.0-6.5 (seismic strain rate); $M_W$ 7.6-8.4 (Tapti Fault paleoseismic) |
| Kachchh Mainland Fault | ~600-650 km (WSW) | 0.04-0.36 mm/yr (paleoseismic/geodetic) | $M_W$ 7.6 (2001 Bhuj; zone); $M_W$ 5.8-6.5 (per segment) |

The Great Boundary Fault has no published fault-specific geodetic or paleoseismic slip rate. A seismogenic zone $M_{max}$ of $7.60 \pm 0.67$ has been estimated from historical seismicity catalogs for the zone encompassing the GBF [@BanerjeeEtAl2022b]; paleoseismic inference from the Usawan event independently supports a range of $M_W$ 6.5-7.2 for the fault itself, based on the scale of soft-sediment deformation structures and liquefaction features [@GBFGeomorph2023][@IyengarGhosh2004].

The Narmada-Son Lineament has a GPS-derived convergence rate of approximately 2 $\pm$ 1 mm/yr, determined from PSInSAR and GNSS data [@NSLGnss2021]. Its maximum magnitude is estimated at $M_W$ 6.0-6.5 from seismic strain rate analysis; the 1997 Jabalpur earthquake ($M_W$ 5.8) is the largest instrumentally recorded event on the lineament. Paleoseismic investigations of the parallel Tapti Fault document Holocene surface ruptures of $M_W$ 7.6-8.4, confirming the capability of the broader central Indian rift system for large events [@CopleyEtAl2014].

The Kachchh Mainland Fault system is the best-characterized intraplate fault in the broader region, with Holocene slip rates of 0.04-0.36 mm/yr documented from surface-rupture paleoseismology and geomorphic methods. An $M_{max}$ of approximately $M_W$ 7.6 is constrained by the 2001 Bhuj earthquake, while paleoseismic data from individual KMF segments constrain per-segment $M_{max}$ values at $M_W$ 5.8-6.5 [@KMFPaleoseis2024][@KMFScRep2024].

Slip rates for the Jahazpur Thrust and the GBF are not directly published; values inferred from the overall low geodetic strain rates of the Indian Shield suggest rates below 0.1 mm/yr and below 1 mm/yr, respectively, consistent with the very slow deformation characteristic of stable continental interiors [@BanerjeeEtAl2022b][@IndiaSlipRatesMap].
