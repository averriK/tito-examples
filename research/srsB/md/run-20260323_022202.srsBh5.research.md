# Weight Calibration with Sum-to-One Normalization Constraint: Formulation and Validation

## SLOT 1: Extend weight calibration formulation for sum-to-one constraint

The foundational weight calibration problem in spectral matching casts record selection as a minimization of prediction error across a discretized period mesh. [KB:spec.compile.research.md] The linear-combination framework assembles candidate spectral ordinates into a design matrix and minimizes residual error, with the log-log reformulation providing improved homoscedasticity and alignment with PSHA conventions where $\ln S_a(T)$ is modeled as normally distributed. [KB:spec.compile.research.md] When weights must form a discrete probability distribution, an additional constraint requiring $\sum_{k=1}^{N} w_k = 1$ defines a normalized weighting scheme. This section extends the constrained optimization formulation to incorporate this normalization requirement alongside existing physical constraints.

^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The foundational concepts and log-log reformulation are directly sourced from the KB document, which provides detailed background on the linear-combination framework and its mathematical structure.]

Define the transformed design matrix $\tilde{\mathbf{A}} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times K}$ with entries $\tilde{A}_{ik} = \ln S_{a,k}(T_i)$, where $S_{a,k}(T_i)$ is the log-spectral acceleration of the $k$-th candidate record at period $T_i$, and the target vector $\tilde{\mathbf{y}} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ with components $\tilde{y}_i = \ln S_a^{\mathrm{obj}}(T_i)$. [KB:spec.compile.research.md] The weight vector $\mathbf{w} = (w_1, \dots, w_K)^\top$ is constrained to lie within a box defined by physical bounds and a normalization requirement. The sum-to-one constrained formulation is expressed as:

$$\mathbf{w}^* = \underset{\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^K}{\arg\min} \; \|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w} - \tilde{\mathbf{y}}\|_2^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad w_k \geq 0, \quad \sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k = 1, \quad k = 1, \dots, K.$$

^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: This formulation directly extends the NNLS formulation from SLOT 5 of the KB by adding the linear equality constraint $\sum w_k = 1$. The log-log objective and non-negativity constraints are explicitly defined in the KB.]

This formulation can be generalized to encompass box constraints on individual weight magnitudes. When strictly positive lower and upper bounds on weights are required, the constrained formulation becomes:

$$\mathbf{w}^* = \underset{\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^K}{\arg\min} \; \|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w} - \tilde{\mathbf{y}}\|_2^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad w_{\min} \leq w_k \leq w_{\max}, \quad \sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k = 1, \quad k = 1, \dots, K.$$

^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: Box constraints on weights are introduced in SLOT 6 of the KB as a standard extension to enforce physical plausibility. The addition of the linear equality constraint maintains the convexity of the feasible region and preserves the uniqueness of the global minimizer.]

The feasible set is now a simplex intersected with box constraints—a compact, convex, lower-dimensional polytope in $\mathbb{R}^K$ defined by the $K+1$ linear equality and inequality constraints. The strictly convex quadratic objective function guarantees a unique global minimizer over this feasible region, provided the design matrix $\tilde{\mathbf{A}}$ has full column rank (equivalently, the candidate spectral shapes are linearly independent over the period mesh).

^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The convexity argument is a standard result from convex optimization theory. The feasible set (intersection of a simplex with a box) is convex, and the squared residual norm has a positive-semidefinite Hessian ($2\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\tilde{\mathbf{A}}$), ensuring convexity of the objective and uniqueness of the global solution.]

The solution method employs constrained quadratic programming, similar to the bounded-variable least-squares (BVLS) approach described in SLOT 6 of the KB. [KB:spec.compile.research.md] The standard formulation of the constrained QP is:

$$\text{minimize} \quad \frac{1}{2} \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{H} \mathbf{w} - \mathbf{g}^\top \mathbf{w} \quad \text{subject to} \quad \mathbf{A}_{\text{eq}} \mathbf{w} = \mathbf{b}_{\text{eq}}, \quad w_{\min} \leq w_k \leq w_{\max}},$$

where the Hessian is $\mathbf{H} = 2\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\tilde{\mathbf{A}}$, the linear term is $\mathbf{g} = 2\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\tilde{\mathbf{y}}$, the equality constraint matrix is $\mathbf{A}_{\text{eq}} = \mathbf{1}^\top$ (a $1 \times K$ row vector of ones), and the equality right-hand side is $\mathbf{b}_{\text{eq}} = 1$.

^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The QP form is a standard reformulation of the constrained least-squares problem. The Hessian and gradient terms follow from differentiating the squared residual norm with respect to $\mathbf{w}$. The equality constraint encodes the sum-to-one requirement.]

In practice, the optimization can be solved using interior-point QP solvers (such as OSQP or those available in optimization suites like CVXR discussed in SLOT 7 of the KB [KB:spec.compile.research.md]), active-set methods with equality-constraint handling, or specialized simplex-constrained least-squares algorithms. Interior-point methods scale well for moderate problem sizes (typical candidate set sizes of $K \leq 30$ and period meshes of $n = 50$ to $100$), making them suitable for routine spectral-matching applications.

^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The solver recommendations are based on standard convex optimization practice and the KB's discussion of solver selection in SLOT 7. However, the specific scaling performance and numerical stability properties of particular solvers applied to this constrained problem are not evaluated in depth in the KB.]

The sum-to-one constraint represents a material departure from the unconstrained OLS and NNLS formulations of SLOT 3 and SLOT 5. [KB:spec.compile.research.md] Without normalization, the unconstrained OLS solution permits negative weights and unbounded positive weights, while the NNLS solution allows weights to grow without upper limit. The sum-to-one constraint ensures that the weights form a proper discrete probability distribution, with each weight interpreted as the relative contribution of its corresponding record to the final spectral combination. This normalization is particularly important in the logic-tree framework of PSHA, where weights must represent branch probabilities and sum exactly to unity across all alternatives.

^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The distinction between unconstrained, NNLS, and normalized formulations is clearly established in the KB. The interpretation of weights as probability branches in logic-tree PSHA is a standard seismic hazard practice consistent with the PSHA framework described in the TASK_FILE context.]

## SLOT 2: Validate normalization approach and justify mathematical treatment

The question of whether post-hoc normalization of an unconstrained or NNLS weight solution is mathematically equivalent to solving the sum-to-one constrained problem directly requires careful analysis of the optimization structure.

^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: This is a fundamental question about constrained optimization and equivalence of formulations. The analysis relies on standard convex optimization theory.]

Consider first the post-hoc normalization procedure: solve the unconstrained problem or the NNLS problem to obtain $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_0 = (\hat{w}_{0,1}, \dots, \hat{w}_{0,K})^\top$, then rescale by the sum of components:

$$\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\mathrm{norm}} = \frac{\hat{\mathbf{w}}_0}{\sum_{k=1}^{K} \hat{w}_{0,k}}.$$

This normalized vector satisfies $\sum_{k=1}^{K} \hat{w}_{\mathrm{norm},k} = 1$ by construction. However, in general, $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\mathrm{norm}}$ does **not** minimize the original objective $\|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w} - \tilde{\mathbf{y}}\|_2^2$ subject to the sum-to-one constraint.

^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: This is a direct consequence of constrained optimization theory. Normalization of an unconstrained solution does not satisfy the necessary conditions (KKT conditions) for optimality of the constrained problem. The proof follows from the structure of the Lagrangian.]

To establish this, consider the Lagrangian of the sum-to-one constrained problem:

$$\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w}, \lambda) = \|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w} - \tilde{\mathbf{y}}\|_2^2 + \lambda \left(\sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k - 1\right),$$

where $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$ is the Lagrange multiplier for the equality constraint. The Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) first-order necessary conditions for optimality require:

$$2\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w}^* - \tilde{\mathbf{y}}) + \lambda^* \mathbf{1} = \mathbf{0}, \quad \sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k^* = 1,$$

where $\mathbf{1} = (1, 1, \dots, 1)^\top$. These conditions imply that the gradient of the Lagrangian with respect to $\mathbf{w}$ is proportional to $\mathbf{1}$: all components of the gradient are equal at optimality.

^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The KKT conditions for this constrained quadratic program are standard from convex optimization. The condition that all gradient components are equal at optimality is a direct consequence of the Lagrange multiplier condition.]

In contrast, the unconstrained OLS solution $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_0$ satisfies:

$$2\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\hat{\mathbf{w}}_0 - \tilde{\mathbf{y}}) = \mathbf{0},$$

so the gradient is zero at every component. When $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_0$ is normalized to $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\mathrm{norm}}$, the gradient vector becomes:

$$\nabla_{\mathbf{w}} \left\|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\mathrm{norm}} - \tilde{\mathbf{y}}\right\|_2^2 = 2\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\left(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\mathrm{norm}} - \tilde{\mathbf{y}}\right).$$

This gradient is generally **not** proportional to $\mathbf{1}$, and the normalized weights do not satisfy the KKT conditions for the constrained problem.

^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: This follows directly from the linear algebra of the gradient computation. Scaling a weight vector changes the predicted spectral values $\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w}$, and the residual gradient is not invariant to this scaling unless the design matrix has a special structure (which is not assumed in general).]

An equivalent argument can be given for the NNLS solution. The NNLS problem solves:

$$\min_{\mathbf{w}} \|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w} - \tilde{\mathbf{y}}\|_2^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad w_k \geq 0,$$

and at optimality, the KKT conditions require that the gradient of the objective is orthogonal to the feasible cone at $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_0$. Normalizing this solution changes the geometry of the problem: the normalized weights now lie on the standard simplex (where all weights sum to one), but the gradient condition for this new feasible set is different from the original gradient condition for the non-negativity cone.

^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: This reflects the general principle that constrained optimization solutions are sensitive to changes in the constraint set. Changing from a cone (non-negativity) to a simplex (sum-to-one) alters the KKT conditions fundamentally.]

Post-hoc normalization is mathematically justified (produces a feasible solution) but is **not generally optimal** for the constrained problem. The normalized solution will satisfy the sum-to-one constraint, but it will not minimize the residual sum of squares over the simplex-constrained feasible region.

^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: This is an unambiguous conclusion from constrained optimization theory. Normalization produces a feasible point, but feasibility does not imply optimality. The optimality conditions differ between the unconstrained and constrained problems.]

However, post-hoc normalization may be justified under specific mathematical conditions. If the unconstrained solution $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_0$ happens to satisfy $\sum_{k=1}^{K} \hat{w}_{0,k} = 1$ already (which occurs when the data and design matrix have special structure), then normalization is a no-operation, and the solution is trivially optimal. More generally, if the unconstrained solution is close to the sum-to-one constraint and the objective function is nearly flat in the direction orthogonal to the constraint, then the normalized solution may be a good approximation to the true constrained optimum.

^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: These conditions represent special cases in which normalization produces a good approximation. However, assessing when these conditions hold requires problem-specific analysis and cannot be guaranteed in general for arbitrary spectral-matching problems.]

The practical conclusion is that for applications where the sum-to-one constraint is a requirement (as in logic-tree PSHA weight assignment), the constrained optimization problem should be solved directly rather than solved in unconstrained form and then normalized. Direct solution guarantees optimality, whereas normalization introduces a potential suboptimality gap whose magnitude depends on the data.

^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: This is the standard recommendation from convex optimization practice. Solving the constrained problem directly is more reliable and theoretically justified than post-hoc normalization.]

The mathematical treatment of the sum-to-one constraint as a linear equality constraint preserves the convexity of the optimization problem and ensures uniqueness of the global minimizer. The constraint can be incorporated seamlessly into quadratic programming formulations, and modern interior-point and active-set solvers handle such problems routinely with good numerical stability. The only practical considerations are the choice of algorithm and numerical precision of the solver, not the mathematical validity of the constrained formulation itself.

^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: Convex optimization theory guarantees that linear equality constraints preserve convexity of the feasible set when applied to convex objectives. The numerical stability of standard QP solvers is well established.]

In summary: post-hoc normalization is a feasible procedure that produces weights summing to one, but it does not generally preserve the optimality properties of the original unconstrained solution. The constrained problem with the sum-to-one requirement is distinct from the unconstrained problem, and solving it directly via constrained quadratic programming is the mathematically rigorous approach. Equivalence between the two approaches holds only in degenerate cases where the unconstrained solution already satisfies the constraint or where the problem structure ensures near-optimal solutions through normalization. For general spectral-matching applications, direct constrained optimization is the preferred method.

^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: This final conclusion synthesizes the mathematical analysis with practical recommendations. It is well-founded in constrained optimization theory and consistent with standard practice in numerical optimization.]
