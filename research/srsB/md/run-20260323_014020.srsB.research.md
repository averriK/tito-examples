# Epistemic Logic-Tree Weight Calibration with Unit-Sum Constraint

Ground-motion models (GMMs) constitute a principal source of epistemic uncertainty
in probabilistic seismic hazard analysis (PSHA). The weights assigned to each
alternative GMPE in the logic tree define a discrete probability distribution over
the space of candidate models, and their calibration is formulated as a constrained
optimization problem. The knowledge base provides a non-negative least-squares (NNLS)
baseline, in which the weight vector $\mathbf{w} = (w_1, \dots, w_N)^\top$ is
determined by minimizing the mean-square log-spectral error over the non-negative
orthant. [KB:spec.compile.research.md] The two sections below address the extension
of that baseline to the unit-sum-constrained case and the mathematical validity of
replacing the constrained solve with a simpler normalization step.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The introductory framing accurately describes the NNLS baseline as documented in spec.compile.research.md (SLOT 5 and SLOT 4) and correctly identifies the two research questions addressed in the slots. The description of GMM weights as a discrete probability distribution is consistent with the PSHA logic-tree formulation in the task CONTEXT.]

## SLOT 1: Extension of Error Minimization Formulation to the Unit-Sum Weight Constraint

### Baseline Non-Negative Least-Squares Formulation

The knowledge base formulates the weight-calibration problem as an NNLS minimization
over the non-negative orthant $\mathbb{R}^N_+$. Given the log-transformed design
matrix $\tilde{\mathbf{A}} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times N}$ with entries
$\tilde{A}_{ik} = \ln S_{a,k}(T_i)$ and the log-domain target vector
$\mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ with $y_i = \ln S_a^{\mathrm{obj}}(T_i)$, the NNLS
problem is: [KB:spec.compile.research.md]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The design matrix, target vector notation, and NNLS problem statement are reproduced directly from spec.compile.research.md SLOT 5. No extraneous claims are made.]

$$\min_{\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^N} \;\|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}\|_2^2
\quad \text{subject to} \quad w_k \geq 0, \quad k = 1, \dots, N.$$

The feasible set $\mathbb{R}^N_+$ is a closed convex cone. Because the objective is
strictly convex when $\tilde{\mathbf{A}}$ has full column rank (the Gram matrix
$\mathbf{G} = \tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\tilde{\mathbf{A}} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$
is then positive definite), the solution is unique. [KB:spec.compile.research.md] The
Lawson-Hanson active-set algorithm partitions the index set $\{1, \dots, N\}$ into a
passive set $\mathcal{P} = \{k : \hat{w}_k > 0\}$ and an active set
$\mathcal{Z} = \{k : \hat{w}_k = 0\}$, iterating until the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT)
optimality conditions are satisfied. [KB:spec.compile.research.md]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The uniqueness argument from positive-definiteness of the Gram matrix and the Lawson-Hanson algorithm description are both documented in spec.compile.research.md SLOT 5. The connection between full column rank and positive-definiteness is a standard linear algebra fact.]

The KKT conditions for the NNLS problem are necessary and sufficient for global
optimality at $\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{\mathrm{NNLS}}$:^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The NNLS KKT conditions as stated follow directly from standard convex optimization theory for quadratic programs with non-negativity constraints and are consistent with the Lawson-Hanson characterization in the KB.]

$$\left[2\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\!\left(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{\mathrm{NNLS}}
- \mathbf{y}\right)\right]_k = 0 \quad \forall\, k \in \mathcal{P},$$

$$\left[2\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\!\left(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{\mathrm{NNLS}}
- \mathbf{y}\right)\right]_k \geq 0 \quad \forall\, k \in \mathcal{Z}.$$

### Constrained Problem with Unit-Sum Equality

The unit-sum requirement $\sum_{k=1}^N w_k = 1$ appends a single linear equality
constraint to the NNLS formulation. Writing $\mathbf{1} \in \mathbb{R}^N$ for the
all-ones vector, the fully constrained problem is:^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The problem statement directly combines the NNLS non-negativity constraint from the KB with the normalization condition $\sum w_k = 1$ specified in the task CONTEXT. The formulation is standard for simplex-constrained quadratic programs.]

$$\min_{\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^N} \;\|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}\|_2^2
\quad \text{subject to} \quad \mathbf{1}^\top\mathbf{w} = 1, \quad w_k \geq 0
\;\text{for all}\; k = 1, \dots, N. \tag{P}$$

The feasible set of (P) is the probability simplex^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The identification of the feasible set as the probability simplex follows immediately from the two constraints in (P): non-negativity and unit sum. This is a standard definition in convex analysis requiring no additional justification.]

$$\Delta_N = \left\{\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^N : \mathbf{1}^\top\mathbf{w} = 1,\;
w_k \geq 0\right\},$$

a compact convex polytope. Compactness ensures existence of a minimizer; strict
convexity of the objective (under full column rank) ensures uniqueness. The
unit-sum constraint reduces the effective dimension of the feasible set from $N-1$
degrees of freedom in the NNLS cone to $N-1$ degrees of freedom on the simplex, the
difference being the enforcement of an exact total mass of one. [DOI:10.5705/ss.202016.0220][DOI:10.3389/feart.2022.1067802]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The compactness of the simplex, the existence-uniqueness argument, and the degree-of-freedom count are all standard results in convex analysis. The external citations (Li et al. 2020 and Jang et al. 2023) confirm that this simplex formulation is treated as the standard framework for constrained least-squares weight estimation in related applied settings.]

### Lagrangian Formulation

The Lagrangian of (P) is constructed by introducing a scalar multiplier
$\nu \in \mathbb{R}$ for the equality constraint $\mathbf{1}^\top\mathbf{w} = 1$ and
a non-negative multiplier vector $\boldsymbol{\mu} \in \mathbb{R}^N$ (with
$\mu_k \geq 0$) for the $N$ non-negativity constraints $w_k \geq 0$:^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: Standard Lagrangian construction for a QP with one equality and N inequality constraints. The sign convention (subtracting mu'w for the non-negativity constraints written as w >= 0) is the standard form used in active-set QP theory.]

$$\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w},\,\nu,\,\boldsymbol{\mu}) = \|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w} -
\mathbf{y}\|_2^2 + \nu\!\left(\mathbf{1}^\top\mathbf{w} - 1\right) -
\boldsymbol{\mu}^\top\mathbf{w}.$$

Because the objective is convex and the constraints are affine, Slater's constraint
qualification holds whenever the relative interior of $\Delta_N$ is non-empty (which
requires $N \geq 2$), guaranteeing that KKT conditions are both necessary and
sufficient for global optimality. [DOI:10.5705/ss.202016.0220]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: Slater's condition for affinely constrained convex programs is a standard result in convex optimization. The requirement N >= 2 for the relative interior of the simplex to be non-empty is elementary. The KKT necessity and sufficiency under Slater's condition is a classical theorem.]

### Karush-Kuhn-Tucker Optimality Conditions

Computing the gradient of $\mathcal{L}$ with respect to $\mathbf{w}$ and setting it
to zero yields the stationarity condition. Together with primal feasibility, dual
feasibility, and complementary slackness, the complete KKT system characterizing the
unique global minimizer $\mathbf{w}^*$ of (P) is:^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The four conditions below follow directly from differentiation of the Lagrangian and the standard KKT theorem for constrained convex programs. Each condition is algebraically exact.]

$$2\left[\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\!\left(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w}^* -
\mathbf{y}\right)\right]_k + \nu^* = \mu_k^* \geq 0,
\quad k = 1, \dots, N, \tag{KKT-S}$$

$$\sum_{k=1}^N w_k^* = 1, \quad w_k^* \geq 0 \quad \forall\, k, \tag{KKT-P}$$

$$\mu_k^* w_k^* = 0 \quad \forall\, k. \tag{KKT-CS}$$

### Geometric Interpretation and Gradient Equalization

The complementary slackness condition (KKT-CS) partitions the branch set into the
passive set $\mathcal{P}^* = \{k : w_k^* > 0\}$ and the active set
$\mathcal{Z}^* = \{k : w_k^* = 0\}$. For each $k \in \mathcal{P}^*$, the condition
$\mu_k^* = 0$ reduces the stationarity equation (KKT-S) to the equalization
condition:^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The gradient-equalization property on the active set follows directly by substituting mu_k = 0 into (KKT-S). This is an exact algebraic implication of the KKT conditions; no approximation is involved.]

$$\left[2\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\!\left(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w}^* -
\mathbf{y}\right)\right]_k = -\nu^* \quad \forall\, k \in \mathcal{P}^*.$$

For each $k \in \mathcal{Z}^*$, dual feasibility requires $\mu_k^* \geq 0$, and substituting into (KKT-S) gives:^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The inequality for inactive components is an exact algebraic consequence of dual feasibility (mu_k* >= 0) inserted into the stationarity equation (KKT-S): the equation gives [gradient]_k + nu* = mu_k* >= 0, which rearranges directly to the displayed bound.]

$$\left[2\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\!\left(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w}^* -
\mathbf{y}\right)\right]_k \geq -\nu^* \quad \forall\, k \in \mathcal{Z}^*.$$

This gradient-equalization structure on the passive set is the essential difference
between the simplex-constrained problem (P) and the NNLS baseline: the NNLS KKT
conditions require the passive-set gradient components to equal zero, whereas the
simplex KKT conditions require them to equal a common constant $-\nu^*$ that is in
general nonzero. [KB:spec.compile.research.md][DOI:10.5705/ss.202016.0220]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The contrast between zero-gradient (NNLS) and equal-gradient (simplex) on the passive set follows rigorously from the two KKT systems. The NNLS condition (zero gradient on active/passive set) is explicit in the KB (SLOT 5), and the simplex condition is derived from the Lagrangian above. The distinction is exact and well-supported by the cited external literature on simplex-constrained optimization.]

### Closed-Form for the Equality-Only Relaxation

When no non-negativity constraint is binding (all candidate models receive positive
weight at the optimum), a closed-form solution is available by solving the
stationarity condition $2\mathbf{G}\mathbf{w} - 2\mathbf{h} + \nu\mathbf{1} = \mathbf{0}$
jointly with $\mathbf{1}^\top\mathbf{w} = 1$, where
$\mathbf{G} = \tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\tilde{\mathbf{A}}$ and
$\mathbf{h} = \tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\mathbf{y}$, and $\hat{\mathbf{w}} = \mathbf{G}^{-1}\mathbf{h}$
denotes the unconstrained OLS solution. Substituting $\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{G}^{-1}(\mathbf{h} - \frac{\nu}{2}\mathbf{1})$
into the equality constraint and solving for $\nu$ gives:^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The closed-form derivation is a standard Lagrangian calculation for an equality-constrained QP with positive-definite objective; no external citations are needed beyond the standard convex optimization framework already established. The steps are algebraically straightforward and exact.]

$$\nu^* = \frac{2\!\left(\mathbf{1}^\top\hat{\mathbf{w}} - 1\right)}
{\mathbf{1}^\top\mathbf{G}^{-1}\mathbf{1}}, \qquad
\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{eq}} = \hat{\mathbf{w}} -
\frac{\mathbf{1}^\top\hat{\mathbf{w}} - 1}
{\mathbf{1}^\top\mathbf{G}^{-1}\mathbf{1}}\,\mathbf{G}^{-1}\mathbf{1}.$$

The vector $\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{eq}}$ is the projection of the unconstrained OLS
solution $\hat{\mathbf{w}}$ onto the hyperplane $\mathbf{1}^\top\mathbf{w} = 1$ under
the inner product induced by $\mathbf{G}$ (the Gram-metric projection). When
$\hat{\mathbf{w}} \in \Delta_N$, the equality constraint is inactive, $\nu^* = 0$,
and $\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{eq}} = \hat{\mathbf{w}} = \mathbf{w}^*$. When
$\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{eq}} \geq \mathbf{0}$ but $\hat{\mathbf{w}} \notin \Delta_N$,
then $\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{eq}} = \mathbf{w}^*$ is the unique simplex-constrained
optimum. When some components of $\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{eq}}$ are negative, those
components bind at zero and an active-set iteration is required. [DOI:10.1007/BF02591962]^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The Gram-metric projection interpretation and the conditional equivalence are standard results from constrained QP theory. MEDIUM rather than HIGH because the closed-form relies on G being invertible (full column rank assumption), which may not hold in practice for highly correlated candidate GMPEs; the active-set iteration required in the binding case is described at a high level and not derived in detail here. The Goldfarb-Idnani reference (DOI:10.1007/BF02591962) supports the active-set resolution approach.]

### Computational Implementation

The fully constrained problem (P) is a strictly convex quadratic program with $N$
inequality constraints and one equality constraint. The Goldfarb-Idnani dual
active-set method, implemented in the R function `quadprog::solve.QP`, handles
equality constraints directly through the `meq` argument (setting `meq = 1` to treat
the first column of `Amat` as an equality constraint); this approach has been adopted
in recent seismological studies combining candidate GMPEs under the unit-sum
requirement. [DOI:10.3389/feart.2022.1067802][DOI:10.1007/978-3-031-11898-2_69][DOI:10.1007/BF02591962]
The R `lsei` package, which solves minimum-norm problems subject to simultaneous
equality and inequality constraints via the Lawson-Hanson extension, provides an
equivalent path. [KB:spec.compile.research.md] For the general penalized case --
including ridge ($\lambda_2 > 0$) or Lasso ($\lambda_1 > 0$) terms as defined in the
knowledge base -- the CVXR disciplined convex programming interface handles the
simplex constraint by adding `list(w >= 0, sum(w) == 1)` to the constraint block,
dispatching to interior-point solvers such as CLARABEL or OSQP. [KB:spec.compile.research.md]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The Goldfarb-Idnani implementation and its seismological application are well-supported by the cited DOIs (Jang et al. 2023 and Kwak et al. 2022). The lsei and CVXR descriptions are consistent with the KB (SLOT 7 documents CVXR; the Lawson-Hanson extension is well-known). No unsupported claims are made.]

## SLOT 2: Validity of Post-hoc Normalization versus Constrained Optimization

### The Normalization Heuristic

An operationally simpler alternative to solving (P) is to solve the NNLS problem (non-negativity
only) and then rescale the solution by its $\ell_1$ norm. For $S = \mathbf{1}^\top\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{\mathrm{NNLS}} > 0$,
the normalized vector is:^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The description of the post-hoc normalization procedure is factually standard. The notation is consistent with the KB and with the normalization approach documented in the ensemble learning literature (DOI:10.2202/1544-6115.1309).]

$$\tilde{\mathbf{w}} = \frac{\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{\mathrm{NNLS}}}{S} =
\frac{\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{\mathrm{NNLS}}}{\mathbf{1}^\top\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{\mathrm{NNLS}}}.$$

By construction, $\tilde{\mathbf{w}}$ satisfies $\mathbf{1}^\top\tilde{\mathbf{w}} = 1$
and $\tilde{w}_k \geq 0$, so it is a feasible point of (P). The central question is
whether this feasible point coincides with the unique minimizer $\mathbf{w}^*$, i.e.,
whether $\tilde{\mathbf{w}} = \mathbf{w}^*$.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The feasibility of the normalized vector follows trivially from division by a positive L1 norm and non-negativity of the NNLS solution. The question of coincidence with the constrained optimum is the precise question posed in the slot.]

### KKT Verification at the Normalized Point

To determine whether $\tilde{\mathbf{w}} = \mathbf{w}^*$, it suffices to verify
whether $\tilde{\mathbf{w}}$ satisfies the KKT conditions (KKT-S)--(KKT-CS) of (P).
Normalization preserves the zero-nonzero structure of $\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{\mathrm{NNLS}}$
(since $S > 0$), so the passive and active sets of $\tilde{\mathbf{w}}$ and
$\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{\mathrm{NNLS}}$ are identical: $\mathcal{P} = \{k : \hat{w}_k^{\mathrm{NNLS}} > 0\}$.
The residual at $\tilde{\mathbf{w}}$ decomposes as:^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The observation that normalization preserves the support (zero-nonzero structure) follows from S > 0 and non-negativity. The algebraic decomposition below is an exact identity from linearity of the matrix-vector product.]

$$\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\tilde{\mathbf{w}} - \mathbf{y} = \frac{1}{S}
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{\mathrm{NNLS}} - \mathbf{y} =
\frac{1}{S}\!\left(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{\mathrm{NNLS}} - \mathbf{y}\right)
+ \left(\frac{1}{S} - 1\right)\mathbf{y}.$$

Applying $\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top$ and using the NNLS KKT condition
$[\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{\mathrm{NNLS}} - \mathbf{y})]_k = 0$
for $k \in \mathcal{P}$, the $k$-th component of the gradient at $\tilde{\mathbf{w}}$
reduces for each passive-set index to:^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The gradient at the normalized point is computed exactly by applying the NNLS passive-set KKT condition (zero gradient) to the decomposed residual. The result is an algebraically exact expression with no approximation.]

$$\left[\nabla f(\tilde{\mathbf{w}})\right]_k =
2\left(\frac{1}{S} - 1\right)[\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\mathbf{y}]_k,
\quad k \in \mathcal{P}.$$

For $\tilde{\mathbf{w}}$ to satisfy the simplex KKT stationarity condition (KKT-S)
with $\mu_k^* = 0$ for $k \in \mathcal{P}$, this expression must equal a single
constant $-\nu^*$ for all $k \in \mathcal{P}$. Since the prefactor $2(1/S - 1)$ is
a scalar common to all $k$, the requirement reduces to: the cross-correlation
$[\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\mathbf{y}]_k = \sum_{i=1}^n \tilde{A}_{ik} y_i$ must be
identical for every $k \in \mathcal{P}$.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The constancy requirement for the simplex KKT stationarity condition follows rigorously from the gradient expression derived above: the expression 2(1/S - 1)[A'y]_k must be constant over P, and because the prefactor is a nonzero scalar (when S != 1), the requirement reduces to the constancy of [A'y]_k. No approximation is involved.]

### Conditions for Equivalence

The analysis identifies two and only two conditions under which the post-hoc
normalized solution $\tilde{\mathbf{w}}$ is guaranteed to equal the constrained
optimum $\mathbf{w}^*$. [DOI:10.5705/ss.202016.0220]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The two conditions below are derived rigorously from the KKT analysis above. They are necessary and sufficient for the simplex KKT conditions to hold at the normalized point, given the gradient expression established in the preceding paragraph.]

The first condition is $S = 1$: the NNLS solution already satisfies the unit-sum
constraint, so $\tilde{\mathbf{w}} = \hat{\mathbf{w}}^{\mathrm{NNLS}}$ and no
rescaling occurs. Inserting $\tilde{\mathbf{w}} = \hat{\mathbf{w}}^{\mathrm{NNLS}}$
with $S = 1$ into the gradient expression gives
$[\nabla f(\tilde{\mathbf{w}})]_k = 0$ for all $k \in \mathcal{P}$, which satisfies
the simplex KKT condition with $\nu^* = 0$ and $\mu_k^* = 0$. For the inactive set,
the NNLS condition $[\nabla f(\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{\mathrm{NNLS}})]_k \geq 0$
translates to $[\nabla f(\tilde{\mathbf{w}})]_k \geq 0 = -\nu^*$, also satisfying
(KKT-S). All four KKT conditions are therefore met, confirming
$\tilde{\mathbf{w}} = \mathbf{w}^*$ when $S = 1$.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The verification is complete and algebraically rigorous: all four KKT conditions (stationarity, primal feasibility, dual feasibility, complementary slackness) are explicitly checked at the NNLS solution when S = 1, with nu* = 0. Uniqueness of the constrained optimum then identifies this point as w*.]

The second condition is that $[\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\mathbf{y}]_k$ is the same
constant for all $k \in \mathcal{P}$, i.e., every active candidate model has
identical cross-correlation with the target log-spectral vector $\mathbf{y}$. Under
this condition, the prefactor $2(1/S - 1)$ yields a uniform constant
$-\nu^* = 2(1/S - 1)[\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\mathbf{y}]_k$, and the simplex KKT
conditions are satisfied with the corresponding $\nu^*$. This is an exceptional
structural coincidence: physically distinct GMPEs have distinct spectral shapes and
anelastic-attenuation characteristics, producing different cross-correlations with
any given target spectrum under realistic regional conditions.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The cross-correlation constancy condition is derived algebraically from the gradient expression above. The physical remark about distinct GMPEs is well-motivated: the entire premise of the GMPE logic-tree framework is that candidate models differ in their predictions, which directly implies non-uniform cross-correlations with a fixed target under generic conditions.]

### General Non-Equivalence

Outside the two exceptional cases above, the gradient of the objective at
$\tilde{\mathbf{w}}$ is not constant over $\mathcal{P}$, and therefore $\tilde{\mathbf{w}}$
does not satisfy the KKT stationarity condition (KKT-S) of problem (P). By the
uniqueness of the constrained optimum, $\tilde{\mathbf{w}} \neq \mathbf{w}^*$.
Post-hoc normalization thus yields a feasible but generically sub-optimal point: the
constraints are satisfied, but the objective $\|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\tilde{\mathbf{w}} - \mathbf{y}\|_2^2$
exceeds the minimum $\|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w}^* - \mathbf{y}\|_2^2$. The two
problems require fundamentally different mathematical treatments because they optimize
over different feasible sets ($\mathbb{R}^N_+$ versus $\Delta_N$) and their respective
optimality conditions impose different constraints on the gradient at the passive set.
[DOI:10.3389/feart.2022.1067802][DOI:10.5705/ss.202016.0220][ARXIV:1605.00507]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The non-equivalence conclusion follows rigorously and directly from the KKT analysis: a point that does not satisfy the necessary-and-sufficient KKT conditions of a strictly convex QP cannot be its optimum. The citations (Jang et al. 2023, Li et al. 2020, Li/Rangapuram/Slawski arXiv:1605.00507) provide independent external corroboration that simplex-constrained LS is treated as a structurally distinct problem from NNLS in the literature.]

A further structural distinction reinforces this conclusion. The NNLS solution on
$\mathbb{R}^N_+$ has at most $\mathrm{rank}(\tilde{\mathbf{A}})$ nonzero components,
because the unrestricted OLS sub-problem solved on the passive set $\mathcal{P}$ at
each Lawson-Hanson iteration has at most $\mathrm{rank}(\tilde{\mathbf{A}})$
independent equations. [ARXIV:1205.0953][ARXIV:2410.03014] The simplex-constrained
problem (P) adds one binding equality constraint, permitting the optimal passive set
to contain up to $\mathrm{rank}(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}) + 1$ strictly positive components.
Because the two problems operate on generically different active sets, a post-hoc
rescaling of the NNLS solution will not produce a point satisfying the stationarity
conditions of the simplex problem except under the coincidences identified above.^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The NNLS sparsity bound (at most rank(A) nonzero components) is consistent with results in Slawski & Hein (2013) (arXiv:1205.0953) and Yang & Hastie (2024) (arXiv:2410.03014). The claim that the simplex-constrained solution may have up to rank+1 nonzero components follows from the addition of one equality constraint to the active-set structure; this is standard in active-set QP theory but not stated verbatim in the cited sources, so MEDIUM rather than HIGH confidence is appropriate.]

### Practical Consequence for Seismic Hazard Weights

The distinction between the two approaches carries practical significance in PSHA.
The mean hazard curve $\bar{\lambda}_I(i^*) = \sum_{k=1}^N w_k\,\lambda_I^{(k)}(i^*)$
depends on the weight vector, and deviations from the optimum $\mathbf{w}^*$ alter
the relative contribution of each GMPE to the epistemic-uncertainty band,
particularly at low annual exceedance rates where divergence among candidate GMPEs is
largest. [KB:spec.compile.research.md][DOI:10.3389/feart.2022.1067802] The
normalization error is most pronounced when $S = \mathbf{1}^\top\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{\mathrm{NNLS}}$
deviates substantially from unity and when the cross-correlations
$[\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\mathbf{y}]_k$ vary considerably across the passive set. In
the PSHA context, where the unit-sum constraint carries a probability-distribution
interpretation, the simplex-constrained QP (P) is the mathematically correct
formulation, and post-hoc normalization is an approximation whose bias with respect
to $\mathbf{w}^*$ depends on the degree of violation of the two equivalence
conditions identified above. [DOI:10.3389/feart.2022.1067802][DOI:10.1007/978-3-031-11898-2_69][DOI:10.1785/0120080347]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The practical significance argument is grounded in the PSHA mean hazard formula from the task CONTEXT and supported by the seismological literature (Jang et al. 2023, Kwak et al. 2022, Scherbaum et al. 2009), which formulates GMM weight calibration as a proper constrained QP rather than normalized NNLS. The identification of S deviation and cross-correlation variability as the drivers of the normalization error follows from the gradient expression derived in the KKT analysis.]
