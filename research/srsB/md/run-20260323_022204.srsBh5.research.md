# Weight Calibration with Sum-to-One Normalization: Extended Formulations and Validation

## SLOT 1: Extend weight calibration formulation for sum-to-one constraint

The weight calibration problem in spectral matching combines multiple candidate records into a weighted linear combination that approximates a target spectrum. The logical framework described in the knowledge base develops unconstrained and constrained optimizations without explicit sum-to-one normalization. To properly represent the weights as a discrete probability distribution over candidate ground-motion prediction equations (GMPEs), the weights must satisfy the equality constraint $\sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k = 1$. This slot extends the formulations from the knowledge base to incorporate this normalization constraint. ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The KB document provides detailed mathematical formulations for spectral matching through SLOT 7, and the constraint sum of weights equals one is a standard requirement in probability theory and epistemic logic tree construction. The extension is straightforward application of constrained optimization principles.]

### Extended Classical OLS Formulation with Sum-to-One Constraint

The classical unconstrained OLS problem from the knowledge base minimizes the residual sum of squares without restricting weight magnitudes. [KB:spec.compile.research.md] To impose both non-negativity and sum-to-one normalization, the extended formulation becomes: ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The classical OLS formulation is well-established in the KB. Adding an equality constraint and non-negativity constraint is a standard extension in constrained optimization, and the simplex constraint is the natural domain for probability distributions.]

$$\min_{\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^K} \|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{b}\|_2^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad \sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k = 1, \quad w_k \geq 0 \; \text{for all} \; k.$$

Here $\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times K}$ contains the spectral ordinates of $K$ candidate records at $n$ period points, $\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is the target spectrum vector, and $\mathbf{w}$ is the non-negative weight vector. The feasible region is a simplex, the standard probability simplex $\Delta_K = \{\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^K : \sum w_k = 1, w_k \geq 0\}$. Since the objective is a convex quadratic and the constraint set is a convex polytope, a unique global minimum exists when $\mathbf{A}$ has full column rank. ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: This formulation is a standard constrained least-squares problem. The objective function is strictly convex (Hessian is $2\mathbf{A}^\top\mathbf{A}$, positive definite when $\mathbf{A}$ has full rank), and the simplex constraint set is compact and convex, guaranteeing existence and uniqueness of the global minimizer.]

The KKT optimality conditions at the solution $\hat{\mathbf{w}}$ are:

$$2\mathbf{A}^\top(\mathbf{A}\hat{\mathbf{w}} - \mathbf{b}) = \lambda \mathbf{1} + \boldsymbol{\mu},$$

where $\lambda$ is the multiplier for the equality constraint $\sum w_k = 1$, $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ is the vector of multipliers for the non-negativity constraints $w_k \geq 0$, $\mathbf{1}$ is the vector of ones, and complementary slackness requires $\mu_k w_k = 0$ and $\mu_k \geq 0$ for all $k$. At any index where $\hat{w}_k > 0$, the multiplier $\mu_k = 0$ and the stationarity condition reduces to $2(\mathbf{A}^\top(\mathbf{A}\hat{\mathbf{w}} - \mathbf{b}))_k = \lambda$, meaning the gradient of the objective is identical for all positive-weight records. ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The KKT conditions are a direct consequence of Lagrangian analysis for constrained optimization. The stationarity condition shows that at the optimum, all records with positive weight have equal marginal cost, a fundamental principle of constrained optimization in probability spaces.]

### Extended Log-Log OLS with Sum-to-One Constraint

The log-log formulation [KB:spec.compile.research.md] transforms both spectral ordinates and periods to logarithmic scales, improving homoscedasticity and alignment with PSHA conventions. With sum-to-one normalization, the extended problem is:

$$\min_{\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^K} \|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w} - \tilde{\mathbf{y}}\|_2^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad \sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k = 1, \quad w_k \geq 0 \; \text{for all} \; k,$$

where $\tilde{\mathbf{A}} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times K}$ contains $\tilde{A}_{ik} = \ln S_{a,k}(T_i)$ and $\tilde{\mathbf{y}} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ contains $\tilde{y}_i = \ln S_a^{\mathrm{obj}}(T_i)$. The weighted spectral combination in the original domain becomes a geometric mean:

$$\hat{S}_a(T_i) = \prod_{k=1}^{K} S_{a,k}(T_i)^{w_k}.$$

This geometric weighting emerges naturally from the log-space formulation when weights are constrained to sum to one. [KB:spec.compile.research.md] The combination now represents a probabilistic mixture over candidate spectra in log-normal space, which aligns with the distributional model of ground-motion variability. ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The log-log formulation is established practice in PSHA [KB:spec.compile.research.md]. When weights are constrained to sum to one, the exponents satisfy the normalization constraint, and the resulting geometric mean is a standard way to combine log-normal distributions with specified weights. The interpretation as a probability mixture is mathematically sound.]

### Extended NNLS with Sum-to-One Constraint

The non-negative least-squares (NNLS) formulation [KB:spec.compile.research.md] enforces $w_k \geq 0$ to ensure physical plausibility. Adding the sum-to-one constraint yields:

$$\min_{\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^K} \|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w} - \tilde{\mathbf{y}}\|_2^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad \sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k = 1, \quad w_k \geq 0 \; \text{for all} \; k.$$

This is a non-negative least-squares problem restricted to the probability simplex. Computationally, this problem can be solved via active-set methods adapted for simplex constraints, or by interior-point methods. The active-set approach partitions the index set into a free set $\mathcal{F}$ (indices with $w_k > 0$) and a zero set $\mathcal{Z}$ (indices with $w_k = 0$), and iteratively solves the unconstrained OLS problem restricted to $\mathcal{F}$ while enforcing the sum-to-one constraint on $\mathcal{F}$ only. [KB:spec.compile.research.md] ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: NNLS algorithms are well-established in numerical optimization. Restricting to the simplex is a standard modification; the active-set principle extends directly to equality constraints combined with inequality bounds. The computational feasibility is assured by convexity of both the objective and the simplex constraint set.]

Importantly, the zero set $\mathcal{Z}$ now represents records that contribute zero weight to the final combination, and the simplex constraint ensures the selected records (those in $\mathcal{F}$) contribute exactly unit probability. This sparse-selection property is preserved when sum-to-one normalization is added. ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The sparse-selection behavior of active-set NNLS algorithms is well-documented. The addition of the sum-to-one constraint does not interfere with this sparsity pattern; the constraint is enforced only on the free set, preserving the automatic record elimination mechanism.]

### Extended Bounded-Weight LS with Sum-to-One Constraint

The bounded-weight formulation [KB:spec.compile.research.md] imposes lower and upper bounds $w_{\min}$ and $w_{\max}$ on each weight to prevent single-record dominance and ensure minimum participation. With sum-to-one normalization, the constraints must be reconciled. The sum-to-one constraint typically dominates over per-weight bounds unless the bounds are chosen carefully. The extended formulation is:

$$\min_{\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^K} \|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w} - \tilde{\mathbf{y}}\|_2^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad \sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k = 1, \quad w_{\min} \leq w_k \leq w_{\max} \; \text{for all} \; k.$$

For feasibility, the constraints must satisfy $K \cdot w_{\min} \leq 1 \leq K \cdot w_{\max}$, or equivalently $w_{\min} \leq 1/K \leq w_{\max}$. When $w_{\min} = 1/K$ and $w_{\max} = 1/K$, the problem collapses to uniform weighting. More generally, the feasible region is a compact convex polytope formed by the intersection of the probability simplex with the box $[w_{\min}, w_{\max}]^K$, and the unique global minimizer is guaranteed to exist. [KB:spec.compile.research.md] ^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The feasibility condition $K \cdot w_{\min} \leq 1 \leq K \cdot w_{\max}$ is mathematically sound; it ensures the simplex and box constraints have non-empty intersection. However, specific choices of $w_{\min}$ and $w_{\max}$ in practical applications require domain knowledge, and the interaction between bounds and the sum-to-one constraint is less commonly discussed in the literature.]

### Extended Convex Optimization with Sum-to-One Constraint

The general convex optimization framework [KB:spec.compile.research.md] combines a squared-error fidelity term with Lasso ($\ell_1$) and Ridge ($\ell_2$) penalty terms. With sum-to-one normalization, the extended formulation becomes:

$$\min_{\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^K} \left(\|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w} - \tilde{\mathbf{y}}\|_2^2 + \lambda_1 \|\mathbf{w}\|_1 + \lambda_2 \|\mathbf{w}\|_2^2\right) \quad \text{subject to} \quad \sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k = 1, \quad w_k \geq 0.$$

The objective is a sum of convex functions: the quadratic fidelity term (convex, Hessian $2\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\tilde{\mathbf{A}}$ positive semidefinite), the $\ell_1$ norm (convex), and the squared $\ell_2$ norm (strictly convex when $\lambda_2 > 0$). The constraint set is the non-negative simplex, which is closed and convex. Therefore, any local minimum is a global minimum, and standard interior-point solvers (CLARABEL, SCS, OSQP) converge to the unique global solution in polynomial time. [KB:spec.compile.research.md] ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The convexity analysis follows directly from composition rules for convex functions. The sum of convex functions is convex, and the non-negative simplex is a convex polytope. The claim about solver convergence is well-established in convex optimization theory, and the specific solvers mentioned are reliable implementations of standard algorithms.]

When the Lasso penalty $\lambda_1 > 0$, the $\ell_1$ norm interacts with the simplex constraint to produce sparse solutions: many weights are driven to exactly zero, and the positive-weight records form an approximate minimum-cardinality solution. This combined effect is more subtle than either penalty alone and depends critically on the penalty coefficients and the signal-to-noise properties of the spectral data. ^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The interaction between Lasso penalties and simplex constraints is less thoroughly documented in the standard literature than unconstrained Lasso or simplex-constrained least-squares alone. The statement about sparse solutions is sound in principle, but the specific behavior depends on problem-dependent factors not fully characterized in general theory.]

## SLOT 2: Validate normalization approach and justify mathematical treatment

A practitioner may obtain an unconstrained optimal solution $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\mathrm{unc}}$ from one of the formulations above (without the sum-to-one constraint), then post-hoc normalize by dividing by the sum: $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\mathrm{norm}} = \hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\mathrm{unc}} / \sum_k \hat{w}_{\mathrm{unc},k}$. The question is whether this normalized solution $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\mathrm{norm}}$ is mathematically equivalent to solving the constrained problem directly, or whether the two approaches yield different results. ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: This is a well-defined mathematical question with a precise answer. The validity of post-hoc normalization depends on the structure of the objective function and constraints, and can be rigorously analyzed using optimization theory.]

### Post-Hoc Normalization Does Not Preserve Unconstrained Optimality

The fundamental issue is that the unconstrained optimum $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\mathrm{unc}}$ satisfies a different stationarity condition than the constrained optimum. For the unconstrained quadratic objective $f(\mathbf{w}) = \|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w} - \tilde{\mathbf{y}}\|_2^2$, the optimality condition is:

$$\nabla f(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\mathrm{unc}}) = 2\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\mathrm{unc}} - \tilde{\mathbf{y}}) = \mathbf{0}.$$

This means the gradient is zero at every component. However, when we normalize by setting $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\mathrm{norm},k} = \hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\mathrm{unc},k} / \sum_j \hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\mathrm{unc},j}$, the resulting weights do not satisfy the stationarity condition for the constrained problem. The constrained optimality condition (from KKT theory) requires:

$$\nabla f(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\mathrm{const}}) = \lambda \mathbf{1} + \boldsymbol{\mu},$$

where $\lambda$ is the Lagrange multiplier for the equality constraint and $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ is the vector of multipliers for non-negativity. At any index $k$ with $\hat{w}_{\mathrm{const},k} > 0$, the gradient component must equal $\lambda$, meaning all positive-weight records have identical gradient values. ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: These are direct consequences of Lagrangian stationarity conditions in convex optimization. The unconstrained gradient must be zero everywhere, while the constrained gradient must be balanced across all positive-weight indices. These are fundamentally different conditions, so the unconstrained solution cannot generically satisfy the constrained conditions without special structure.]

### Fundamental Incompatibility with Least-Squares Objectives

Consider a simple illustrative case: $K = 2$ records and $n = 1$ period point. The design matrix is $\tilde{\mathbf{A}} = [a_1, a_2] \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times 2}$ (a row vector), and the target is $\tilde{y} \in \mathbb{R}$. The unconstrained OLS solution minimizes $f(w_1, w_2) = (a_1 w_1 + a_2 w_2 - \tilde{y})^2$, yielding the stationarity condition:

$$2a_1(a_1 w_1 + a_2 w_2 - \tilde{y}) = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad 2a_2(a_1 w_1 + a_2 w_2 - \tilde{y}) = 0.$$

If both $a_1 \neq 0$ and $a_2 \neq 0$, both equations imply $a_1 w_1 + a_2 w_2 = \tilde{y}$, and the unconstrained problem has infinitely many solutions (the entire line $a_1 w_1 + a_2 w_2 = \tilde{y}$ in $(w_1, w_2)$-space). Any point on this line can be normalized to lie on the sum-to-one simplex $w_1 + w_2 = 1$. The constrained problem, by contrast, selects a unique point on the simplex that also satisfies $a_1 w_1 + a_2 w_2 = \tilde{y}$ (if the line intersects the simplex). Post-hoc normalization of an unconstrained solution from the infinite solution set will generally not yield the constrained optimum unless special alignment conditions hold. ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: This example illustrates a fundamental issue: in underdetermined or weakly determined settings, the unconstrained problem has multiple solutions, and normalizing one arbitrary solution will not recover the constrained optimum. The logic is sound for the special case and generalizes to higher dimensions.]

### Necessary and Sufficient Conditions for Equivalence

Normalization yields the same result as direct constrained optimization if and only if the unconstrained solution $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\mathrm{unc}}$ lies in a homogeneous cone aligned with the constraint. More precisely, if the objective function $f(\mathbf{w})$ is homogeneous of degree $d$ (meaning $f(c \mathbf{w}) = c^d f(\mathbf{w})$ for all $c > 0$), then the gradient also scales homogeneously: $\nabla f(c\mathbf{w}) = c^{d-1} \nabla f(\mathbf{w})$. For the standard quadratic objective in spectral matching, $f(\mathbf{w}) = \|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w} - \tilde{\mathbf{y}}\|_2^2$, this function is **not homogeneous** because of the constant term $-\tilde{\mathbf{y}}$. [KB:spec.compile.research.md] ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The least-squares objective includes a target vector $\tilde{\mathbf{y}}$ that does not scale with the weights. By definition, a homogeneous function of degree $d$ must satisfy $f(c\mathbf{w}) = c^d f(\mathbf{w})$ for all $c > 0$. Since the target term is fixed, this property fails. This is a fundamental structural property that does not depend on the specific data.]

In contrast, consider a modified objective that is a weighted sum of squared residuals relative to zero (rather than relative to a fixed target):

$$g(\mathbf{w}) = \|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w}\|_2^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n \left(\sum_{k=1}^K w_k \tilde{A}_{ik}\right)^2.$$

This function is homogeneous of degree 2. For such a function, $\nabla g(c\mathbf{w}) = c \nabla g(\mathbf{w})$, and if $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\mathrm{unc}}$ minimizes $g$, then so does any positive scalar multiple. Dividing by the sum of components (a positive scalar normalization) preserves optimality for the homogeneous problem $\min g(\mathbf{w})$ subject to $\sum w_k = 1$. However, this modified objective has no target term and is not useful for spectral matching, where the goal is to approximate a specific target spectrum $\tilde{\mathbf{y}}$. ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: Homogeneity is a well-defined property in optimization. The modified objective without a target term is homogeneous, but it is not the problem being solved in spectral matching. The statement that homogeneity would permit normalization to preserve optimality is mathematically correct but not applicable to the practical problem.]

### Empirical Consequence: Normalization Changes the Solution

In practical computation, normalizing an unconstrained solution yields a different objective value than solving the constrained problem directly. Denote the unconstrained optimum as $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\mathrm{unc}} = \arg\min_{\mathbf{w}} f(\mathbf{w})$, and let $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\mathrm{norm}} = \hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\mathrm{unc}} / \|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\mathrm{unc}}\|_1$ be its normalization to the simplex. The constrained optimum is $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\mathrm{const}} = \arg\min_{\mathbf{w} \in \Delta_K} f(\mathbf{w})$. In general, $f(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\mathrm{norm}}) > f(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\mathrm{const}})$ because the unconstrained minimum $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\mathrm{unc}}$ lies outside the simplex (in the unconstrained space $\mathbb{R}^K$), and projecting it onto the simplex increases the objective value. ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: This inequality follows from the definition of constrained optimization: the constrained minimum must have a smaller or equal objective value than any feasible point. Since the unconstrained optimum is outside the feasible region (the simplex), normalizing it yields an infeasible point that has worse objective value than the constrained optimum. This is a foundational principle of constrained optimization.]

### Correct Approach: Solve the Constrained Problem Directly

To obtain weights that sum to one while minimizing prediction error, the constrained problem must be solved directly using methods suited to simplex constraints. Algorithm options include:

1. **Active-set methods adapted for simplex constraints**, in which the algorithm iteratively identifies which weights are zero and which are positive, then solves an unconstrained OLS sub-problem on the positive-weight subset while enforcing the sum-to-one constraint on those components only.

2. **Interior-point methods** (barrier or primal-dual), which treat the simplex as a smooth manifold and follow a central path to the optimum, respecting the constraint throughout.

3. **Quadratic programming solvers** for the special case where no regularization penalties are used (e.g., using CVXR with CLARABEL or SCS, or using dedicated QP solvers like `quadprog` in R).

4. **Projected gradient or Frank-Wolfe methods**, which iteratively move within the simplex by projecting gradient steps onto the feasible region.

[KB:spec.compile.research.md] These algorithms guarantee that the resulting weights $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\mathrm{const}}$ satisfy the sum-to-one constraint exactly and minimize the objective over all feasible points. Post-hoc normalization of an unconstrained solution does not achieve this optimality. ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: These methods are standard in optimization and are implemented in modern software libraries. Their correctness is guaranteed by convex optimization theory: the problem is convex (convex objective, convex feasible set), so any algorithm that respects feasibility and converges to a KKT point finds the global minimum.]

### Summary: Mathematical Validity and Practical Recommendation

Post-hoc normalization of an unconstrained solution is **not mathematically equivalent** to solving the constrained problem directly. The unconstrained objective $f(\mathbf{w}) = \|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w} - \tilde{\mathbf{y}}\|_2^2$ is not homogeneous due to the fixed target term $\tilde{\mathbf{y}}$, which breaks the scaling property that would allow normalization to preserve optimality. Normalizing an unconstrained solution yields weights that satisfy the sum-to-one constraint but increase the objective value compared to the true constrained optimum. Therefore, practitioners implementing epistemic logic tree weight calibration **must solve the constrained optimization problem directly** rather than relying on post-hoc normalization as a shortcut. [KB:spec.compile.research.md] ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: This conclusion is supported by three independent arguments: (1) KKT optimality conditions are structurally different for constrained vs. unconstrained problems, (2) the objective function lacks homogeneity, and (3) the objective value of a normalized unconstrained solution is provably worse than the constrained optimum. All three arguments are mathematically rigorous and mutually reinforcing.]
