# Epistemic Logic-Tree Weight Calibration Under Unit-Sum Constraint

## SLOT 1: Extension of the Error-Minimization Formulation to the Unit-Sum Weight Constraint

The ground-motion logic tree assigns a non-negative weight $w_k$ to each of $K$ candidate
ground-motion prediction equations (GMPEs), and the calibration of these weights is
formulated as a constrained optimization problem that generalizes classical ordinary
least-squares regression. The knowledge-base formulation establishes the log-log design
matrix $\tilde{\mathbf{A}} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times K}$ with entries
$\tilde{A}_{ik} = \ln S_{a,k}(T_i)$, the log-space target vector
$\mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ with components $y_i = \ln S_a^{\mathrm{obj}}(T_i)$,
and the non-negative least-squares (NNLS) problem in which the weight vector
$\mathbf{w} = (w_1, \dots, w_K)^\top$ is required to lie in the non-negative orthant
$\mathbb{R}^K_+$ [KB:spec.compile.research.md]. The extension to the unit-sum
requirement appends an equality constraint to this formulation, restricting the feasible
region from the non-negative orthant to the standard probability simplex.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The NNLS base formulation is fully consistent with the KB source material (spec.compile.research.md, SLOT 5), which defines the log-log design matrix, target vector, and non-negativity constraint in the same notation. No quantitative claims lack citation support; the setup is a direct transcription of the KB formulation with no modifications.]

The extended constrained optimization problem is stated as the minimization of the
mean-squared log-spectral error subject to both the non-negativity of individual weights
and the requirement that the weights sum to unity:^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The prose lead-in correctly characterizes the extended problem before the formal statement. The identification of the feasible set as the probability simplex and the strict convexity of the quadratic objective under full column rank are standard results supported by Lawson & Hanson (1995) and Boyd & Vandenberghe (2004).]

$$\min_{\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^K} \|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}\|_2^2
\quad \text{subject to} \quad \sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k = 1, \quad w_k \geq 0,
\quad k = 1, \dots, K.$$

The feasible set $\Delta^{K-1} = \{\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^K : \mathbf{1}^\top\mathbf{w} = 1,\; w_k \geq 0\}$
is the $(K-1)$-dimensional probability simplex, a compact convex polytope. The objective
$f(\mathbf{w}) = \|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}\|_2^2$ is a strictly convex
quadratic with positive-semidefinite Hessian $2\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\tilde{\mathbf{A}}$
(strictly positive definite when $\tilde{\mathbf{A}}$ has full column rank), so the
problem has a unique global minimiser within $\Delta^{K-1}$. This is a quadratic program
(QP) with one linear equality constraint and $K$ linear inequality constraints, the
standard simplex-constrained QP structure treated extensively in the numerical optimization
literature [DOI:10.1137/1.9781611971217][WEB:https://web.stanford.edu/~boyd/cvxbook/bv_cvxbook.pdf].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The characterization of the simplex as compact and convex, and of the strict convexity of the objective under full column rank, is a standard result confirmed by the cited references (Lawson & Hanson 1995 for NNLS theory; Boyd & Vandenberghe 2004, Section 4.4 for QP structure). Uniqueness of the global minimiser on a compact convex set under a strictly convex objective is a standard theorem in convex analysis.]

The Lagrangian of the constrained problem is constructed by introducing a scalar Lagrange
multiplier $\mu \in \mathbb{R}$ for the equality constraint and a vector of non-negative
multipliers $\boldsymbol{\nu} = (\nu_1, \dots, \nu_K)^\top \geq \mathbf{0}$ for the $K$
non-negativity inequality constraints:^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The Lagrangian construction is standard for QP with mixed equality and inequality constraints. The sign convention (unrestricted multiplier for equality, non-negative multiplier for inequality) is the canonical form used in Boyd & Vandenberghe (2004) and Nocedal & Wright (2006). No unsupported claims are present in this lead-in.]

$$\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w}, \mu, \boldsymbol{\nu}) =
\|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}\|_2^2
+ \mu\!\left(\mathbf{1}^\top\mathbf{w} - 1\right)
- \sum_{k=1}^{K} \nu_k w_k.$$

Because the objective is convex and all constraints are affine, the problem satisfies
the linear independence constraint qualification (LICQ) at every feasible point,
guaranteeing that the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions are both necessary and
sufficient for global optimality. The equality constraint is penalized with an
unrestricted multiplier $\mu$ (which may be positive or negative), while the inequality
constraints $w_k \geq 0$ are penalized with non-negative multipliers $\nu_k \geq 0$
[DOI:10.1007/978-0-387-40065-5][WEB:https://web.stanford.edu/~boyd/cvxbook/bv_cvxbook.pdf].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The statement that LICQ holds for affine constraints and that KKT conditions are necessary and sufficient for convex problems with linear constraints is a standard theorem in constrained optimization, confirmed by Nocedal & Wright (2006), Chapter 12, and Boyd & Vandenberghe (2004), Section 5.5. No unsupported claims are present.]

The KKT optimality conditions at the global minimiser $\mathbf{w}^*$ consist of four
components. The stationarity condition (vanishing gradient of the Lagrangian with
respect to $\mathbf{w}$) is:^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: This lead-in correctly identifies the four KKT components to be presented and correctly attributes the stationarity condition as the gradient of the Lagrangian. The formulation is consistent with the Lagrangian defined in the preceding display equation.]

$$2\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\!\left(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w}^* - \mathbf{y}\right)
+ \mu\mathbf{1} - \boldsymbol{\nu} = \mathbf{0}.$$

Primal feasibility requires $\mathbf{1}^\top\mathbf{w}^* = 1$ and $w_k^* \geq 0$ for all
$k = 1, \dots, K$. Dual feasibility requires $\nu_k \geq 0$ for all $k$. Complementary
slackness requires $\nu_k w_k^* = 0$ for all $k = 1, \dots, K$
[DOI:10.1007/978-0-387-40065-5][WEB:https://web.stanford.edu/~boyd/cvxbook/bv_cvxbook.pdf].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The four KKT conditions (stationarity, primal feasibility, dual feasibility, complementary slackness) are derived by standard differentiation of the Lagrangian and are consistent with the problem formulation above. They match the canonical form for QP with one linear equality constraint and K non-negativity inequalities, as presented in Nocedal & Wright (2006) and Boyd & Vandenberghe (2004).]

The complementary slackness condition partitions the index set $\{1, \dots, K\}$ into
the passive set $\mathcal{P} = \{k : w_k^* > 0\}$ (branches with strictly positive
weight at the optimum) and the active set $\mathcal{Z} = \{k : w_k^* = 0\}$ (branches
eliminated by the non-negativity constraint). Defining the gradient of the objective
$\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{w}) = 2\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y})$,
the KKT stationarity and complementary slackness conditions combine to yield distinct
conditions for each subset. For $k \in \mathcal{P}$, complementary slackness forces
$\nu_k = 0$ and stationarity reduces to:^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The partition into passive and active sets via complementary slackness is the standard active-set interpretation of KKT conditions for inequality-constrained problems, consistent with the Lawson-Hanson active-set algorithm described in KB SLOT 5. The definition of g(w) is correct by differentiation of the quadratic objective.]

$$g_k(\mathbf{w}^*) = -\mu, \quad k \in \mathcal{P}.$$

For $k \in \mathcal{Z}$, the stationarity condition gives $\nu_k = g_k(\mathbf{w}^*) + \mu$,
and dual feasibility $\nu_k \geq 0$ imposes:^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The derivation for the zero-weight subset is symmetric to the passive-set case: with w_k*=0 complementary slackness does not constrain nu_k, so stationarity gives nu_k = g_k(w*) + mu, and dual feasibility nu_k >= 0 then directly yields g_k(w*) >= -mu. This is a straightforward algebraic consequence of the KKT system already stated.]

$$g_k(\mathbf{w}^*) \geq -\mu, \quad k \in \mathcal{Z}.$$

These conditions state that all passive-set components of the gradient $\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{w}^*)$
share a common value equal to $-\mu$, while gradient components corresponding to
zero-weight branches are no smaller than $-\mu$. The equality multiplier $\mu$ thus
acts as a uniform shift that the unit-sum constraint introduces into the gradient
optimality conditions, distinguishing this formulation from the pure NNLS case (without
unit-sum), in which the analogous conditions are $g_k(\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{\mathrm{NNLS}}) = 0$
for $k \in \mathcal{P}$ and $g_k(\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{\mathrm{NNLS}}) \geq 0$ for
$k \in \mathcal{Z}$ [KB:spec.compile.research.md][DOI:10.1137/1.9781611971217].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The display equations for the two subsets follow directly from the stationarity equation and complementary slackness. The comparison with the pure NNLS conditions (mu=0) is accurate: in NNLS the equality multiplier is absent and the passive-set gradient condition reduces to g_k=0. This structure is consistent with KB SLOT 5 and with Lawson & Hanson (1995), whose active-set algorithm is precisely the iterative procedure that enforces these conditions. No unsupported claims.]

The value of the equality multiplier $\mu$ is determined implicitly by the requirement
that $\mathbf{1}^\top\mathbf{w}^* = 1$ be satisfied at the optimum. An equivalent
characterization of the solution is as the result of minimizing the shifted objective
$\|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}\|_2^2 + \mu(\mathbf{1}^\top\mathbf{w} - 1)$
over the non-negative orthant for the unique value of $\mu$ such that the sum of the
resulting positive-weight components equals one [ARXIV:1101.6081][DOI:10.1007/BF01584232].
This observation underlies efficient simplex-projection algorithms: iterating over
candidate values of $\mu$ (equivalently, candidate support sets $\mathcal{P}$) at
$O(K \log K)$ cost identifies the unique $\mu^*$ such that the projection onto the
simplex is obtained [ARXIV:1101.6081]. In direct QP implementations (for example,
using `quadprog` in R), the unit-sum constraint is imposed as an explicit linear
equality, and the KKT system above is solved numerically by the active-set solver with
both equality and inequality multipliers treated as distinct dual variables.^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The characterization of mu as the unique shift producing unit-sum positive components is supported by Chen & Ye (2011) arXiv:1101.6081, which derives this structure for Euclidean projection onto the simplex. The O(K log K) complexity claim for the simplex-projection algorithm is stated in that reference. Haskell & Hanson (1981) DOI:10.1007/BF01584232 covers the combined equality and non-negativity constrained LS case. The claim about uniqueness of mu requires full column rank of A_tilde, which is a stated hypothesis of the KB formulation; confidence is MEDIUM because this condition is referenced but not formally re-derived here.]

## SLOT 2: Validity of Post-Hoc Normalization Versus Direct Imposition of the Unit-Sum Constraint

A procedural alternative to solving the simplex-constrained QP directly is to first
solve the non-negative least-squares problem without the unit-sum constraint, obtaining
$\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{\mathrm{NNLS}}$ with $\hat{w}_k^{\mathrm{NNLS}} \geq 0$, and
subsequently normalize the resulting weight vector by dividing each component by the
total weight sum $S = \mathbf{1}^\top\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{\mathrm{NNLS}} = \sum_{k=1}^K \hat{w}_k^{\mathrm{NNLS}}$.
This yields the candidate $\bar{\mathbf{w}} = \hat{\mathbf{w}}^{\mathrm{NNLS}} / S$,
which satisfies $\bar{w}_k \geq 0$ and $\sum_k \bar{w}_k = 1$ by construction. The
question of whether $\bar{\mathbf{w}}$ coincides with the true simplex-constrained
optimum $\mathbf{w}^*$ is answered by examining the mathematical structure of the
objective function and the KKT optimality conditions: in general, these two vectors
are distinct [KB:spec.compile.research.md][DOI:10.1007/BF01584232].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The description of the post-hoc normalization procedure is a straightforward arithmetic definition and is correct. The conclusion that the normalized solution generally differs from the simplex-constrained optimum is the principal claim of this section and is developed rigorously in the paragraphs that follow. The setup paragraph does not overstate or anticipate conclusions without justification; citation to KB and Haskell & Hanson (1981) is appropriate for the formal combined-constraint treatment.]

The fundamental obstruction to the validity of post-hoc normalization is that the
squared-error objective $f(\mathbf{w}) = \|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}\|_2^2$
is not scale-invariant: scaling $\mathbf{w}$ by a positive factor $\alpha \neq 1$
produces a different function value. Expanding explicitly,^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The premise that the objective is not scale-invariant is a direct mathematical fact, demonstrated in the display equation that follows. The characterization as the fundamental obstruction is logically prior to the gradient argument developed subsequently.]

$$f(\alpha\mathbf{w}) = \|\alpha\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}\|_2^2
= \alpha^2\|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w}\|_2^2
- 2\alpha\,\langle\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w},\, \mathbf{y}\rangle
+ \|\mathbf{y}\|_2^2.$$

This expression equals $f(\mathbf{w})$ only when $\alpha = 1$ (trivially) or in the
degenerate case $\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{0}$ and $\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{0}$,
neither of which arises in the ground-motion application where the log-target spectrum
is nonzero. Consequently, the location of the minimiser of $f$ over the non-negative
orthant is not preserved under rescaling, and the minimiser over the simplex
(which is the minimiser of $f$ over a one-parameter family of scaled cross-sections
of the orthant) occupies a different location in weight space
[WEB:https://web.stanford.edu/~boyd/cvxbook/bv_cvxbook.pdf].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The algebraic expansion of f(alpha*w) is elementary and correct. The conclusion that f(alpha*w) = f(w) only when alpha=1 or under degenerate conditions follows directly from the quadratic structure: the cross-term -2*alpha*(A*w, y) and the squared term alpha^2*||A*w||^2 are not equal to their alpha=1 counterparts in general. The geometric interpretation (minimiser over orthant vs. over simplex) is consistent with convex analysis as presented in Boyd & Vandenberghe (2004).]

A more explicit demonstration proceeds through the gradient optimality conditions.
Defining $\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{w}) = 2\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y})$,
the NNLS KKT conditions at $\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{\mathrm{NNLS}}$ require
$g_k(\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{\mathrm{NNLS}}) = 0$ for all $k \in \mathcal{P}$ and
$g_k(\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{\mathrm{NNLS}}) \geq 0$ for all $k \in \mathcal{Z}$
[KB:spec.compile.research.md]. Evaluating the gradient of $f$ at the normalized
point $\bar{\mathbf{w}} = \hat{\mathbf{w}}^{\mathrm{NNLS}}/S$ by direct computation:^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: Citing KB SLOT 5 for the NNLS KKT conditions is appropriate; these conditions are explicitly stated in the KB. The gradient computation at the normalized point follows algebraically from the definition of g(w) and the linearity of the gradient in w. The lead-in correctly sets up the argument before the display equation.]

$$g_k(\bar{\mathbf{w}})
= \frac{1}{S}\,g_k\!\left(\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{\mathrm{NNLS}}\right)
+ \frac{2(1-S)}{S}\left[\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\mathbf{y}\right]_k,$$

where $[\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\mathbf{y}]_k = \sum_{i=1}^n \ln S_{a,k}(T_i)\cdot\ln S_a^{\mathrm{obj}}(T_i)$
is the inner product of the log-spectrum of candidate $k$ with the log-target spectrum
across the period mesh. For the passive-set indices $k \in \mathcal{P}$, the NNLS
condition $g_k(\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{\mathrm{NNLS}}) = 0$ eliminates the first term, giving:^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The definition of [A_tilde^T*y]_k as the inner product of the k-th log-spectral column with the log-target is correct by matrix-vector multiplication. The simplification for k in P uses the NNLS stationarity condition g_k(w_hat)=0, which holds exactly for all passive-set indices at the NNLS optimum as stated in KB SLOT 5.]

$$g_k(\bar{\mathbf{w}}) = \frac{2(1-S)}{S}\left[\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\mathbf{y}\right]_k,
\quad k \in \mathcal{P}.$$

For the normalized point $\bar{\mathbf{w}}$ to satisfy the KKT stationarity condition
of the simplex-constrained problem (Slot 1), the gradient must equal $-\mu$ uniformly
across all $k \in \mathcal{P}$. Substituting, this requires
$[\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\mathbf{y}]_k$ to take the same value for every record in the
passive set. In the ground-motion application, this inner product quantifies the
spectral affinity between the log-spectrum of the $k$-th GMPE branch and the log-target
spectrum; there is no physical mechanism that enforces these affinities to be equal
across all positively weighted branches, so the condition is not satisfied in general
[DOI:10.1214/13-EJS868][DOI:10.1007/BF01584232].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The gradient identity g_k(w-bar) = (1/S)*g_k(w_hat) + 2*(1-S)/S * [A^T*y]_k is obtained by writing g(w-bar) = 2*A^T*(A*(w_hat/S) - y) = (1/S)*2*A^T*A*w_hat - 2*A^T*y = (1/S)*g(w_hat) + 2*A^T*y*(1/S - 1), confirming the formula. The simplification for k in P uses g_k(w_hat)=0. The conclusion that [A^T*y]_k must be constant across P for the KKT condition to hold is a direct algebraic consequence. The claim that no physical mechanism enforces this is qualitatively supported by the spectral interpretation of the inner product and by the general theory in the cited references (Slawski & Hein 2013 for NNLS conditions; Haskell & Hanson 1981 for combined constraints).]

The conditions under which post-hoc normalization yields the correct simplex-constrained
solution are identifiable precisely. First, if $S = \mathbf{1}^\top\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{\mathrm{NNLS}} = 1$,
the normalization is the identity map: $\bar{\mathbf{w}} = \hat{\mathbf{w}}^{\mathrm{NNLS}}$
trivially satisfies the unit-sum constraint, and $\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{\mathrm{NNLS}}$ is
simultaneously the NNLS minimiser and the simplex-constrained minimiser. This coincidence
holds only when the specific scale relationship between the candidate log-spectral
columns and the log-target happens to produce $S = 1$, a non-generic condition that
depends entirely on the data. Second, if all passive-set inner products
$[\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\mathbf{y}]_k$ are equal for $k \in \mathcal{P}$, the
gradient condition at $\bar{\mathbf{w}}$ is uniformly satisfied with multiplier
$\mu^* = -2(1-S)/S \cdot [\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\mathbf{y}]_{\mathcal{P}}$. This
constitutes a symmetry condition on the spectral data that likewise holds only in
special cases. Outside both conditions, $\bar{\mathbf{w}}$ is a strictly sub-optimal
feasible point on the simplex: by strict convexity of $f$ on $\Delta^{K-1}$, the
objective value $f(\bar{\mathbf{w}})$ exceeds $f(\mathbf{w}^*)$
[WEB:https://web.stanford.edu/~boyd/cvxbook/bv_cvxbook.pdf][DOI:10.1007/978-0-387-40065-5].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The two sufficient conditions for normalization validity (S=1 and equal cross-correlations across P) are derived directly from the gradient analysis in the preceding paragraph. The claim that f(w-bar) > f(w*) whenever w-bar != w* follows from strict convexity of f on the compact convex simplex and the fact that w* is the unique global minimiser; this is confirmed by Boyd & Vandenberghe (2004) and Nocedal & Wright (2006). No unsupported quantitative claims are present.]

The practical implication is that post-hoc normalization constitutes a heuristic
approximation rather than a mathematically rigorous solution to the unit-sum calibration
problem. The approximation may be acceptable when $S$ is close to unity, because in
that regime the perturbation $1/S$ is close to one and the gradient deviation
$2(1-S)/S \cdot [\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\mathbf{y}]_k$ is small; the sub-optimality
$f(\bar{\mathbf{w}}) - f(\mathbf{w}^*)$ is then bounded by a quantity proportional to
$|S - 1|^2$ by a second-order Taylor argument. However, when the columns of
$\tilde{\mathbf{A}}$ are not scaled commensurate with the log-target $\mathbf{y}$ -
a common situation when the candidate log-spectra collectively overshoot or undershoot
the target amplitude - $S$ can deviate substantially from one, and the sub-optimality
of $\bar{\mathbf{w}}$ is not bounded a priori by any intrinsic property of the
formulation. The rigorous treatment therefore requires the unit-sum constraint to be
imposed directly within the optimization, either via a general QP solver with an
explicit equality constraint or via a dedicated simplex-projection algorithm, as
formalized through the simplex-constrained QP and its KKT conditions in Slot 1
[DOI:10.1137/1.9781611971217][DOI:10.1007/BF01584232][ARXIV:1101.6081].^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The order-of-magnitude estimate that the sub-optimality scales as |S-1|^2 for S near 1 is qualitatively correct by second-order Taylor expansion but no formal error bound with explicit constants is provided or cited, which limits confidence. The characterization of situations where S deviates substantially from one is stated qualitatively without specific numerical thresholds. The recommendation for direct QP formulation is well-supported by the cited references (Lawson & Hanson 1995, Haskell & Hanson 1981, Chen & Ye 2011). The overall conclusion is sound but the approximation-quality claim is not quantified with a formal bound.]
