# Weight Calibration in Epistemic Logic Trees: Sum-to-One Constraint Formulation and Normalization Validity

## SLOT 1: Extended weight calibration formulation for sum-to-one constraint

Weight calibration in epistemic logic trees for seismic hazard analysis requires assigning weights $w_k$ to $N$ candidate Ground Motion Prediction Equations (GMPEs) such that the discrete probability distribution they define is well-formed, meaning $\sum_{k=1}^{N} w_k = 1$ with $w_k > 0$ for all $k$. [KB:spec.compile.research.md] This constraint arises from the fundamental requirement that logic tree branch weights must represent a complete probability distribution over the space of candidate model alternatives. The weight calibration problem extends classical constrained linear regression methodologies by adding an equality constraint reflecting this probabilistic normalization. ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The sum-to-one constraint is a well-established requirement in probability theory and PSHA logic tree construction; the characterization of weight calibration as constrained linear regression is directly supported by the KB formulations in SLOT 3 through SLOT 7.]

The extended optimization problem can be formulated as a constrained least-squares problem in log-spectral space. Let $\tilde{\mathbf{A}} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times N}$ be the design matrix with entries $\tilde{A}_{ik} = \ln S_{a,k}(T_i)$ representing the log-spectral accelerations of the $k$-th GMPE at period $T_i$, and let $\tilde{\mathbf{y}} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ be the target log-spectral vector with components $y_i = \ln S_a^{\mathrm{obj}}(T_i)$. [KB:spec.compile.research.md] The complete constrained weight calibration problem is formulated as follows. ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The notation and design matrix construction are standard in spectral matching literature and directly correspond to formulations in SLOT 3 and SLOT 4 of the KB.]

$$\mathbf{w}^* = \underset{\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^N}{\arg\min} \; \|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w} - \tilde{\mathbf{y}}\|_2^2$$

subject to the following constraints: ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The constraint specification (sum-to-one and strict positivity) is derived from the probability distribution requirement for logic tree weights and is consistent with standard probability theory.]

$$\sum_{k=1}^{N} w_k = 1,$$

$$w_k > 0, \quad k = 1, \ldots, N.$$

This formulation combines three mathematical elements derived from existing literature in spectral matching and constrained optimization. [KB:spec.compile.research.md][WEB:https://www.mathworks.com/help/optim/ug/lsqlin.html] The objective function, derived from the log-log regression methodology [KB:spec.compile.research.md], measures prediction error in log-spectral units. The equality constraint $\sum_{k=1}^{N} w_k = 1$ ensures that the weights form a probability distribution over candidate models. The strict positivity constraint $w_k > 0$ ensures that each candidate model contributes to the ensemble, preventing the elimination of branches and maintaining the integrity of the epistemic uncertainty representation in the logic tree. ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The individual components are well-established in the KB (log-log formulation in SLOT 4, non-negativity in SLOT 5, bounded formulations in SLOT 6); the combination with the sum-to-one constraint follows standard constrained optimization practice as confirmed by external sources on constrained least squares.]

The constraint structure differs fundamentally from the partial-constraint formulations presented in the knowledge base. The non-negative least-squares (NNLS) problem [KB:spec.compile.research.md] enforces $w_k \geq 0$ but imposes no sum-to-one normalization, allowing the objective function value to determine the scale of the weight vector. The bounded-weight formulation [KB:spec.compile.research.md] imposes lower and upper bounds $w_{\min} \leq w_k \leq w_{\max}$ but again does not normalize to a probability distribution. The sum-to-one constraint reduces the effective dimension of the feasible solution space from $N$ degrees of freedom to $N-1$ degrees of freedom, because once $N-1$ weights are specified, the final weight is determined by the constraint. [WEB:https://www.mathworks.com/help/optim/ug/lsqlin.html] This dimensionality reduction changes the optimization landscape: the gradient of the Lagrangian now includes a scalar multiplier $\lambda$ associated with the equality constraint, and the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions require both stationarity of the Lagrangian with respect to the weights and satisfaction of the constraint equation. ^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The structural differences from NNLS and bounded formulations are correctly stated based on standard optimization theory; however, the practical implications for weight calibration in PSHA have not been verified through empirical PSHA studies in the available sources.]

This constrained formulation may be solved using established algorithms for constrained quadratic programming. Standard implementations include interior-point methods, active-set methods, or methods employing singular value decomposition to eliminate the equality constraint. [WEB:https://www.mathworks.com/help/optim/ug/lsqlin.html] For problems combining non-negativity, strict positivity, and sum-to-one constraints, specialized solvers such as those based on projected gradient methods or sequential least-squares programming (SLSQP) are appropriate. The convex optimization framework [KB:spec.compile.research.md] subsumes this constrained variant as a special case: the objective remains convex, the feasible set defined by linear constraints is convex, and therefore any local minimum is a global minimum. Solving the problem via general convex programming libraries (e.g., CVXR with an equality constraint specification) provides a robust and numerically stable approach. ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The convexity argument is mathematically sound (sum of convex functions over a convex set); solver recommendations align with standard numerical optimization practice.]

## SLOT 2: Validate normalization approach and justify mathematical treatment

A critical practical question arises in weight calibration: given a solution $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\mathrm{unconstrained}}$ from an unconstrained or partially constrained problem (such as NNLS or bounded-weight least squares), is it valid to apply post-hoc normalization, that is, to compute $\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_k = \hat{w}_k / \sum_j \hat{w}_j$ and use this normalized vector as a substitute for the solution to the sum-to-one constrained problem? The answer depends critically on the form of the objective function and the structure of the original problem, and the mathematical treatment reveals fundamental differences between post-hoc normalization and direct constraint enforcement. ^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: Post-hoc normalization's validity is a theoretical question that depends on properties of the specific objective function; the following analysis is derived from standard optimization theory, but empirical validation in PSHA contexts would strengthen confidence.]

**Preservation of optimality under post-hoc normalization**: Post-hoc normalization does NOT preserve the optimality property of the original unconstrained or bounded solution. [WEB:https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-negative_least_squares] To understand why, consider the objective function $f(\mathbf{w}) = \|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w} - \tilde{\mathbf{y}}\|_2^2$. If $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\mathrm{NNLS}}$ minimizes $f$ subject only to $w_k \geq 0$, then applying a scalar multiplier $c > 0$ to obtain $\mathbf{w}' = c \hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\mathrm{NNLS}}$ yields:

$$f(\mathbf{w}') = \|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}(c\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\mathrm{NNLS}}) - \tilde{\mathbf{y}}\|_2^2 = \|c(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\mathrm{NNLS}}) - \tilde{\mathbf{y}}\|_2^2.$$

The scaled prediction $c(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\mathrm{NNLS}})$ moves farther from the target $\tilde{\mathbf{y}}$ unless $c = 1$. The function $f$ is NOT scale-invariant: doubling all weights scales the prediction but increases the squared error to the target. Therefore, the normalized vector $\tilde{\mathbf{w}}$ generally satisfies $f(\tilde{\mathbf{w}}) > f(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\mathrm{NNLS}})$, meaning post-hoc normalization degrades the objective value and yields a suboptimal solution to the constrained problem. ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The non-scale-invariance of the squared-error objective is a fundamental property of least-squares optimization; this argument applies directly to the log-spectral formulation.]

**Equivalence to constrained optimization**: Post-hoc normalization is mathematically inequivalent to solving the constrained problem directly using Lagrangian methods or constraint elimination. The constrained problem is specified as follows. ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The problem statement is a standard constrained optimization formulation.]

$$\min_{\mathbf{w}} \|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w} - \tilde{\mathbf{y}}\|_2^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad \sum_{k=1}^{N} w_k = 1, \quad w_k > 0,$$

When solving this constrained problem, the sum-to-one constraint enters the Lagrangian as follows: ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The Lagrangian formulation and KKT conditions are standard results in constrained optimization theory.]

$$\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w}, \lambda) = \|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w} - \tilde{\mathbf{y}}\|_2^2 + \lambda \left(\sum_{k=1}^{N} w_k - 1\right),$$

where $\lambda$ is the Lagrange multiplier associated with the equality constraint. The KKT stationarity condition requires:

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w_k} = 2\tilde{\mathbf{A}}_{\cdot k}^\top(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w} - \tilde{\mathbf{y}}) + \lambda = 0 \quad \text{for all} \quad k = 1, \ldots, N,$$

where $\tilde{\mathbf{A}}_{\cdot k}$ denotes the $k$-th column of $\tilde{\mathbf{A}}$. [WEB:https://www.mathworks.com/help/optim/ug/lsqlin.html] This system couples all weights through the common Lagrange multiplier $\lambda$, ensuring that at optimality, the gradient of the objective (scaled by $2$) is uniform across all weights: each weight adjustment produces the same marginal change in objective value. By contrast, the NNLS solution has no such uniformity; gradients typically differ across components, and the active set (indices where $w_k = 0$) is determined by a distinct optimization mechanism. The normalized NNLS solution respects neither the optimality conditions nor the Lagrangian structure of the constrained problem. ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The KKT conditions and Lagrangian formulation are standard results in constrained optimization theory; the distinction between constrained and post-hoc approaches is mathematically rigorous.]

**Conditions under which post-hoc normalization might be acceptable**: Post-hoc normalization could be justified only under very restrictive assumptions. If the original unconstrained solution $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\mathrm{unconstrained}}$ satisfies $\sum_k \hat{w}_k = 1$ already (for example, by coincidence or design), then no rescaling is needed and post-hoc normalization is a null operation. More generally, if the objective function were known to be scale-invariant (such as entropy-based objectives or certain divergence measures), post-hoc normalization might preserve optimality; however, the squared-error objective used in spectral matching is explicitly scale-dependent. [KB:spec.compile.research.md] For the specific application of weight calibration in epistemic logic trees, post-hoc normalization applied to an NNLS or bounded-weight solution introduces an unquantified approximation error in the objective function and produces weights that are suboptimal in the constrained formulation. If computational constraints make solving the constrained problem infeasible, post-hoc normalization is at best a heuristic approximation, and its deviation from the true constrained optimum should be quantified through numerical comparison. ^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The identification of scale-invariance as the key distinguishing factor is theoretically sound; however, practical guidance on when post-hoc normalization might be acceptable requires empirical PSHA case studies not present in available sources.]

**Conclusion**: The answer to the validation question is negative: post-hoc normalization of an unconstrained or partially constrained solution is NOT mathematically equivalent to solving the sum-to-one constrained problem directly. The squared-error objective is scale-dependent, the constrained and post-hoc approaches satisfy different optimality conditions, and the normalized solution is generally suboptimal in terms of the original objective function. For weight calibration in epistemic logic trees representing seismic hazard, solving the constrained problem directly using standard convex optimization methods is the only rigorous approach. Post-hoc normalization may be used as a computational shortcut only if the deviation in objective value is acceptable for the intended application, and this deviation must be quantified and reported. ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The mathematical arguments against post-hoc normalization are rigorous and well-supported by standard optimization theory; the recommendation to solve directly is consistent with best practices in constrained optimization.]
