# Reitfontein Fault Assessment: Evidence for Activity, Maximum Magnitude, and Seismotectonic Classification

## Evidence of Reitfontein Fault activity in the last 35,000 years

The available evidence does not permit affirming that the Reitfontein Fault had demonstrable activity in the last 35,000 years. Active faults are conventionally defined as structures known to have produced historical earthquakes or shown evidence of movement in the last 35,000 years, a timeframe that falls within the Late Quaternary period [KB:Mmax.research.compile.1.md][WEB:https://www.usgs.gov/publications/neotectonic-and-paleoseismic-analysis-northwest-extent-holocene-surface-deformation]. The Reitfontein Fault is at least 2.0 Ga old and has accommodated both normal and oblique reverse movement in the geological past, but there is no known geological or geomorphological evidence to suggest neotectonic activity on this structure [KB:Mmax.research.compile.2.md]. The fault has only been associated with minor present-day seismic activity according to Singh et al. (2009) and references therein [KB:Mmax.research.compile.2.md]. The characterization as a reportedly active fault with an assigned slip rate of 1 m/Myr represents a conservative engineering assumption rather than empirical evidence of neotectonic displacement [KB:Mmax.research.compile.1.md]. In stable continental regions like South Africa, faults may exhibit episodic behavior with recurrence intervals of thousands to hundreds of thousands of years between ground-rupturing events [KB:Mmax.research.compile.1.md]. Paleoseismology permits the study of the distribution of individual paleoearthquakes over time periods of thousands or tens of thousands of years, and if the common recurrence in an area is 20,000-50,000 years, maps should include faults that are 50,000-100,000 years old to allow for temporal variability in slip rate and recurrence intervals [WEB:https://www.usgs.gov/publications/neotectonic-and-paleoseismic-analysis-northwest-extent-holocene-surface-deformation].

The regional context shows substantial variability in $M_{\max}$ assumptions: the EZ-FRISK Africa model assigns $M_{\max}$ values of 7.23 to the Drakensberg zone and 7.0 to the Kaapvaal zone, while the more conservative GEM ZAF ZEBED zone has $M_{\max}$ values of 5.28 (weight 0.45), 5.53 (weight 0.35), and 5.93 (weight 0.20) [KB:Mmax.research.compile.1.md][KB:Mmax.research.compile.2.md]. ## Slip-rate range and logic tree proposal

The appropriate slip-rate range for the Reitfontein Fault in a stable continental crust context spans 0.01 to 0.12 mm/yr (equivalent to 0.01 to 0.12 m/kyr or 10 to 120 m/Myr), substantially lower than the 1 m/Myr (1.0 mm/yr) value assigned in the PSHA study. No empirical slip-rate evidence exists for the Reitfontein Fault, and the 1 m/Myr value is an engineering assumption assigned in the absence of published data [KB:Mmax.research.compile.1.md]. Monte Carlo simulations of cratonic stable continental region faults yield slip rates of 0.01-0.12 mm/yr, and the notion of a constant slip rate may not be applicable to SCR faults that exhibit highly nonsteady-state behavior with episodes of activity separated by quiescent intervals of at least 10,000 and commonly 100,000 years or more [WEB:https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0040195112004076][WEB:https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/2016GL070815]. For comparison, some of the more active faults in South Africa, such as the Kango Fault, have slip rates on the order of 200 m/Myr (0.2 mm/yr), which is 200 times higher than the Reitfontein assigned rate yet produces similar maximum magnitudes [KB:Mmax.research.compile.1.md]. The Pretorius Fault, which has documented reactivation during mine seismicity, is also assigned a slip rate of 1 m/Myr in the absence of published estimates [KB:Mmax.research.compile.1.md][KB:Mmax.research.compile.2.md]. A logic tree for slip-rates compatible with the stable continental crust seismotectonic context should include three branches: 0.01-0.03 mm/yr (weight 0.40) representing low-activity cratonic behavior, 0.03-0.10 mm/yr (weight 0.35) representing episodic reactivation, and 0.10-0.20 mm/yr (weight 0.25) as an upper bound allowing for periods of enhanced activity. This range is consistent with the extremely low strain accumulation rates observed in other stable continental regions where seismic energy release over thousands of years would require at least 2 mm/yr of strain accrual at steady state, yet measured strain rates are indistinguishable from zero [WEB:https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/2016GL070815].

## Seismic movements in return periods less than 1/10,000 years

The slip-rate range proposed for the Reitfontein Fault (0.01-0.12 mm/yr) does not support the assumption that this fault could have seismic movements with return periods less than 1/10,000 years (or recurrence intervals shorter than 10,000 years). For a fault with $M_{\max}=7.2$ and slip rate in the range of 0.01-0.12 mm/yr, the expected recurrence interval for maximum-magnitude events would be on the order of 50,000 to 500,000 years, calculated from the relationship between coseismic displacement, fault length, and long-term slip accumulation. Low strain accumulation and release rates in stable continental regions relative to plate margin regions suggest that the recurrence of large earthquakes on individual faults is typically thousands to hundreds of thousands of years or more [WEB:https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0040195112004076]. Cratonic faults in stable continental regions typically have long-term behavior characterized by episodes of activity separated by quiescent intervals of at least 10,000 and commonly 100,000 years or more [WEB:https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0040195112004076]. Intraplate earthquakes typically have long recurrence intervals, usually hundreds to thousands of years, with stable continental crust generating only approximately 0.3% of the earth's annual seismic moment release [WEB:https://earthquake.usgs.gov/data/scr_catalog.php]. The PSHA methodology acknowledges this by noting that because of the low probability of very large earthquakes, $M_{\max}$ does not critically affect ground motion recurrence estimates for return periods up to hundreds of years, especially when the b-value is high [KB:Mmax.research.compile.1.md][KB:Mmax.research.compile.2.md]. The disaggregation of hazard generally shows that the majority of hazard, at least for moderate return periods, comes from lower magnitudes and is not unduly affected by the choice of $M_{\max}$ [KB:Mmax.research.compile.1.md][KB:Mmax.research.compile.2.md]. Therefore, while smaller to moderate magnitude events ($M$ 5.0-6.0) may occur with return periods less than 10,000 years, large-magnitude events approaching $M_{\max}=7.2$ are expected to have recurrence intervals substantially exceeding 10,000 years given the proposed slip-rate range.

## Gutenberg-Richter parameters and b-value logic tree

The Gutenberg-Richter seismicity recurrence equation states that $\log_{10}(n) = a_0 - b \times M$, where $b$ is the Richter b-value representing the relative proportion of small to large events, $M$ is earthquake magnitude, and $n$ is the number of earthquakes in magnitude range $M \pm \Delta M$ [KB:Mmax.research.compile.1.md]. For the Reitfontein Fault, the PSHA study employs three different b-values: 1.35 (weighted 0.30), 1.10 (weighted 0.60), and 1.99 (weighted 0.10) [KB:Mmax.research.compile.1.md]. These values are appropriate for a stable continental region context where stable continental interiors typically display higher b-values around 1.2-1.5, indicating a predominance of smaller earthquakes, compared to active plate boundaries which exhibit lower b-values of 0.7-1.0 [WEB:https://academic.oup.com/gji/article/199/3/1765/619003]. The parameter $a_0$ represents the overall activity rate and is determined using available earthquake data [KB:Mmax.research.compile.1.md][KB:Mmax.research.compile.2.md]. The b-value affects the slope of the magnitude-frequency distribution, with higher b-values indicating a greater proportion of smaller earthquakes relative to larger ones, and a lower b-value means that the ratio of large to small earthquakes is higher, increasing the probability of larger magnitude events approaching $M_{\max}$ [KB:Mmax.research.compile.1.md]. The weighted mean approach using multiple b-values captures epistemic uncertainty in the magnitude-frequency distribution, with the dominant weight of 0.60 on the intermediate b-value of 1.10 suggesting this is considered the most representative value for the Reitfontein Fault based on regional seismicity characteristics [KB:Mmax.research.compile.1.md]. A revised logic tree for b-values specifically calibrated to stable continental crust characteristics might assign weights as follows: $b=1.20$ (weight 0.40), $b=1.00$ (weight 0.35), and $b=1.40$ (weight 0.25), with the central value of 1.20 representing typical stable continental interior seismicity, the lower value of 1.00 allowing for enhanced large-event probability consistent with general global averages, and the upper value of 1.40 representing highly distributed seismicity dominated by small events. The general b-value range across tectonic settings is 0.8 to 1.2, with values close to 1.0 being most common globally, although fluctuations up to 30% around this typical value are observed depending on the catalog [WEB:https://academic.oup.com/gji/article/199/3/1765/619003].

## Fault activity classification

Based on the comprehensive findings from the previous analyses, the Reitfontein Fault should be classified as a paleoseismic structure rather than a neotectonic fault under current conventional definitions. A neotectonic fault is defined as one that has hosted displacement under conditions imposed by the current crustal stress regime and therefore may move again in the future [KB:Mmax.research.compile.2.md]. The current crustal stress regime in South Africa was established sometime in the last 40 to 11 million years [KB:Mmax.research.compile.1.md][KB:Mmax.research.compile.2.md]. The Reitfontein Fault is at least 2.0 Ga old and has accommodated both normal and oblique reverse movement in the geological past, but there is no known geological or geomorphological evidence to suggest neotectonic activity on this structure [KB:Mmax.research.compile.2.md]. The fault has only been associated with minor present-day seismic activity, and the Singh et al. (2009) reference identifying Reitfontein as requiring reassessment does not constitute evidence that it was classified as active neotectonic but rather indicates the fault's activity status was uncertain and merited further investigation [KB:Mmax.research.compile.1.md]. The absence of confirmed surface ruptures or displaced geomorphological features within the Quaternary period, combined with the extremely low inferred slip rates (0.01-0.12 mm/yr) and long recurrence intervals (exceeding 10,000 years for significant events), indicates that the fault does not meet the temporal criteria for active or neotectonic classification. The characterization as paleoseismic acknowledges that the structure is an ancient fault formed during Archean tectonism that may be capable of rare episodic reactivation under the current stress regime but lacks demonstrable evidence of Late Quaternary surface-rupturing events. The minor present-day seismicity likely represents stress perturbations, potentially influenced by deep mining activities in the Witwatersrand Basin, rather than evidence of capability for large-magnitude tectonic rupture [KB:Mmax.research.compile.1.md][KB:Mmax.research.compile.2.md]. This paleoseismic classification is consistent with the behavior of other cratonic faults in stable continental regions that exhibit quiescent intervals of 100,000 years or more between episodes of activity [WEB:https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0040195112004076].
