## SLOT 3: Probabilistic Functional Structure of Newmark Displacement Estimation Methods

The Newmark sliding-block method conceptualizes the seismically loaded slope as either a rigid or a flexible (compliant) block resting on an inclined frictional surface. When the driving acceleration component along the failure plane exceeds the yield acceleration $k_y g$, the block undergoes incremental downslope movement; relative motion ceases when the driving acceleration falls below this threshold. The total permanent displacement $D$ accumulates over all exceedance episodes throughout the duration of shaking. In probabilistic applications, displacement is characterized using empirical regression relationships expressed in lognormal space, and the probabilistic functional structure of all models in the ensemble follows a common lognormal representation that permits propagation of both aleatory and epistemic uncertainty.

All models in the ensemble follow the general functional form in which the logarithm of displacement is expressed as a deterministic mean plus a random residual:

$$\ln D = \mu_{\ln D}(\mathrm{IM},\,k_y,\,T_n,\,M_w) + \epsilon\,\sigma_{\ln D}$$

In this expression, $\mathrm{IM}$ denotes the set of ground-motion intensity measures specific to each model (e.g., $\mathrm{PGA}$, spectral acceleration $S_a$ at a reference period, Arias intensity $\mathrm{AI}$, or peak ground velocity $\mathrm{PGV}$); $T_n$ is the fundamental period of the sliding mass; $M_w$ is the earthquake moment magnitude where included as a model predictor; $\mu_{\ln D}$ is the model-predicted mean displacement in natural-log space; $\sigma_{\ln D}$ is the associated standard deviation in natural-log space; and $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$ is a standard-normal variate capturing record-to-record variability not explained by the regression. Where hazard is derived from a ground-motion model or site response analysis, spectral accelerations are evaluated at the periods required by each model, typically $S_a(\alpha T_n)$ with $\alpha$ in the range 1.3 to 1.5. This convention reflects the observation that the peak response of a flexible sliding mass does not occur at its undegraded fundamental period but at a somewhat longer period, accounting for partial stiffness degradation under seismic loading.

The probabilistic functional structure of rigid-block models is determined primarily by the critical acceleration ratio $r = k_y / \mathrm{PGA}$. This ratio captures the fundamental controlling variable identified by Newmark: the margin between the peak ground acceleration and the yield resistance of the slope. Rigid-block models differ in their choice of secondary predictors; some retain $r$ as the sole predictor, while others augment the model with Arias intensity $\mathrm{AI}$ to capture the cumulative energy content and duration of shaking, or introduce $\mathrm{PGA}$ and $\mathrm{AI}$ simultaneously in a multi-parameter formulation. The common thread across all rigid-block models is the absence of $T_n$ as an explicit predictor, reflecting the assumption that the sliding mass responds as an undistorted body to the base excitation without significant internal dynamic amplification. Four rigid-block models are retained in the ensemble: Ambraseys and Menu (1988) [@AmbraseysMenu1988], which expresses displacement as a function of $r$ alone; Yegian, Marciano, and Varzaghian (1991) [@YegianEtAl1991], which employs the same single predictor in a polynomial form; Jibson (2007) [@Jibson2007], which adds Arias intensity to capture duration effects; and Saygili and Rathje (2008) [@SaygiliRathje2008], a multi-parameter formulation incorporating both $\mathrm{PGA}$ and $\mathrm{AI}$ simultaneously with amplitude-dependent dispersion.

The probabilistic functional structure of flexible-block models introduces a qualitatively different dependence on seismic demand. These models replace the simple acceleration ratio with a spectral acceleration $S_a$ evaluated at a period proportional to $T_n$, thereby incorporating the resonant response of the compliant sliding mass. The multiplicative factor relating the spectral evaluation period to $T_n$ equals 1.5 in the earlier subduction zone and general tectonic models and 1.3 in the calibrated shallow crustal update, reflecting the degree of period elongation associated with large-amplitude response and post-yield stiffness degradation. All flexible-block models include $M_w$ as an explicit predictor to capture the duration dependence of accumulated displacement under long ground motions, such as those produced by subduction zone events. The functional form retains the lognormal structure with a quadratic polynomial in $\ln S_a$, with the quadratic coefficient reflecting curvature of the displacement-intensity relationship at high demand levels. Three flexible-block models are employed: Bray and Travasarou (2007) [@BrayTravasarou2007], calibrated against 688 recordings spanning diverse tectonic environments; Bray, Macedo, and Travasarou (2017) [@BrayEtAl2017], calibrated for subduction zone interface earthquakes against 810 recordings with piecewise period-dependent coefficients; and Bray and Macedo (2019) [@BrayMacedo2019], calibrated for shallow crustal earthquakes against 6,711 NGA-West2 recordings with an additional near-fault velocity correction.

The ensemble of models is assembled into a logic tree in which each model is assigned a weight reflecting its applicability and calibration quality relative to the tectonic and geotechnical conditions at the site. The logic-tree structure retains model-to-model epistemic uncertainty explicitly, rather than collapsing it into a single representative prediction. In multi-tectonic settings - where both shallow crustal and subduction zone events contribute to site hazard - event-type-specific model subsets are weighted according to their respective contributions to the seismic hazard at each return period. The full distribution of $D_n(k_y)$ is obtained by Monte Carlo integration over the joint distribution of intensity measure values and model residuals, with each draw sampling a model from the logic tree and evaluating the corresponding log-displacement prediction.
