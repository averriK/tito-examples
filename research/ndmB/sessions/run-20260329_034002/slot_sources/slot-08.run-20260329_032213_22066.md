## SLOT 8: Rigid Block Assembly Methods and Applicability

The rigid-block assembly model is an ensemble of four independently calibrated regression relationships - Ambraseys and Menu (1988), Yegian, Marciano, and Varzaghian (1991), Jibson (2007), and Saygili and Rathje (2008) - assembled within a logic tree and evaluated against the rigid-block intensity measure predictors $\mathrm{PGA}$, Arias intensity $\mathrm{AI}$, and the critical acceleration ratio $r = k_y/\mathrm{PGA}$. The rigid-block assembly does not require $T_n$ or spectral acceleration $S_a$ as inputs, because the fundamental assumption of rigid-block behavior is that the sliding mass responds as an undistorted body to the base excitation without frequency-dependent internal amplification or de-amplification. The assembly retains within-model aleatory uncertainty through the model-specific $\sigma_{\ln D}$ and between-model epistemic uncertainty through logic-tree weight variation, yielding a composite lognormal predictive distribution analogous in structure to the flexible-block assembly. The rigid-block idealization is appropriate for shallow, translational failure surfaces in relatively stiff geomaterials whose fundamental period is short relative to the dominant periods of the input ground motion. This condition is characteristic of thin surficial layers, stiff compacted fills, and shallow planar slides in rock or dense soils where the deformability of the sliding mass is insufficient to produce significant internal dynamic amplification. Shallow failure surfaces involve sliding masses with small height-to-length ratios, so that the rigid-body motion assumption is kinematically consistent with the expected displacement field. In these cases, the critical acceleration ratio $r = k_y/\mathrm{PGA}$ captures the dominant first-order control on displacement, and the addition of Arias intensity as a secondary predictor accounts for record-to-record variability in cumulative energy not captured by $\mathrm{PGA}$ alone. Within the rigid-block ensemble, the four models exhibit a range of calibration database sizes, predictor sets, and functional forms that collectively provide complementary coverage of the displacement prediction space. Ambraseys and Menu (1988) and Yegian et al. (1991) represent foundational single-parameter models using only $r$ as predictor, providing displacement estimates anchored to the earliest systematic calibration efforts. Jibson (2007) introduces Arias intensity as a second predictor, capturing duration effects through cumulative ground-motion energy, and draws on a calibration database of 2,270 records from 30 worldwide earthquakes. Saygili and Rathje (2008) employs both $\mathrm{PGA}$ and $\mathrm{AI}$ simultaneously in a polynomial formulation calibrated against more than 2,000 records, with the distinctive feature that its dispersion $\sigma_{\ln D} = 0.46 + 0.56\,r$ increases as $r$ decreases, reflecting greater prediction uncertainty when the slope operates near its yield threshold. The assignment of logic-tree weights reflects the relative calibration quality and applicability range of each model for the site conditions under evaluation, with the multi-parameter models generally receiving higher weights where the additional predictors are well-characterized by the site hazard analysis.
