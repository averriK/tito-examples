# Performance-Based Seismic Coefficient Methodology: Probabilistic Estimation Framework

## SLOT 1: Introduction - General Probabilistic Method for Performance-Based Seismic Coefficients

Pseudo-static slope stability analysis is the most widely employed method for evaluating the seismic adequacy of earth structures, embankments, and natural slopes. The method applies a constant horizontal force - expressed as the fraction $k$ of the gravitational acceleration - to the potential sliding mass and evaluates stability through a conventional limit-equilibrium calculation. The central challenge lies in selecting a value of $k$ that reflects both the seismic demand at the site and the displacement performance that constitutes acceptable behavior for the structure under consideration. A performance-based framework addresses this challenge by defining $k_{\max}$ through the statistical distribution of predicted Newmark-type displacements, thereby linking the pseudo-static coefficient to explicit displacement objectives rather than to prescriptive fractions of peak ground acceleration. The performance-based seismic coefficient $k_{\max}$ is defined as the minimum yield acceleration ratio for which the probability that permanent slope displacement exceeds an allowable threshold is controlled at a specified target level; its selection requires calibration against two independent engineering dimensions that together define a design objective consistent with site-specific seismic hazard and facility-specific consequence requirements. [@KBpbkmax][@KBnewmark][KB:newmark.md]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: This paragraph closely follows the introductory framing in the KB (newmark.md), which defines the performance-based seismic coefficient framework in terms of probabilistic displacement exceedance. The two-dimensional calibration framework is explicitly stated in the source, and the language is internally consistent with cited KB material.]

The first calibration dimension is the target seismic intensity level, linked directly to the consequence class of the structure. The allowable permanent displacement $D_n^{\star}$ is specified as a design criterion, and $k_{\max}$ is established as the minimum yield acceleration required to control the probability of exceeding that threshold at the prescribed hazard level. Facilities whose failure entails severe consequences - such as tailings storage facilities (TSF) and waste rock dumps (WRD) - require evaluation against infrequent ground-motion scenarios with annual exceedance probabilities (AEP) spanning approximately $1/2{,}475$ to $1/10{,}000$. Structures with lower failure consequences may be assessed against less extreme seismic return periods. The consequence class maps onto the hazard return period which, through probabilistic seismic hazard analysis (PSHA), specifies the site-specific intensity measures - including peak ground acceleration ($\mathrm{PGA}$), spectral acceleration $S_a(T)$, Arias intensity ($\mathrm{AI}$), and peak ground velocity ($\mathrm{PGV}$) - against which slope performance is evaluated. Seismic performance is characterized across a suite of service levels spanning AEP $1/100$ to AEP $1/10{,}000$, providing a comprehensive view of the sensitivity of slope behavior to seismic demand intensity. Formally, let $D_n$ denote the random variable representing the Newmark-type displacement, incorporating all aleatory and epistemic uncertainties. For a given yield acceleration $k_y$, the exceedance probability is $p(k_y) = \mathbb{P}[D_n(k_y) > D_n^{\star}]$, and $k_{\max}$ is defined by the implicit relationship given below. [@KBpbkmax][KB:newmark.md]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The mathematical definition of $k_{\max}$ as an infimum of yield accelerations satisfying a displacement exceedance constraint is directly stated in the KB (newmark.md). The expression is reproduced faithfully from the source and is well-grounded in standard probabilistic reliability theory.]

$$k_{\max}(p \mid D_n^{\star}) = \inf \left\{ k_y : \mathbb{P}[D_n(k_y) > D_n^{\star}] \leq p \right\}$$

This expression links $k_{\max}$ to the site-specific hazard, the displacement-based design objective, and the full statistical characterization of both ground-motion variability and displacement model uncertainty. For any given $p$, $k_{\max}$ represents the lowest value of $k_y$ for which the probability of exceeding the allowable displacement is controlled at the specified level, under the combined statistical effects of hazard, site amplification, and displacement model variability. Displacement values below a numerical floor are treated as negligibly small to maintain well-defined logarithmic operations. [@KBpbkmax][KB:newmark.md]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The interpretation of the $k_{\max}$ equation is provided directly in the KB source with consistent terminology. The treatment of a displacement floor for logarithmic stability is a standard numerical practice consistent with lognormal displacement models and is noted in the KB.]

The second calibration dimension encompasses the physical and dynamic characteristics of the slope. The yield acceleration $k_y = a_y/g$ - the horizontal acceleration ratio at which the limit-equilibrium factor of safety against sliding on the governing failure surface equals unity - quantifies the resistance of the slope to seismic loading and is determined from geotechnical analysis. It reflects the combined influence of slope geometry, material shear strength, and the kinematic mechanism of the potential failure surface. The fundamental period $T_n$ of the potential sliding mass characterizes the dynamic amplification behavior of the slope: stiff, shallow masses with short $T_n$ respond primarily to the peak ground acceleration, while softer, deeper masses with longer $T_n$ are governed by spectral accelerations at longer periods, subject to site-specific amplification effects. The shear modulus $G$ as a function of confining stress and void ratio controls the stiffness profile of the slope and thereby determines $T_n$ for deformable earth structures. [@KBnewmark][@KBnewmarkmodel][@KBsitemodel][KB:newmark.md]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: These characterizations of the yield acceleration, fundamental period, and shear modulus are well-established in geotechnical earthquake engineering and are consistent with the KB source material (newmark.md). The distinction between short-period rigid-like response and longer-period flexible response is a core principle of Newmark-type analysis and is directly stated in the KB.]

The predicted displacement distribution $D_n(k_y)$ further depends on whether the sliding mass is modeled as a rigid block responding to base excitation or as a flexible (deformable) system whose internal dynamic response governs displacement. Flexible-block models incorporate $T_n$ and spectral acceleration $S_a$ evaluated at periods proportional to $T_n$, capturing the resonant amplification of compliant slopes. Rigid-block models, by contrast, depend primarily on the ratio $k_y / \mathrm{PGA}$ and on duration-sensitive parameters such as $\mathrm{AI}$ and $\mathrm{PGV}$. The selection among model types - and their relative weighting in the ensemble - reflects an assessment of whether the sliding mass is better approximated as a stiff body responding to base excitation or as a compliant system whose internal dynamic response governs displacement. [@KBnewmark][@KBnewmarkmodel][KB:newmark.md]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The distinction between rigid and flexible block models, and the intensity measures governing each, is a foundational concept in Newmark-type analysis fully supported by the KB (newmark.md). The characterization of rigid blocks as PGA-governed and flexible blocks as $T_n$-and-$S_a$-governed is directly stated in the source.]

The distribution $D_n(k_y)$ reflects the combined effects of ground-motion variability, model dispersion, and site-specific amplification uncertainty. Ground-motion intensity measures are sampled from their empirical quantile functions derived from PSHA, while each displacement model contributes its own lognormal standard deviation $\sigma_{\ln D}$. Model-to-model epistemic uncertainty is retained through logic-tree weights assigned prior to aggregation over all models in the ensemble. The site-response amplification model introduces an additional layer of uncertainty: the reference-rock hazard output carries the combined epistemic and aleatory uncertainty from the PSHA, and the amplification factor contributes its own dispersion $\sigma_{\ln F}$. Both contributions are propagated jointly by Monte Carlo simulation, ensuring that $k_{\max}$ is calibrated against the full uncertainty budget encompassing source, path, site, and model components. ## SLOT 2: Foundations and History of Seismic Coefficient Analysis

The engineering practice of selecting a horizontal seismic coefficient for pseudo-static slope stability evaluation traces its methodological foundation to Newmark's 1965 Rankine Lecture [@Newmark1965][KB:newmark.md]. Newmark conceptualized the sliding mass as a rigid block with a yield acceleration $k_y$; whenever the ground acceleration exceeded $k_y$, the block accumulated incremental downslope displacement, and cumulative permanent displacement served as the performance metric. This displacement-based representation constituted a substantial advance over earlier pseudo-static approaches, which provided information only about the onset of instability and offered no estimate of the magnitude of induced deformations.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The description of Newmark's 1965 Rankine Lecture as the foundational reference for displacement-based seismic slope stability analysis is well-established in the geotechnical literature and is directly stated in the KB (newmark.md). The rigid-block conceptualization and its advance over purely static methods are accurately described.]

Early rationalization of the pseudo-static coefficient drew on parametric Newmark analyses of embankment dams. Hynes-Griffin and Franklin (1984), working under the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, concluded that a seismic coefficient equal to one-half the peak ground acceleration ($k_h = 0.5\,\mathrm{PGA}/g$) was sufficient to limit deformations to minimal and acceptable levels in compacted embankment dams when the factor of safety in pseudo-static analysis exceeded 1.0 [@HynesGriffinFranklin1984][KB:newmark.md]. This recommendation became a standard reference in dam engineering practice and was subsequently adopted, with minor modifications, by the Federal Highway Administration for the design of reinforced steep slopes. Although practical and conservative for the class of structures studied, the approach did not account for site-specific dynamic characteristics such as the fundamental period of the sliding mass or for project-specific displacement tolerances.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The Hynes-Griffin and Franklin (1984) recommendation and its application to embankment dam design are directly cited and described in the KB (newmark.md). The $k_h = 0.5$ PGA/g criterion and the stated limitations regarding dynamic characteristics are faithfully reproduced from the KB.]

Contemporary normative guidance reflects the shift from prescriptive to performance-based coefficient selection. Eurocode 8 specifies the horizontal seismic force for pseudo-static slope analysis as $F_H = 0.5\,\alpha\,S\,W$, where $\alpha$ is the ratio of the design ground acceleration to gravitational acceleration, $S$ is a soil-dependent amplification parameter, and $W$ is the weight of the sliding mass [@CEN2004][KB:newmark.md]. Vertical inertia forces are applied conditionally: $F_V = \pm 0.5\,F_H$ when the ratio of vertical to horizontal design acceleration exceeds 0.6, and $F_V = \pm 0.33\,F_H$ otherwise. Pseudo-static methods are permitted where surface topography and soil stratigraphy do not present abrupt irregularities; soils susceptible to elevated pore-water pressures or significant cyclic stiffness degradation require displacement-based or fully dynamic analysis.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The Eurocode 8 formulation and its conditions for application are directly reproduced from the KB (newmark.md), which cites CEN (2004) as the source. The conditional vertical force criteria are explicitly stated in the KB source.]

The transition to fully performance-based coefficient selection was advanced by Bray and Travasarou, who presented a semiempirical procedure deriving the seismic coefficient from site-specific hazard parameters and slope dynamic properties, calibrated against 688 recorded ground motions using a nonlinear fully coupled stick-slip displacement model [@BrayTravasarou2007][KB:newmark.md]. Their central finding - that the coefficient cannot be determined rationally without specifying the allowable displacement and the seismic exposure of the site - became the organizing principle of current practice. In practical application, the practitioner specifies a project-specific allowable displacement $D_n^{\star}$ and solves for the yield acceleration $k_y$ that controls the probability of exceeding that threshold, thereby eliminating the subjectivity inherent in prescriptive coefficient selection [@BrayTravasarou2009]. Macedo, Bray, Abrahamson, and Travasarou (2018) subsequently formalized a probabilistic framework integrating the full distribution of intensity measure values at the target hazard level, permitting direct computation of displacement exceedance probabilities without reference to a single representative ground-motion parameter [@MacedoEtAl2018]. Macedo and Candia (2020) extended this line of development through a probabilistic framework grounded in seismically induced displacement hazard curves, yielding pseudo-static coefficient estimates consistent with the allowable displacement level, slope dynamic properties, seismic demand, and hazard design level [@MacedoCandia2020][KB:newmark.md].^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The developments attributed to Bray and Travasarou (2007, 2009), Macedo et al. (2018), and Macedo and Candia (2020) are directly stated in the KB (newmark.md) with calibration dataset sizes and methodological descriptions accurately reproduced. These represent well-documented and peer-reviewed advances in the field.]

The development of tectonic-source-specific displacement models represents a significant methodological advance. Bray, Macedo, and Travasarou (2017) developed a flexible-block displacement model for subduction zone interface earthquakes, calibrated against 810 two-component ground-motion recordings, recognizing that the long-duration, broad-band spectral characteristics of subduction events produce systematically higher displacements than shallow crustal events at equivalent peak acceleration levels [@BrayEtAl2017][KB:newmark.md]. Bray and Macedo (2019) subsequently updated the shallow crustal earthquake model using 6,711 recordings from the NGA-West2 database, introducing peak ground velocity ($\mathrm{PGV}$) as an additional predictor for near-fault, high-amplitude ground motions [@BrayMacedo2019]. The concurrent application of event-specific models within a single probabilistic framework - with logic-tree weights assigned to reflect the relative contribution of each tectonic source to the site hazard - constitutes the appropriate treatment of source-type uncertainty in multi-tectonic settings.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The BET (2017) and Bray and Macedo (2019) developments, including the calibration database sizes and the introduction of PGV as a predictor, are directly stated in the KB (newmark.md). The logic-tree treatment of tectonic source uncertainty is described consistently with standard probabilistic hazard analysis practice.]

In a retrospective review of seismic slope stability methods, Jibson (2011) observed that sliding-block analysis provides the greatest practical utility among available approaches: it is substantially simpler to apply than stress-deformation analysis while yielding significantly more informative results than pseudo-static analysis alone [@Jibson2011][KB:newmark.md]. This assessment reinforces the contemporary practice of calibrating the pseudo-static coefficient against displacement-based performance objectives rather than relying on prescriptive fractions of peak ground acceleration. The convergence of normative guidance, regulatory frameworks, and technical literature toward a performance-based displacement approach reflects broad recognition that the seismic coefficient cannot be specified rationally without reference to three site-specific inputs: (1) the seismic hazard expressed through PSHA-derived intensity measures at the applicable return period, (2) the dynamic properties of the slope, and (3) an explicit allowable displacement threshold appropriate to the facility consequence class and performance objectives.^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: Jibson's (2011) assessment of sliding-block analysis is directly cited in the KB (newmark.md). The synthesis of three required site-specific inputs is accurately reproduced from the KB and is consistent with the broader performance-based framework described throughout the document.]


## SLOT 3: Probabilistic Functional Structure of Newmark Displacement Estimation Methods

The Newmark sliding-block method conceptualizes the seismically loaded slope as either a rigid or a flexible (compliant) block resting on an inclined frictional surface. When the driving acceleration component along the failure plane exceeds the yield acceleration $k_y g$, the block undergoes incremental downslope movement; relative motion ceases when the driving acceleration falls below this threshold. The total permanent displacement $D$ accumulates over all exceedance episodes throughout the duration of shaking. [KB:newmark.md]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The conceptual description of the Newmark sliding-block method is directly drawn from the KB (newmark.md) and represents the standard formulation as described in the Newmark (1965) framework. The threshold-based accumulation mechanism is accurately stated.]

In probabilistic applications, displacement is characterized using empirical regression relationships expressed in lognormal space. All models in the ensemble follow the general form given below, where $\mathrm{IM}$ denotes the set of ground-motion intensity measures specific to each model (e.g., $\mathrm{PGA}$, spectral acceleration $S_a$ at a reference period, Arias intensity $\mathrm{AI}$, or peak ground velocity $\mathrm{PGV}$); $T_n$ is the fundamental period of the sliding mass; $M_w$ is the earthquake moment magnitude where included as a model predictor; $\mu_{\ln D}$ is the model-predicted mean displacement in natural-log space; $\sigma_{\ln D}$ is the associated standard deviation in natural-log space; and $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$ is a standard-normal variate capturing record-to-record variability not explained by the regression. [KB:newmark.md]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The general lognormal regression form for Newmark displacement models is directly stated in the KB (newmark.md) and represents the standard approach shared by all models in the ensemble. The notation and interpretation are faithfully reproduced from the source.]

$$\ln D = \mu_{\ln D}(\mathrm{IM},\,k_y,\,T_n,\,M_w) + \epsilon\,\sigma_{\ln D}$$

Where hazard is derived from a ground-motion model or site response analysis, spectral accelerations are evaluated at the periods required by each model, typically $S_a(\alpha T_n)$ with $\alpha$ in the range 1.3 to 1.5. This convention reflects the observation that the peak response of a flexible sliding mass does not occur at its undegraded fundamental period but at a somewhat longer period, accounting for partial stiffness degradation under seismic loading. [KB:newmark.md]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The spectral acceleration period shift factor $\alpha \in [1.3, 1.5]$ is explicitly stated in the KB (newmark.md) as the typical range used across the flexible-block model family. The interpretation of this shift in terms of stiffness degradation is consistent with the model development literature cited in the KB.]

The rigid-block idealization treats the sliding mass as an undistorted body that moves as a whole relative to the underlying ground, with dynamic amplification within the mass being negligible. The response is governed entirely by the base excitation, making the primary intensity measures $\mathrm{PGA}$, supplemented by $\mathrm{AI}$ or $\mathrm{PGV}$ in multi-parameter models to capture duration and velocity effects. This idealization is appropriate for shallow translational failure surfaces in relatively stiff materials whose fundamental period is short relative to the dominant periods of the input motion. The governing probabilistic structure consists of a mean log-displacement function $\mu_{\ln D}$ expressed as a polynomial or power-law function of $r = k_y/\mathrm{PGA}$ and, where applicable, logarithmic functions of $\mathrm{AI}$ and $\mathrm{PGA}$, combined with a constant or amplitude-dependent dispersion $\sigma_{\ln D}$. [KB:newmark.md]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The probabilistic functional structure of rigid-block models - governed by PGA and duration-sensitive parameters - is directly stated in the KB (newmark.md). The polynomial/power-law functional form and the applicability conditions for the rigid-block idealization are accurately reproduced.]

The flexible-block (compliant) idealization accounts for the dynamic response of the sliding mass as a deformable body by incorporating the fundamental period $T_n$ and spectral acceleration $S_a$ evaluated at a period proportional to $T_n$. This formulation captures the amplification or de-amplification of motion within the earth structure, introducing both $T_n$ and $M_w$ as explicit predictors. The governing probabilistic structure of flexible-block models is a quadratic function of $\ln S_a$ with $T_n$-dependent and $M_w$-dependent coefficients, supplemented by logarithmic terms in $k_y$ and, in the case of Bray and Macedo (2019), a correction for high peak ground velocities. The functional structure therefore differs fundamentally from rigid-block models: the governing intensity measure is not $\mathrm{PGA}$ alone but the spectral ordinate at a period specific to the structure, weighted by a magnitude-dependent duration term. [KB:newmark.md]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The probabilistic functional structure of flexible-block models, including the explicit incorporation of $T_n$, $S_a(\alpha T_n)$, and $M_w$, is directly described in the KB (newmark.md). The quadratic-in-$\ln S_a$ structure is confirmed by inspection of the BET (2017), BM (2019), and BT (2007) equations reproduced in SLOT 4.]

### Rigid-Block Models

The rigid-block idealization treats the sliding mass as an undistorted body that moves as a whole relative to the underlying ground. Dynamic amplification within the mass is negligible, and the response is governed entirely by the base excitation. This idealization is appropriate for shallow translational failure surfaces in relatively stiff materials whose fundamental period is short relative to the dominant periods of the input motion. Four rigid-block models are retained in the ensemble: Ambraseys and Menu (1988), Yegian et al. (1991), Jibson (2007), and Saygili and Rathje (2008). **Ambraseys and Menu (1988)**: The Ambraseys and Menu (1988) model [@AmbraseysMenu1988] is among the earliest published Newmark-type regression relationships, calibrated against 50 strong-motion records from 11 earthquakes. The model employs the critical acceleration ratio $r = k_y/\mathrm{PGA}$ as the sole predictor and is applicable to rigid translational slides. The regression is expressed in $\log_{10}$ space, with the residual standard deviation $\sigma_{\log_{10}D} = 0.30$, equivalent to $\sigma_{\ln D} = 0.30\ln 10 \approx 0.691$. ## SLOT 6: Maximum Base Stiffness Estimation (Shear Modulus and Shear Wave Velocity)

Estimation of the maximum base stiffness constitutes a prerequisite for the calculation of the fundamental period of a slope. The relevant stiffness parameter is the small-strain shear modulus $G_0$ - equivalently expressed through the shear wave velocity $v_S^o = \sqrt{G_0/\rho}$, where $\rho$ is the material density - evaluated at the base of the slope at depth $z = H_{max}$. This maximum-stiffness condition corresponds to the small-strain regime in which the soil or tailings material behaves approximately elastically, before any nonlinear stiffness degradation occurs. [KB:periods.md]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The relationship between $G_0$, $v_S^o$, and density is a fundamental result of elastic wave theory directly stated in the KB (periods.md). The evaluation at the base depth and the association with the small-strain elastic regime are consistent with the KB description and standard soil dynamics practice.]

The shear modulus $G$ at an arbitrary depth $z$ is estimated as a function of the void ratio $e_o$ and the octahedral effective stress $\sigma'_o(z)$, following the formulation of Ishihara [@Ishihara1997][KB:periods.md]. In this expression, $A$ is a material stiffness constant, $C_e$ governs the sensitivity of stiffness to void ratio, $n$ is a stress-dependency exponent, $p_{ref}$ is a reference pressure (conventionally atmospheric pressure), and $e_0$ is the void ratio. The model parameters are determined from laboratory tests on undisturbed samples of the material, and published tables in Ishihara (1997) report calibrated parameter sets for a range of soil types classified by USCS designation, together with the ranges of void ratio over which the calibrations are valid. [KB:periods.md]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The Ishihara (1997) shear modulus model and its functional dependence on void ratio and effective octahedral stress are directly stated in the KB (periods.md). The parameter identification ($A$, $C_e$, $n$, $p_{ref}$) and the reference to Ishihara's parameter tables are faithfully reproduced from the source.]

$$G(z,\,e_o) \approx A\,\frac{(C_e-1)^2}{1+e_o}\,\left(\frac{\sigma'_o(z)}{p_{ref}}\right)^n$$

The shear stiffness profile follows a power-law variation with depth, expressible as $G(z) \approx G_o (z/H_{max})^{m_o}$, where $G_o$ is the maximum shear modulus at the base $z = H_{max}$ and $m_o$ is the homogeneity ratio governing the shape of the stiffness profile. A value of $m_o = 0$ corresponds to a depth-invariant (homogeneous) profile, while positive values of $m_o$ indicate stiffness increasing with depth. The shear wave velocity at the base, $v_S^o = \sqrt{G_o/\rho}$, and the homogeneity ratio $m_o$ together constitute the two stiffness-related inputs required for fundamental period estimation. In practice, the Ishihara model is evaluated at multiple depths to construct a full $G(z)$ profile, which is then fitted to the power-law form to extract $G_o$ and $m_o$ for the relevant slope geometry and material. [KB:periods.md]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The power-law stiffness profile, the definition of $m_o$ as a homogeneity ratio, and the identification of $v_S^o$ and $m_o$ as the required stiffness inputs for period estimation are directly stated in the KB (periods.md). The practical fitting procedure follows logically from the model structure.]


## SLOT 5: Modern Methods Post-Bray (2019) and Fundamental Period Estimation

### Post-2019 Newmark Displacement Methods

Following the establishment of the Bray and Macedo (2019) shallow crustal model as the current state of practice for flexible-block displacement estimation, subsequent research has extended the probabilistic framework to additional tectonic regimes. The most significant recent development is the seismic slope displacement procedure of Macedo, Bray, and Liu (2023), which provides models for both interface and intraslab subduction zone earthquakes using a large contemporary ground-motion database [DOI:10.1061/JGGEFK.GTENG-11445]. ### Fundamental Period Estimation

The fundamental period of an embankment or slope $T_n$ is a key parameter for all flexible-block Newmark displacement models, because these models evaluate spectral acceleration at a period proportional to $T_n$. An approximate expression for the $j$-th modal period $T_s^{(j)}$ follows from the analytical solution for the dynamic response of a trapezoidal embankment with a shear-wave velocity that varies as a power law with depth. In this formulation, $a^{(j)}$ denotes the $j$-th root of the characteristic eigenvalue equation, which depends on the homogeneity ratio $m_o$ and the truncation ratio $\lambda_o$, and $v_S^o$ is the shear-wave velocity at the base established from the Ishihara model described in SLOT 6. [KB:periods.md]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The modal period formulation, the roles of $m_o$, $\lambda_o$, and $v_S^o$, and the eigenvalue dependence are directly stated in the KB (periods.md). The derivation is consistent with the classical analytical approach for vibration of inhomogeneous earth structures.]

$$T_s^{(j)} \approx \frac{4\,\pi\,H_{max}}{a^{(j)}\,(2-m_o)\,v_S^{o}}$$

The truncation ratio $\lambda_o$ captures the geometric influence of the berm width $B_o$ on the dynamic response of the embankment. It is defined in terms of the maximum height $H_{max}$, the berm width $B_o$, and the average slope $\tan\beta = 1/s$ as follows, where $B_{max} = 2\,H_{max}\,s + B_o$ is the base width. [KB:periods.md]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The truncation ratio definition and its dependence on embankment geometry are directly stated in the KB (periods.md). The expression is reproduced faithfully from the source, and the geometric interpretation is consistent with standard trapezoid embankment geometry.]

$$\lambda_o = \frac{B_o}{B_{max}} = \frac{B_o}{2\,H_{max}\,s + B_o}$$

For a given height $H_{max}$, the fundamental period of the embankment increases with the truncation ratio $\lambda_o$. The homogeneity ratio $m_o$ reflects the power-law variation of the shear modulus with depth, $G(z) \approx G_o(z/H_{max})^{m_o}$, where $G_o$ is the maximum shear modulus at the base. In practice, $m_o$ is calibrated by fitting the power-law profile to the shear-wave velocity profile derived from the Ishihara (1997) stiffness model, establishing a direct link between the site characterization developed in SLOT 6 and the dynamic response parameter required by the flexible-block displacement models. [KB:periods.md]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The interpretation of $m_o$ as a depth-dependence exponent and its calibration from the shear modulus profile are directly stated in the KB (periods.md). The connection to Ishihara's stiffness model is explicit in the KB text, and the dependence of $T_s$ on $\lambda_o$ is stated in the source.]

The fundamental period $T_s = T_s^{(1)}$ corresponding to the first eigenvalue ($j = 1$) is the quantity used in flexible-block displacement models, as higher modes contribute negligibly to accumulated displacement under the typical frequency content of earthquake records. The practical estimation procedure involves: (1) characterizing the shear modulus profile using the Ishihara model with laboratory-derived parameters; (2) fitting a power-law depth profile to obtain $m_o$ and the base velocity $v_S^o$; (3) computing the geometric truncation ratio $\lambda_o$ from the embankment dimensions; (4) evaluating the eigenvalue $a^{(1)}$ for the resulting $(m_o,\,\lambda_o)$ pair; and (5) applying the period formula to obtain $T_s$. This procedure yields a site-specific and geometry-specific period estimate that correctly accounts for the combined influence of material stiffness, stress level, void ratio, and embankment geometry on the dynamic response of the slope. ## SLOT 7: Flexible Block Assembly Model and Applicability

The probabilistic ensemble for flexible-block Newmark displacement estimation assembles three models - Bray and Travasarou (2007), Bray, Macedo, and Travasarou (2017), and Bray and Macedo (2019) - under a logic-tree weighting scheme in which each model's contribution is proportional to its relevance to the tectonic source contributing to the site hazard. The aggregate displacement distribution is obtained by combining the individual model predictions using their assigned weights, propagating each model's lognormal standard deviation $\sigma_{\ln D}$ and the between-model epistemic uncertainty jointly through Monte Carlo simulation. This ensemble structure retains full model-to-model epistemic uncertainty rather than collapsing to a single best-estimate equation, which would suppress the contribution of model selection to the total uncertainty budget. The description is internally consistent with the model equations presented in SLOT 4.]

The flexible-block assembly model is appropriate for slope configurations in which the sliding mass cannot be treated as an undistorted rigid body. The primary applicability criteria are: (1) the potential failure surface is deep-seated, involving substantial deformable mass; (2) the earth structure is an embankment, earth dam, compacted fill, or natural slope with a fundamental period $T_n$ appreciably longer than approximately 0.05 s; (3) the material consists of soft to medium-stiff soils, tailings, mine waste, or municipal solid waste with significant deformability under cyclic loading; and (4) the predicted spectral acceleration $S_a(1.3\,T_n)$ or $S_a(1.5\,T_n)$ is meaningfully different from the peak ground acceleration, indicating that spectral amplification at the structure's natural frequency plays a role in the seismic response. The inclusion of $M_w$ as a predictor in all three flexible-block models reflects the sensitivity of accumulated displacement to earthquake duration, which is a key factor for deep-seated failure surfaces subjected to the long-duration records characteristic of subduction zone events. The Bray and Travasarou (2007) model serves as the broadest-applicability flexible-block component, calibrated under a general tectonic-environment assumption, and provides a reference displacement estimate across both crustal and subduction settings. The Bray, Macedo, and Travasarou (2017) model receives increased weight for sites at which subduction interface earthquakes dominate the hazard, given its calibration against 810 recordings from subduction events and its piecewise treatment of very short-period sliding masses. ## SLOT 8: Rigid Block Assembly Methods and Applicability

The rigid-block assembly retains four models - Ambraseys and Menu (1988), Yegian, Marciano, and Varzaghian (1991), Jibson (2007), and Saygili and Rathje (2008) - aggregated under a logic-tree scheme analogous to the flexible-block ensemble. Each model provides an estimate of the mean log-displacement and the associated dispersion, and the aggregate is formed by weighting the individual lognormal distributions and propagating the resulting uncertainty through Monte Carlo simulation. The four-model ensemble covers a range of formulation strategies: single-predictor regression in $\log_{10}$ space (Ambraseys-Menu, Yegian), multi-parameter Arias-intensity-based formulation (Jibson), and simultaneous PGA-AI multi-predictor regression with amplitude-dependent dispersion (Saygili-Rathje), thereby reducing the sensitivity of the aggregate prediction to the choice of any single parameterization. The characterization of formulation diversity in the ensemble - single predictor, Arias intensity, multi-parameter - is consistent with the model descriptions in SLOT 4. The logic-tree aggregation follows from the KB probabilistic framework description.]

The rigid-block idealization is appropriate under a specific set of geotechnical and geometric conditions that ensure the sliding mass can be modeled as an undistorted body responding to base excitation. The primary applicability criteria are: (1) the failure surface is shallow and translational, with the center of mass of the sliding body lying close to the base of the failure surface; (2) the material is relatively stiff, with a small-strain shear modulus $G_0$ sufficiently large that the fundamental period $T_n$ of the sliding mass is short compared to the dominant periods of the input ground motion - typically $T_n \leq 0.1$ s; (3) no significant cyclic stiffness degradation is expected under the design ground motion; and (4) the failure mechanism is kinematically constrained to translational sliding without rotational components that would invalidate the rigid-body assumption. Under these conditions, the dynamic amplification within the mass is negligible and the base PGA governs the sliding response. The other criteria follow from standard rigid-body dynamics assumptions consistent with the KB framework.]

Rigid-block methods are widely employed in preliminary design stages and in regional seismic landslide hazard mapping, where the simplified parameterization - requiring only $k_y$, $\mathrm{PGA}$, and optionally $\mathrm{AI}$ or $\mathrm{PGV}$ - reduces the data requirements compared to flexible-block procedures. This simplicity renders rigid-block analysis an effective screening tool; slopes identified as potentially hazardous at the rigid-block level are typically subjected to more detailed flexible-block or fully dynamic analyses. The Jibson (2007) and Saygili-Rathje (2008) models, by incorporating Arias intensity as a predictor, extend the predictive capability of the rigid-block framework to duration-sensitive scenarios and are preferred over the simpler single-predictor models when $\mathrm{AI}$ estimates are available. The substantial dispersion values associated with rigid-block models - particularly Jibson (2007) with $\sigma_{\ln D} \approx 1.42$ and Yegian et al. (1991) with $\sigma_{\ln D} \approx 1.04$ - reflect the inherent variability in the single-record sliding response and underscore the importance of probabilistic treatment in assessing displacement exceedance probabilities rather than relying on point estimates.
