## SLOT 1: Introduction - General Probabilistic Method for Performance-Based Seismic Coefficients

Pseudo-static slope stability analysis is the most widely employed method for evaluating the seismic adequacy of earth structures, embankments, and natural slopes. The method applies a constant horizontal force - expressed as the fraction $k$ of the gravitational acceleration - to the potential sliding mass and evaluates stability through a conventional limit-equilibrium calculation. The central challenge lies in selecting a value of $k$ that reflects both the seismic demand at the site and the displacement performance that constitutes acceptable behavior for the structure under consideration. A performance-based framework addresses this challenge by defining $k_{\max}$ through the statistical distribution of predicted Newmark-type displacements, thereby linking the pseudo-static coefficient to explicit displacement objectives rather than to prescriptive fractions of peak ground acceleration. The performance-based seismic coefficient $k_{\max}$ is defined as the minimum yield acceleration ratio for which the probability that permanent slope displacement exceeds an allowable threshold is controlled at a specified target level; its selection requires calibration against two independent engineering dimensions that together define a design objective consistent with site-specific seismic hazard and facility-specific consequence requirements. [KB:newmark.md]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: This paragraph accurately describes the pseudo-static method and the performance-based kmax framework as explicitly stated in newmark.md SLOT 1. The definitions and motivations are directly supported by the KB content with no evidence of invention or contradiction.]

The first calibration dimension is the target seismic intensity level, linked directly to the consequence class of the structure. The allowable permanent displacement $D_n^{\star}$ is specified as a design criterion, and $k_{\max}$ is established as the minimum yield acceleration required to control the probability of exceeding that threshold at the prescribed hazard level. Facilities whose failure entails severe consequences - such as tailings storage facilities (TSF) and waste rock dumps (WRD) - require evaluation against infrequent ground-motion scenarios with annual exceedance probabilities (AEP) spanning approximately $1/2{,}475$ to $1/10{,}000$. Structures with lower failure consequences may be assessed against less extreme seismic return periods. The consequence class maps onto the hazard return period which, through probabilistic seismic hazard analysis (PSHA), specifies the site-specific intensity measures - including peak ground acceleration ($\mathrm{PGA}$), spectral acceleration $S_a(T)$, Arias intensity ($\mathrm{AI}$), and peak ground velocity ($\mathrm{PGV}$) - against which slope performance is evaluated. Seismic performance is characterized across a suite of service levels spanning AEP $1/100$ to AEP $1/10{,}000$, providing a comprehensive view of the sensitivity of slope behavior to seismic demand intensity. [KB:newmark.md]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The calibration dimension concept, the AEP ranges for TSF and WRD (1/2475 to 1/10000), and the intensity measure types are explicitly described in newmark.md SLOT 1. The specific AEP values are cited from the KB and are not invented.]

Formally, let $D_n$ denote the random variable representing the Newmark-type displacement, incorporating all aleatory and epistemic uncertainties. For a given yield acceleration $k_y$, the exceedance probability is $p(k_y) = \mathbb{P}[D_n(k_y) > D_n^{\star}]$, and $k_{\max}$ is defined by the implicit relationship: [KB:newmark.md]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: This lead-in to the formal mathematical definition of kmax is reproduced directly from newmark.md SLOT 1. The probabilistic framing is consistent with the cited source.]

$$k_{\max}(p \mid D_n^{\star}) = \inf \left\{ k_y : \mathbb{P}[D_n(k_y) > D_n^{\star}] \leq p \right\}$$

This expression links $k_{\max}$ to the site-specific hazard, the displacement-based design objective, and the full statistical characterization of both ground-motion variability and displacement model uncertainty. For any given $p$, $k_{\max}$ represents the lowest value of $k_y$ for which the probability of exceeding the allowable displacement is controlled at the specified level, under the combined statistical effects of hazard, site amplification, and displacement model variability. Displacement values below a numerical floor are treated as negligibly small to maintain well-defined logarithmic operations. [KB:newmark.md]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The interpretation of the kmax expression and the treatment of a displacement floor are directly stated in newmark.md SLOT 1. The explanatory content is faithful to the KB source.]

The second calibration dimension encompasses the physical and dynamic characteristics of the slope. The yield acceleration $k_y = a_y/g$ - the horizontal acceleration ratio at which the limit-equilibrium factor of safety against sliding on the governing failure surface equals unity - quantifies the resistance of the slope to seismic loading and is determined from geotechnical analysis. It reflects the combined influence of slope geometry, material shear strength, and the kinematic mechanism of the potential failure surface. The fundamental period $T_n$ of the potential sliding mass characterizes the dynamic amplification behavior of the slope: stiff, shallow masses with short $T_n$ respond primarily to the peak ground acceleration, while softer, deeper masses with longer $T_n$ are governed by spectral accelerations at longer periods, subject to site-specific amplification effects. The shear modulus $G$ as a function of confining stress and void ratio controls the stiffness profile of the slope and thereby determines $T_n$ for deformable earth structures. [KB:newmark.md][KB:periods.md]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The second calibration dimension and its components (ky, Tn, G) are directly described in newmark.md SLOT 1. The relationship between shear modulus and Tn is further supported by periods.md, which provides the explicit formulas linking G_o to V_s and Tn. No claims are invented.]

The predicted displacement distribution $D_n(k_y)$ further depends on whether the sliding mass is modeled as a rigid block responding to base excitation or as a flexible (deformable) system whose internal dynamic response governs displacement. Flexible-block models incorporate $T_n$ and spectral acceleration $S_a$ evaluated at periods proportional to $T_n$, capturing the resonant amplification of compliant slopes. Rigid-block models, by contrast, depend primarily on the ratio $k_y / \mathrm{PGA}$ and on duration-sensitive parameters such as $\mathrm{AI}$ and $\mathrm{PGV}$. The selection among model types - and their relative weighting in the ensemble - reflects an assessment of whether the sliding mass is better approximated as a stiff body responding to base excitation or as a compliant system whose internal dynamic response governs displacement. [KB:newmark.md]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The distinction between rigid-block and flexible-block models, and the role of their respective predictors, is explicitly described in newmark.md SLOTs 1 and 3. The content is directly supported by the KB.]

The distribution $D_n(k_y)$ reflects the combined effects of ground-motion variability, model dispersion, and site-specific amplification uncertainty. Ground-motion intensity measures are sampled from their empirical quantile functions derived from PSHA, while each displacement model contributes its own lognormal standard deviation $\sigma_{\ln D}$. Model-to-model epistemic uncertainty is retained through logic-tree weights assigned prior to aggregation over all models in the ensemble. The site-response amplification model introduces an additional layer of uncertainty: the reference-rock hazard output carries the combined epistemic and aleatory uncertainty from the PSHA, and the amplification factor contributes its own dispersion $\sigma_{\ln F}$. Both contributions are propagated jointly by Monte Carlo simulation, ensuring that $k_{\max}$ is calibrated against the full uncertainty budget encompassing source, path, site, and model components. ## SLOT 2: Foundations and History of Seismic Coefficient Analysis

The engineering practice of selecting a horizontal seismic coefficient for pseudo-static slope stability evaluation traces its methodological foundation to Newmark's 1965 Rankine Lecture [@Newmark1965]. Newmark conceptualized the sliding mass as a rigid block with a yield acceleration $k_y$; whenever the ground acceleration exceeded $k_y$, the block accumulated incremental downslope displacement, and cumulative permanent displacement served as the performance metric. This displacement-based representation constituted a substantial advance over earlier pseudo-static approaches, which provided information only about the onset of instability and offered no estimate of the magnitude of induced deformations. [KB:newmark.md]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The historical attribution to Newmark (1965) Rankine Lecture and the description of the rigid-block sliding concept are explicitly stated in newmark.md SLOT 2. The advantage over stability-only approaches is a standard characterization in the literature and is consistent with the KB.]

Early rationalization of the pseudo-static coefficient drew on parametric Newmark analyses of embankment dams. Hynes-Griffin and Franklin (1984), working under the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, concluded that a seismic coefficient equal to one-half the peak ground acceleration ($k_h = 0.5\,\mathrm{PGA}/g$) was sufficient to limit deformations to minimal and acceptable levels in compacted embankment dams when the factor of safety in pseudo-static analysis exceeded 1.0 [@HynesGriffinFranklin1984]. This recommendation became a standard reference in dam engineering practice and was subsequently adopted, with minor modifications, by the Federal Highway Administration for the design of reinforced steep slopes. Although practical and conservative for the class of structures studied, the approach did not account for site-specific dynamic characteristics such as the fundamental period of the sliding mass or for project-specific displacement tolerances. [KB:newmark.md]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The Hynes-Griffin and Franklin (1984) recommendation (kh = 0.5 PGA/g, FS > 1.0) and its adoption by the FHWA are directly cited in newmark.md SLOT 2. The limitations regarding Tn and displacement tolerances are explicitly noted in the KB.]

Contemporary normative guidance reflects the shift from prescriptive to performance-based coefficient selection. Eurocode 8 specifies the horizontal seismic force for pseudo-static slope analysis as $F_H = 0.5\,\alpha\,S\,W$, where $\alpha$ is the ratio of the design ground acceleration to gravitational acceleration, $S$ is a soil-dependent amplification parameter, and $W$ is the weight of the sliding mass [@CEN2004]. Vertical inertia forces are applied conditionally: $F_V = \pm 0.5\,F_H$ when the ratio of vertical to horizontal design acceleration exceeds 0.6, and $F_V = \pm 0.33\,F_H$ otherwise. Pseudo-static methods are permitted where surface topography and soil stratigraphy do not present abrupt irregularities; soils susceptible to elevated pore-water pressures or significant cyclic stiffness degradation require displacement-based or fully dynamic analysis. [KB:newmark.md]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The Eurocode 8 formulations for FH, FV conditions, and applicability constraints are directly cited in newmark.md SLOT 2 with reference to CEN (2004). The conditional expressions for vertical force are reproduced faithfully from the KB.]

The transition to fully performance-based coefficient selection was advanced by Bray and Travasarou, who presented a semiempirical procedure deriving the seismic coefficient from site-specific hazard parameters and slope dynamic properties, calibrated against 688 recorded ground motions using a nonlinear fully coupled stick-slip displacement model [@BrayTravasarou2007]. Their central finding - that the coefficient cannot be determined rationally without specifying the allowable displacement and the seismic exposure of the site - became the organizing principle of current practice. In practical application, the analyst specifies a project-specific allowable displacement $D_n^{\star}$ and solves for the yield acceleration $k_y$ that controls the probability of exceeding that threshold, thereby eliminating the subjectivity inherent in prescriptive coefficient selection [@BrayTravasarou2009]. [KB:newmark.md]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The Bray and Travasarou (2007, 2009) work and its key finding regarding the necessity of specifying allowable displacement and seismic exposure are directly described in newmark.md SLOT 2. The calibration database size (688 recordings) is explicitly stated in the KB.]

Macedo, Bray, Abrahamson, and Travasarou (2018) subsequently formalized a probabilistic framework integrating the full distribution of intensity measure values at the target hazard level, permitting direct computation of displacement exceedance probabilities without reference to a single representative ground-motion parameter [@MacedoEtAl2018]. Macedo and Candia (2020) extended this line of development through a probabilistic framework grounded in seismically induced displacement hazard curves, yielding pseudo-static coefficient estimates consistent with the allowable displacement level, slope dynamic properties, seismic demand, and hazard design level [@MacedoCandia2020]. This formulation operationalized the displacement hazard curve as the primary vehicle for calibrating $k_{\max}$, unifying site hazard characterization and displacement model uncertainty within a single probabilistic calculation. [KB:newmark.md]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The Macedo et al. (2018) and Macedo and Candia (2020) frameworks are explicitly described in newmark.md SLOT 2. The DOI for Macedo and Candia (2020) is confirmed by web search at DOI: 10.1016/j.soildyn.2020.106109. Claims about the probabilistic framework and displacement hazard curve approach are directly supported by the KB.]

The development of tectonic-source-specific displacement models represents a significant methodological advance in the field. Bray, Macedo, and Travasarou (2017) developed a flexible-block displacement model for subduction zone interface earthquakes, calibrated against 810 two-component ground-motion recordings, recognizing that the long-duration, broad-band spectral characteristics of subduction events produce systematically higher displacements than shallow crustal events at equivalent peak acceleration levels [@BrayEtAl2017]. Bray and Macedo (2019) subsequently updated the shallow crustal earthquake model using 6,711 recordings from the NGA-West2 database, introducing peak ground velocity ($\mathrm{PGV}$) as an additional predictor for near-fault, high-amplitude ground motions [@BrayMacedo2019]. The concurrent application of event-specific models within a single probabilistic framework - with logic-tree weights assigned to reflect the relative contribution of each tectonic source to the site hazard - constitutes the appropriate treatment of source-type uncertainty in multi-tectonic settings. [KB:newmark.md]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The Bray et al. (2017) and Bray and Macedo (2019) models, including calibration database sizes and key predictor additions, are explicitly described in newmark.md SLOT 2. The logic-tree treatment of source-type uncertainty is also stated in the KB.]

In a retrospective review of seismic slope stability methods, Jibson (2011) observed that sliding-block analysis provides the greatest practical utility among available approaches: it is substantially simpler to apply than stress-deformation analysis while yielding significantly more informative results than pseudo-static analysis alone [@Jibson2011]. This assessment reinforces the contemporary practice of calibrating the pseudo-static coefficient against displacement-based performance objectives rather than relying on prescriptive fractions of peak ground acceleration. The convergence of normative guidance, regulatory frameworks, and technical literature toward a performance-based displacement approach reflects broad recognition that the seismic coefficient cannot be specified rationally without reference to three site-specific inputs: the seismic hazard expressed through PSHA-derived intensity measures at the applicable return period, the dynamic properties of the slope, and an explicit allowable displacement threshold appropriate to the facility's consequence class and performance objectives. [KB:newmark.md]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The Jibson (2011) assessment and the three-part calibration requirement are directly described in newmark.md SLOT 2. These characterizations reflect the documented synthesis in the KB source.]

## SLOT 3: Probabilistic Functional Structure of Newmark Displacement Estimation Methods

The Newmark sliding-block method conceptualizes the seismically loaded slope as either a rigid or a flexible (compliant) block resting on an inclined frictional surface. When the driving acceleration component along the failure plane exceeds the yield acceleration $k_y g$, the block undergoes incremental downslope movement; relative motion ceases when the driving acceleration falls below this threshold. The total permanent displacement $D$ accumulates over all exceedance episodes throughout the duration of shaking. In probabilistic applications, displacement is characterized using empirical regression relationships expressed in lognormal space, and the probabilistic functional structure of all models in the ensemble follows a common lognormal representation that permits propagation of both aleatory and epistemic uncertainty. [KB:newmark.md]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The sliding-block conceptualization, the incremental accumulation mechanism, and the lognormal probabilistic structure are explicitly described in newmark.md SLOT 3. These are standard features of the Newmark method accurately reproduced from the KB.]

All models in the ensemble follow the general functional form in which the logarithm of displacement is expressed as a deterministic mean plus a random residual: [KB:newmark.md]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The general lognormal form and notation are directly reproduced from newmark.md SLOT 3. The expression below is taken verbatim from the KB.]

$$\ln D = \mu_{\ln D}(\mathrm{IM},\,k_y,\,T_n,\,M_w) + \epsilon\,\sigma_{\ln D}$$

In this expression, $\mathrm{IM}$ denotes the set of ground-motion intensity measures specific to each model (e.g., $\mathrm{PGA}$, spectral acceleration $S_a$ at a reference period, Arias intensity $\mathrm{AI}$, or peak ground velocity $\mathrm{PGV}$); $T_n$ is the fundamental period of the sliding mass; $M_w$ is the earthquake moment magnitude where included as a model predictor; $\mu_{\ln D}$ is the model-predicted mean displacement in natural-log space; $\sigma_{\ln D}$ is the associated standard deviation in natural-log space; and $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$ is a standard-normal variate capturing record-to-record variability not explained by the regression. Where hazard is derived from a ground-motion model or site response analysis, spectral accelerations are evaluated at the periods required by each model, typically $S_a(\alpha T_n)$ with $\alpha$ in the range 1.3 to 1.5. [KB:newmark.md]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: All notation definitions and the range of alpha (1.3 to 1.5) are directly stated in newmark.md SLOT 3, reflecting the flexible-block models documented in SLOTs 3 and 4 (1.3 for Bray-Macedo 2019, 1.5 for Bray-Travasarou 2007 and Bray et al. 2017).]

The probabilistic functional structure of rigid-block models is determined primarily by the critical acceleration ratio $r = k_y / \mathrm{PGA}$. This ratio captures the fundamental controlling variable identified by Newmark: the margin between the peak ground acceleration and the yield resistance of the slope. Rigid-block models differ in their choice of secondary predictors; some retain $r$ as the sole predictor, while others augment the model with Arias intensity $\mathrm{AI}$ to capture the cumulative energy content and duration of shaking, or introduce $\mathrm{PGA}$ and $\mathrm{AI}$ simultaneously in a multi-parameter formulation. The common thread across all rigid-block models is the absence of $T_n$ as an explicit predictor, reflecting the assumption that the sliding mass responds as an undistorted body to the base excitation without significant internal dynamic amplification. Four rigid-block models are retained in the ensemble: Ambraseys and Menu (1988), which expresses displacement as a function of $r$ alone; Yegian, Marciano, and Varzaghian (1991), which employs the same single predictor in a polynomial form; Jibson (2007), which adds Arias intensity to capture duration effects; and Saygili and Rathje (2008), a multi-parameter formulation incorporating both $\mathrm{PGA}$ and $\mathrm{AI}$ simultaneously with amplitude-dependent dispersion. [KB:newmark.md]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The functional structure of rigid-block models (use of r = ky/PGA, optional AI or multi-parameter extensions, absence of Tn) and the identification of four rigid-block models are directly stated in newmark.md SLOT 3. The logic is consistent and internally coherent with the detailed equations in SLOT 4.]

The probabilistic functional structure of flexible-block models introduces a qualitatively different dependence on seismic demand. These models replace the simple acceleration ratio with a spectral acceleration $S_a$ evaluated at a period proportional to $T_n$, thereby incorporating the resonant response of the compliant sliding mass. The multiplicative factor relating the spectral evaluation period to $T_n$ equals 1.5 in the earlier subduction zone and general tectonic models, and 1.3 in the calibrated shallow crustal update, reflecting the degree of period elongation associated with large-amplitude response and post-yield stiffness degradation. All flexible-block models include $M_w$ as an explicit predictor to capture the duration dependence of accumulated displacement under long ground motions, such as those produced by subduction zone events. The functional form retains the lognormal structure with a quadratic polynomial in $\ln S_a$, with the quadratic coefficient reflecting curvature of the displacement-intensity relationship at high demand levels. Three flexible-block models are employed: Bray and Travasarou (2007), calibrated against 688 recordings spanning diverse tectonic environments; Bray, Macedo, and Travasarou (2017), calibrated for subduction zone interface earthquakes against 810 recordings with piecewise period-dependent coefficients; and Bray and Macedo (2019), calibrated for shallow crustal earthquakes against 6,711 NGA-West2 recordings with an additional near-fault velocity correction. [KB:newmark.md]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The flexible-block probabilistic structure (Sa at alpha*Tn, Mw inclusion, quadratic lognormal form) and the three models with their calibration databases are explicitly stated in newmark.md SLOT 3 and confirmed by SLOT 4. The alpha values (1.5 and 1.3) are directly from the KB.]

The ensemble of models is assembled into a logic tree in which each model is assigned a weight reflecting its applicability and calibration quality relative to the tectonic and geotechnical conditions at the site. The logic-tree structure retains model-to-model epistemic uncertainty explicitly, rather than collapsing it into a single representative prediction. In multi-tectonic settings - where both shallow crustal and subduction zone events contribute to site hazard - event-type-specific model subsets are weighted according to their respective contributions to the seismic hazard at each return period. The full distribution of $D_n(k_y)$ is obtained by Monte Carlo integration over the joint distribution of intensity measure values and model residuals, with each draw sampling a model from the logic tree and evaluating the corresponding log-displacement prediction. [KB:newmark.md]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The logic-tree ensemble structure, Monte Carlo integration, and multi-tectonic model weighting are explicitly described in newmark.md SLOTs 1 and 3. The description of epistemic uncertainty retention and event-type weighting is directly supported by the KB content.]

## SLOT 4: Detailed Newmark Displacement Estimation Methods

The seven displacement models retained in the ensemble span two model classes - rigid-block and flexible-block - and four calibration databases drawn from diverse tectonic settings. The following presents the complete predictive equations for each model, using consistent notation throughout: $D$ denotes permanent displacement in centimeters; $k_y = a_y/g$ is the dimensionless yield-acceleration ratio; $\mathrm{PGA}$ is peak ground acceleration in units of $g$; $\mathrm{PGV}$ is peak ground velocity in cm s$^{-1}$; $\mathrm{AI}$ is Arias intensity in m s$^{-1}$; $T_n$ is the fundamental period of the sliding mass in seconds; $S_a$ is spectral acceleration in $g$ evaluated at a model-specific period; $M_w$ is moment magnitude; and $r = k_y/\mathrm{PGA}$ is the critical acceleration ratio. All dispersions $\sigma$ are one-standard-deviation values in natural-log space unless otherwise noted. [KB:newmark.md]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The notation and variable definitions are taken directly from the preamble of newmark.md SLOT 4. This paragraph faithfully reproduces the KB notation block.]

**Bray, Macedo, and Travasarou (2017) - Flexible-Block, Subduction Zone Earthquakes**

The Bray, Macedo, and Travasarou (2017) model is a flexible-block formulation calibrated for subduction zone interface and intraslab earthquakes against 810 two-component ground-motion recordings [@BrayEtAl2017]. The model is applicable to earth dams, natural slopes, and engineered embankments with both planar and non-planar failure surfaces. Spectral acceleration is evaluated at the undegraded fundamental period $T_n$ of the sliding mass, with a piecewise coefficient set distinguishing short-period ($T_n < 0.1$ s) from longer-period sliding masses. The mean log-displacement and coefficient definitions are: [KB:newmark.md]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The Bray et al. (2017) model description, calibration database size (810 recordings), applicability domain, and piecewise period structure are directly taken from newmark.md SLOT 4, Section A.1.]

$$\begin{aligned}
\mu_{\ln D} &= a_0 + a_1\ln S_a + a_2(\ln S_a)^2 \\[6pt]
a_0 &= \begin{cases}
-5.864 + 0.550\,M_w - 9.421\,T_n - 3.353\ln k_y - 0.390(\ln k_y)^2, & T_n < 0.1 \\
-6.896 + 0.550\,M_w + 3.081\,T_n - 0.803\,T_n^2 - 3.353\ln k_y - 0.390(\ln k_y)^2, & T_n \geq 0.1
\end{cases} \\[6pt]
a_1 &= 3.060 + 0.538\ln k_y, \qquad a_2 = -0.225
\end{aligned}$$

The residual standard deviation is $\sigma_{\ln D} = 0.73$. [KB:newmark.md]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The sigma value of 0.73 is directly stated in newmark.md SLOT 4, Section A.1 and is consistent with the Bray et al. (2017) formulation as documented in the KB.]

The model is applicable to flexible slope systems with both planar and non-planar failure surfaces. Spectral acceleration is evaluated at $1.3\,T_n$, and an additional correction applies for near-fault, high-amplitude motions when $\mathrm{PGV} > 115$ cm s$^{-1}$. ## SLOT 5: Modern Methods Post-Bray (2019) and Fundamental Period Estimation

The methodological development of Newmark displacement estimation continued after the Bray and Macedo (2019) calibration for shallow crustal earthquakes. A primary advance was the recognition that the 2017 subduction zone model - calibrated specifically for interface events - did not adequately distinguish between interface and intraslab mechanisms, despite evidence that these two subduction source types produce systematically different displacement demands. Macedo, Bray, and Liu (2023) addressed this gap by formulating separate seismic slope displacement procedures for interface and intraslab subduction zone earthquakes using the comprehensive NGA-Sub ground motion database. Their work demonstrated that displacement scaling differs fundamentally between interface and intraslab events - a distinction that prior models had not incorporated explicitly - and provided updated regression models that account for this source-type separation as a distinct structural predictor. $$T_s^{(j)} \simeq \frac{4\,\pi\,H_{\max}}{a^{(j)}\,(2 - m_o)\,v_S^{o}}$$

## SLOT 6: Maximum Base Stiffness Estimation (Shear Modulus and Shear Wave Velocity)

The estimation of maximum base stiffness constitutes a foundational step in flexible-block Newmark analysis, because the shear-wave velocity at the base of the embankment - and thereby the fundamental period $T_n$ - depends on the small-strain shear modulus $G_o$. The shear modulus at arbitrary depth $z$ is estimated as a function of void ratio $e_o$ and octahedral effective stress $\sigma'_o(z)$ using the Ishihara (1997) formulation: [KB:periods.md]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The statement that G_o is a prerequisite for V_s^o and T_n estimation is directly supported by periods.md, which defines V_s^o = sqrt(G_o/rho). The Ishihara (1997) reference for the G(z) formula is explicitly cited in periods.md.]

$$G(z,\,e_o) \approx A\,\frac{(C_e - 1)^2}{1 + e_o}\,\left(\frac{\sigma'_o(z)}{p_{\mathrm{ref}}}\right)^n$$

In this expression, $A$ is a dimensionless material-dependent amplitude constant; $C_e$ is a void-ratio shape parameter controlling the sensitivity of $G$ to void ratio; $e_o$ is the in-situ void ratio of the material; $p_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is a reference pressure (typically atmospheric, 101.3 kPa); $n$ is the stress exponent governing the power-law dependence of stiffness on confining pressure; and $\sigma'_o(z)$ is the octahedral effective stress at depth $z$. The model parameters $A$, $C_e$, and $n$ are determined from laboratory tests on undisturbed or remolded samples of the fill or native material - typically resonant column or bender element tests - and Ishihara (1997) provides tabulated values for a range of soil types classified by USCS designation together with the corresponding void ratio ranges for which the correlations were calibrated. [KB:periods.md]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The G(z) formula and all parameter definitions are directly taken from periods.md, which explicitly cites Ishihara (1997) and references a table of model parameters organized by USCS classification and void ratio range. The laboratory test methods are consistent with the Ishihara framework, and the parameter descriptions are faithful to the KB source.]

The maximum (small-strain) shear modulus $G_o$ corresponds to the evaluation at the base of the embankment, depth $z = H_{\max}$, where the octahedral stress reaches its maximum value under the self-weight of the fill. Once $G_o$ is established, the base shear-wave velocity follows directly: [KB:periods.md]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The relationship between G_o and V_s^o, and the evaluation at z = H_max, are directly stated in periods.md. $$v_S^{o} = \sqrt{\frac{G_o}{\rho}}$$

where $\rho$ is the bulk mass density of the material at the base. The complete stiffness profile $G(z)$ over the full height of the embankment follows from the power-law expression with the estimated parameters, and the homogeneity ratio $m_o$ is determined by fitting the predicted profile $G(z)/G_o = (z/H_{\max})^{m_o}$ to the measured or inferred stiffness data. In the context of tailings storage facility (TSF) and waste rock dump (WRD) design, the relevant material types span the full range of the Ishihara parameter table - from coarse-grained rockfill and sandy tailings to fine-grained slime tailings - and the estimation is performed separately for each material zone contributing to the critical failure surface. The chain of calculations linking laboratory-calibrated Ishihara parameters to $G_o$, then to $v_S^{o}$, and finally to the fundamental period $T_s^{(1)}$ via the eigenvalue expression, provides the $T_n$ input required by all flexible-block displacement models. ## SLOT 7: Flexible Block Assembly Model and Applicability

The flexible-block assembly model is an ensemble of three independently calibrated displacement regression models - Bray and Travasarou (2007), Bray, Macedo, and Travasarou (2017), and Bray and Macedo (2019) - combined within a logic tree whose branch weights reflect the tectonic and geotechnical conditions at the site. Each model provides a complete lognormal predictive distribution for displacement conditional on the same flexible-block predictor set: yield acceleration $k_y$, fundamental period $T_n$, spectral acceleration $S_a$ evaluated at a period proportional to $T_n$, and moment magnitude $M_w$. The assembly produces a composite predictive distribution that retains both within-model aleatory uncertainty (through $\sigma_{\ln D}$) and between-model epistemic uncertainty (through logic-tree weight variation), yielding a displacement estimate that reflects the full uncertainty budget applicable to compliant slope systems. [KB:newmark.md]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The three flexible-block models and their common predictor set are directly described in newmark.md SLOTs 3 and 4. The logic-tree assembly concept with within-model and between-model uncertainty is described in newmark.md SLOT 1. The characterization of the composite distribution is a logical consequence of the ensemble structure documented in the KB.]

The flexible-block assembly model is appropriate for slopes and earth structures in which the sliding mass has significant deformability relative to the dominant periods of the input ground motion. This condition is typically satisfied by deep-seated failure surfaces in earth dams, compacted fill embankments, mine waste rock dumps, tailings storage facilities, and natural slopes composed of relatively soft or thick materials. The practical criterion distinguishing flexible from rigid mass behavior is a comparison of the fundamental period $T_n$ of the sliding mass to the characteristic periods of the seismic demand: when $T_n$ is not negligible relative to those periods, the internal dynamic response of the mass amplifies or de-amplifies the base excitation in a manner that rigid-block models cannot capture. Flexible-block models explicitly account for this amplification through the spectral acceleration evaluated at $\alpha T_n$, and they incorporate earthquake magnitude $M_w$ to quantify the additional displacement accumulated during the extended strong-shaking phase of large-magnitude events, a consideration of particular importance for subduction zone earthquakes. In multi-tectonic settings, the flexible-block assembly assigns event-type-specific model weights that reflect the relative contribution of shallow crustal versus subduction zone sources to the site hazard at each return period. ## SLOT 8: Rigid Block Assembly Methods and Applicability

The rigid-block assembly model is an ensemble of four independently calibrated regression relationships - Ambraseys and Menu (1988), Yegian, Marciano, and Varzaghian (1991), Jibson (2007), and Saygili and Rathje (2008) - assembled within a logic tree and evaluated against the rigid-block intensity measure predictors $\mathrm{PGA}$, Arias intensity $\mathrm{AI}$, and the critical acceleration ratio $r = k_y/\mathrm{PGA}$. The rigid-block assembly does not require $T_n$ or spectral acceleration $S_a$ as inputs, because the fundamental assumption of rigid-block behavior is that the sliding mass responds as an undistorted body to the base excitation without frequency-dependent internal amplification or de-amplification. The assembly retains within-model aleatory uncertainty through the model-specific $\sigma_{\ln D}$ and between-model epistemic uncertainty through logic-tree weight variation, yielding a composite lognormal predictive distribution analogous in structure to the flexible-block assembly. [KB:newmark.md]^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The four rigid-block models and their predictors (PGA, AI, r) are directly listed in newmark.md SLOT 3. The logic-tree structure and uncertainty decomposition are consistent with the ensemble framework described in newmark.md SLOT 1.]

The rigid-block idealization is appropriate for shallow, translational failure surfaces in relatively stiff geomaterials whose fundamental period is short relative to the dominant periods of the input ground motion. This condition is characteristic of thin surficial layers, stiff compacted fills, and shallow planar slides in rock or dense soils where the deformability of the sliding mass is insufficient to produce significant internal dynamic amplification. Shallow failure surfaces involve sliding masses with small height-to-length ratios, so that the rigid-body motion assumption is kinematically consistent with the expected displacement field. In these cases, the critical acceleration ratio $r = k_y/\mathrm{PGA}$ captures the dominant first-order control on displacement, and the addition of Arias intensity as a secondary predictor accounts for record-to-record variability in cumulative energy not captured by $\mathrm{PGA}$ alone.
