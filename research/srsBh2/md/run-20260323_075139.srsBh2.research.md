# Weight Calibration in Epistemic Logic Trees: Constrained Optimization with Sum-to-Unity Normalization

## SLOT 1: Extend weight formulation with sum-to-1 constraint

The linear-combination framework for spectral matching, developed in classical least-squares form across prior work, can be extended to enforce normalization of weights by incorporating the constraint $\sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k = 1$ into the constrained optimization formulation. [KB:spec.compile.research.md] This extension transforms the problem into a generalization of constrained linear regression in which the weight vector must lie on the standard probability simplex. [KB:spec.compile.research.md] ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The KB provides comprehensive treatment of constrained optimization for weight calibration in SLOT 7 (Convex Optimization Generalization) and establishes the non-negative weight constraint in SLOT 5. The simplex constraint is a standard addition to epistemic logic trees and is directly applicable here.]

The fundamental regression model retains its structure: given the log-spectral design matrix $\tilde{\mathbf{A}} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times K}$ with entries $\tilde{A}_{ik} = \ln S_{a,k}(T_i)$ (the natural logarithm of the pseudo-spectral acceleration of the $k$-th candidate record at period $T_i$) and the log-domain target vector $\tilde{\mathbf{y}} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ with components $\tilde{y}_i = \ln S_a^{\mathrm{obj}}(T_i)$, the weight vector $\mathbf{w} = (w_1, \dots, w_K)^\top$ is sought to minimize the mean squared error in log-spectral space. [KB:spec.compile.research.md] The addition of the normalization constraint produces the constrained least-squares problem: ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The log-spectral domain formulation is established in the KB (SLOT 4, Log-Log Space Reformulation), providing the theoretical foundation for the regression setup. The objective is a direct extension of the classical OLS problem (SLOT 3) with the normalization constraint.]

$$\min_{\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^K} \; \|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w} - \tilde{\mathbf{y}}\|_2^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad \sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k = 1, \quad w_k \geq 0, \quad k = 1, \dots, K.$$

The feasible set $\mathcal{C} = \{\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^K : \sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k = 1, \, w_k \geq 0\}$ is the standard simplex in $\mathbb{R}^K$, a compact convex polytope. The objective function is a strictly convex quadratic (the squared Euclidean norm), and the constraint set is closed and convex, guaranteeing existence and uniqueness of a global minimizer. [KB:spec.compile.research.md] ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The problem formulation follows directly from the constrained optimization framework established in the KB (SLOT 7, Convex Optimization Generalization), with the simplex constraint being a standard addition to weight calibration. The convexity argument is well-founded: the squared norm is strictly convex with Hessian $2\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top\tilde{\mathbf{A}}$ positive semidefinite (positive definite when $\tilde{\mathbf{A}}$ has full column rank), and the simplex is a closed convex set.]

To enforce the normalization constraint, the method of Lagrange multipliers is applied. Define the Lagrangian: ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The Lagrange multiplier method is a foundational technique in constrained optimization, applicable to the equality and inequality constraints present in the simplex-constrained problem. The approach is standard and well-established in mathematical optimization theory.]

$$\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w}, \lambda, \boldsymbol{\mu}) = \|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w} - \tilde{\mathbf{y}}\|_2^2 + \lambda \left(\sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k - 1\right) - \sum_{k=1}^{K} \mu_k w_k,$$

where $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$ is the Lagrange multiplier for the equality constraint $\sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k = 1$ and $\boldsymbol{\mu} = (\mu_1, \dots, \mu_K)^\top$ with $\mu_k \geq 0$ are the Lagrange multipliers for the inequality constraints $w_k \geq 0$. [KB:spec.compile.research.md] The Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions for optimality are: ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The Lagrangian formulation correctly separates the equality constraint (with unrestricted multiplier $\lambda$) and inequality constraints (with non-negative multipliers $\mu_k$). This setup is standard and directly applicable to the simplex constraint problem. The KB provides similar formulations in SLOT 5-7.]

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w_k} = 2[\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w} - \tilde{\mathbf{y}})]_k + \lambda - \mu_k = 0 \quad \text{for all} \quad k = 1, \dots, K,$$

$$\mu_k \geq 0, \quad w_k \geq 0, \quad \mu_k w_k = 0 \quad \text{(complementarity)} \quad \text{for all} \quad k = 1, \dots, K,$$

$$\sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k = 1.$$

At optimality, each weight satisfies: ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: This statement follows directly from setting the first-order condition with respect to $w_k$ to zero, derived from the Lagrangian. The relationship is a standard consequence of the KKT optimality conditions for constrained convex optimization.]

$$2[\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w}^* - \tilde{\mathbf{y}})]_k = \mu_k - \lambda.$$

If $w_k^* > 0$ (the $k$-th weight is active), complementarity forces $\mu_k = 0$, yielding: ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The complementarity condition $\mu_k w_k = 0$ is part of the KKT conditions and directly implies that if $w_k^* > 0$ then $\mu_k = 0$. This is a foundational principle in constrained optimization.]

$$[\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w}^* - \tilde{\mathbf{y}})]_k = \frac{\lambda}{2}.$$

For indices where $w_k^* = 0$ (inactive weights), the dual variable $\mu_k > 0$ ensures: ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: This statement is the dual to the active-weight case, following from complementarity and the stationarity condition. If $w_k = 0$, the complementarity condition is satisfied for any $\mu_k \geq 0$, and the stationarity condition determines the minimum value required.]

$$[\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^\top(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w}^* - \tilde{\mathbf{y}})]_k < \frac{\lambda}{2}.$$

This structure reveals that at the optimum, all active weights contribute an identical marginal reduction in the objective per unit adjustment in the log-spectral prediction error. [KB:spec.compile.research.md] ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The KKT conditions and complementarity characterization are standard results in constrained convex optimization. The KB establishes the active-set framework in SLOT 5 for NNLS (Lawson-Hanson method), and the same principles extend to the simplex constraint. The optimality condition that active weights equalize their marginal contribution is a consequence of convexity and is well-supported by standard optimization theory.]

The solution can be computed numerically using active-set methods, interior-point methods, or sequential quadratic programming. An active-set method maintains two index sets: the active set $\mathcal{A}$ (indices with $w_k > 0$) and the inactive set $\mathcal{I}$ (indices with $w_k = 0$). At each iteration, the algorithm solves the unconstrained sub-problem over the active set, subject only to the equality constraint $\sum_{k \in \mathcal{A}} w_k = 1$. Using Lagrange multipliers for the equality constraint alone, the sub-problem becomes: ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The active-set framework is well-established for constrained optimization and is explicitly discussed in the KB for NNLS (SLOT 5, Lawson-Hanson method). The approach of solving an equality-constrained sub-problem at each iteration is standard and directly applicable to the simplex-constrained formulation.]

$$\min_{\mathbf{w}_{\mathcal{A}}} \; \|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}_{\mathcal{A}} \mathbf{w}_{\mathcal{A}} - \tilde{\mathbf{y}}\|_2^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad \sum_{k \in \mathcal{A}} w_k = 1,$$

which admits a closed-form solution via the Schur complement or orthogonal projection onto the equality-constrained subspace. [KB:spec.compile.research.md] ^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The active-set approach is standard for constrained optimization and is applied in the NNLS context (SLOT 5, Lawson-Hanson method). Extending this to the equality-constrained sub-problem is conceptually straightforward, though the Schur complement solution requires careful numerical implementation. The KB does not explicitly derive the closed form for the simplex-constrained sub-problem, so this step is an extension grounded in optimization theory but not directly cited.]

## SLOT 2: Validate normalization approach vs. mathematical treatment

A natural question arises: can the normalization constraint be satisfied post-hoc by simply solving the unconstrained non-negative least-squares (NNLS) problem and then dividing each weight by the sum $\sum_{k=1}^K \hat{w}_k$ from the NNLS solution? [KB:spec.compile.research.md] This approach—normalization after fitting—differs fundamentally from the constrained formulation developed in SLOT 1, and the distinction is mathematically significant. ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The KB comprehensively covers the NNLS formulation and active-set framework (SLOT 5), which forms the basis for understanding post-hoc normalization. The assertion that this approach differs from constrained optimization is supported by the fundamental distinction between applying constraints before versus after fitting.]

The post-hoc normalization procedure operates in two steps. First, the unconstrained NNLS problem is solved: ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The NNLS formulation is directly described in the KB (SLOT 5, Non-Negative Weight Constraint). The two-step procedure—solve NNLS then normalize—is a straightforward approach that is conceptually simple and computationally tractable.]

$$\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\mathrm{NNLS}} = \arg\min_{\mathbf{w} \geq 0} \; \|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w} - \tilde{\mathbf{y}}\|_2^2.$$

Then, weights are rescaled by their sum: ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: Normalization of weights by dividing by their sum is a standard algebraic operation that ensures $\sum_k \tilde{w}_k = 1$. The operation is trivial mathematically, though its effect on the objective function requires careful analysis.]

$$\tilde{w}_k = \frac{\hat{w}_{k,\mathrm{NNLS}}}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} \hat{w}_{j,\mathrm{NNLS}}}.$$

The critical issue is that normalization changes the prediction, and the normalization step is generally not optimal for minimizing error in the constrained problem. To see this concretely, let $S = \sum_{j=1}^{K} \hat{w}_{j,\mathrm{NNLS}}$. The prediction from the normalized weights becomes: ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The claim that normalization changes the prediction is algebraically evident: scaling weights by $1/S$ scales the prediction by the same factor. The assertion that this step is suboptimal follows logically from the properties of constrained optimization.]

$$\hat{\tilde{\mathbf{y}}}_{\mathrm{norm}} = \tilde{\mathbf{A}} \tilde{\mathbf{w}} = \tilde{\mathbf{A}} \frac{\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\mathrm{NNLS}}}{S} = \frac{1}{S} \tilde{\mathbf{A}} \hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\mathrm{NNLS}}.$$

The residual norm after normalization is: ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The algebraic derivation showing how the rescaled weights affect the residual norm is mathematically straightforward, following from properties of vector norms and scalar multiplication.]

$$\|\hat{\tilde{\mathbf{y}}}_{\mathrm{norm}} - \tilde{\mathbf{y}}\|_2^2 = \left\|\frac{1}{S} \tilde{\mathbf{A}} \hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\mathrm{NNLS}} - \tilde{\mathbf{y}}\right\|_2^2 = \left\|\frac{1}{S}(\tilde{\mathbf{A}} \hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\mathrm{NNLS}} - S\tilde{\mathbf{y}})\right\|_2^2 = \frac{1}{S^2} \|\tilde{\mathbf{A}} \hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\mathrm{NNLS}} - S\tilde{\mathbf{y}}\|_2^2.$$

In contrast, the constrained solution $\mathbf{w}^*$ satisfies: ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: This intro statement follows logically from the post-hoc normalization analysis and leads to the display of the optimality inequality. The constraint solution must have residual at most equal to any feasible point including the normalized NNLS solution.]

$$\|\tilde{\mathbf{A}} \mathbf{w}^* - \tilde{\mathbf{y}}\|_2^2 \leq \|\hat{\tilde{\mathbf{y}}}_{\mathrm{norm}} - \tilde{\mathbf{y}}\|_2^2 = \frac{1}{S^2} \|\tilde{\mathbf{A}} \hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\mathrm{NNLS}} - S\tilde{\mathbf{y}}\|_2^2$$

by the optimality of the constrained solution. Unless $S = 1$ (i.e., the unconstrained NNLS solution already sums to unity), the post-hoc normalization procedure yields a suboptimal fit relative to the true constrained minimizer. [KB:spec.compile.research.md] ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: The algebraic argument is rigorous. The post-hoc normalization rescales the prediction by a factor $1/S$, which inflates the residual unless $S = 1$. The constrained solution explicitly minimizes the residual subject to the normalization constraint, so it must be at least as good as any feasible alternative, including the normalized NNLS solution. This is a direct consequence of the convexity of the objective and the feasibility of the normalized solution in the constrained set.]

However, the practical significance of this optimality gap depends on the magnitude of the residual and the degree to which $S$ deviates from unity. In many practical applications—particularly in spectral-matching contexts where the NNLS solution is already close to satisfying the constraint—the bias introduced by post-hoc normalization may be modest. [KB:spec.compile.research.md] If the NNLS weights naturally sum close to unity (e.g., $S \approx 1.1$), the rescaling factor $1/S$ is close to unity and the additional error introduced by normalization is small relative to the unconstrained residual. Conversely, if the NNLS solution exhibits weights that sum substantially above or below unity (e.g., $S \approx 2$ or $S \approx 0.5$), normalization introduces significant discrepancy from the true constrained optimum, and the difference becomes material. ^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The practical impact assessment is reasonable but not quantified in the KB. The claim that practical discrepancy depends on proximity to $S = 1$ is sound and follows from the algebraic argument above. However, without empirical examples or bounds on typical $S$ values in seismic spectral-matching applications, the assertion about practical significance is supported by reasoning rather than direct evidence. The KB does not provide numerical examples showing the magnitude of error for typical deviations of $S$ from unity.]

The key mathematical distinction is that the constrained optimization problem (SLOT 1) directly minimizes error subject to the normalization constraint as part of the optimization objective, whereas post-hoc normalization treats the constraint as secondary—it is applied after fitting without regard to its effect on the residual. For epistemic logic trees in probabilistic seismic hazard analysis, where weights represent relative plausibility assignments to competing ground-motion prediction equations, the constrained formulation is theoretically preferable because it respects the probabilistic constraint that weights must sum to unity while simultaneously achieving the best fit to the target spectrum under that constraint. [KB:spec.compile.research.md] In contrast, post-hoc normalization may inadvertently degrade the spectral match by rescaling an already-fitted combination without re-optimization. Therefore, when the normalization constraint is a fundamental requirement—as it is in epistemically rigorous logic tree construction—the true constrained solution should be used rather than a post-hoc normalization of the unconstrained fit. ^[Confidence: HIGH, Rationale: This conclusion is well-supported. The KB establishes that constrained optimization is the standard approach for weight calibration when constraints are binding (SLOT 5, 6, 7). The mathematical argument that the constrained problem is distinct from post-hoc normalization of the unconstrained solution is rigorous. The assertion that the constrained solution is preferable for epistemic logic trees is sound given the probabilistic interpretation of weights, which the task context explicitly states.]

From a computational perspective, both the constrained formulation and post-hoc normalization are tractable: the former may require an active-set or interior-point solver (available in R via `quadprog`, `CVXR`, or `osqp` packages [KB:spec.compile.research.md]), while the latter requires only standard NNLS and a scaling operation. However, the computational simplicity of post-hoc normalization should not be mistaken for mathematical equivalence. In problems where precision in the spectral match is essential—such as in seismic design of critical infrastructure—the marginal computational cost of constrained optimization is justified by the guarantee of optimality under the normalization constraint. ^[Confidence: MEDIUM, Rationale: The computational claim about available R packages is drawn from the KB (SLOT 7). The assertion that constrained optimization is justified in critical applications is a reasonable engineering judgment grounded in optimization principles, but the KB does not provide empirical evidence or cost-benefit analysis comparing computational effort against improvements in spectral match fidelity in realistic seismic applications.]
